# The Wake Behind the Jet: CMS Spots the Quark Soup Being Dragged Along

**Hook:** When a fast quark ploughs through the hottest matter ever made, it should drag some of that matter along and leave a thin hole behind it. CMS now reports finding that hole, about one percent of the background, hiding under the jet's own debris.

*Paper: A. Hayrapetyan et al. (CMS Collaboration), "Observation of the Jet Diffusion Wake Using Dijets in Heavy-Ion Collisions," Phys. Rev. Lett. 137, 071902 (2026), DOI 10.1103/g49y-8cjl. Editors' Suggestion and Featured in Physics. Companion preprint: arXiv:2602.19431.*

---

## 2. Big-Picture Context

When two lead nuclei hit each other at nearly the speed of light, they briefly make a droplet of quark-gluon plasma (QGP): quarks and gluons that are no longer locked inside protons and neutrons. It lasts a few trillionths of a trillionth of a second and then cools back into ordinary particles. Nobody can look inside it directly, so physicists send probes through it. The best probes are "jets": when a quark or gluon is knocked out of the collision with high energy, it turns into a narrow spray of ordinary particles heading in one direction. A jet born inside the droplet has to fight its way out, and it loses energy on the way. This "jet quenching" has been measured since the early 2000s at RHIC and the LHC, and it is the main evidence that the QGP is dense and opaque.

Quenching measures what the jet loses. A second question is where that energy and momentum go. Momentum is conserved, so whatever the jet loses must be handed to the medium. Theory says the medium responds in two ways. First, it can form a forward-moving ripple of compressed matter around the jet's direction, called a Mach cone (the same idea as a sonic boom). Second, the jet drags some fluid along in its own direction, which leaves a deficit of particles on the opposite side. That deficit is the "diffusion wake." The paper's own caveat explains why the Mach cone has been hard to see: its signal overlaps with the jet's own modified fragments.

The diffusion wake is attractive because it lies opposite the jet, away from the jet's debris. Until now the evidence was thin. CMS reported a first hint using Z bosons recoiling against jets (ref. 26 in the paper). ATLAS looked for the wake in photon-plus-jet events and saw nothing significant (ref. 25). This Letter is the first to use dijets, and it builds on a recent theory proposal (Yang and Wang, refs. 27-28) that dijets with a large angular gap between them can separate the wake from everything else.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is an experimental measurement paper from a very large collaboration, claiming a first observation of a long-predicted medium response; what's at stake is whether jets can be used as calibrated probes of how the QGP flows and responds, not just how opaque it is.

**Prior Belief Check:** This result agrees with the mainstream expectation: essentially every model of jet-medium interaction (hydrodynamic, linear-response, hybrid strong/weak coupling) predicts a diffusion wake, so experts would be more surprised by its absence. The surprise is not the existence of a wake but that it is finally visible, and that its size looks smaller than the most aggressive model predicts (see Section 4). So I would call this confirmatory and incremental in physics content, with the observation itself as the new part. Not every paper overturns the field.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is a single-collaboration result. The CMS Z-boson hint comes from the same experiment (a different channel with a different background situation), and the one independent look so far, ATLAS photon-jet, found nothing significant. Independent confirmation would be ATLAS running a dijet analysis with the same geometry and seeing the same pattern; that matters because the whole signal is about 1% of the background, where subtle detector or analysis choices can masquerade as physics.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Where things happen: detector geometry.** The beam runs along a line through the middle of the detector. Think of the detector as a barrel around the beam pipe. Position around the barrel is the azimuth angle $\varphi$, like longitude. Position along the barrel is described by "pseudorapidity" $\eta$, like latitude measured from the barrel's middle. At $\eta = 0$ a particle leaves at 90 degrees to the beam. Hand-check: the polar angle is $\theta = 2\arctan(e^{-\eta})$, so $\eta = 1$ gives about 40.4 degrees from the beam, $\eta = 2$ gives about 15.4 degrees, and the edge of CMS's tracker at $\eta = 2.4$ gives about 10.4 degrees. So bigger $\eta$ means closer to the beam line. Two jets can be exactly opposite each other in azimuth (back-to-back around the barrel) while sitting at different $\eta$ (different latitudes). That is the trick of this paper.

**Simple analogy (jet quenching):** A high-priority request crossing a chain of congested network stages gets delayed and taxed at each stage. **Breaks when:** you ask where the lost time goes. A packet's delay vanishes into queues, but the jet's lost energy and momentum must reappear exactly in the medium. That conservation law is the entire reason a wake should exist, and the packet picture has no slot for it.

**Concept / Vehicle for the measurement (the central analogy).**

> **Concept:** The diffusion wake is a tiny deficit (roughly one percent of the local particle density) in a specific region of the angular map, on the side opposite to where the jet pushes the medium. It cannot be seen directly because the jets' own fragments and their quenching-induced modifications sit on top of the same region. The measurement therefore (1) moves the second jet to a different $\eta$ so the wake lands in a clear patch, (2) subtracts a comparison sample where the jets are close together in $\eta$, and (3) subtracts the same procedure applied to proton-proton collisions, where there is no plasma.
>
> **Vehicle:** Profiling a service for a suspected small stall (about 1% of cycles) in one code path. In the normal run, a big hot function runs right on top of the same call path and drowns it. So you change the input so that the hot function moves elsewhere and the stall appears in a clean region of the trace. Then you subtract the trace from the unmoved configuration to cancel everything common to both, and subtract the same two-step procedure run on a reference machine that has no contended resource, to cancel artifacts of the procedure itself.
>
> **Analogy:** Hot function ↔ the jet's own fragments. Suspected stall ↔ the wake. Moving the hot function by changing the input ↔ choosing a large $\eta$ gap between the two jets. Unmoved baseline run ↔ the small-gap sample. Reference machine without the contended resource ↔ proton-proton collisions.
>
> **Breaks when:** you notice that changing the input also changes the stall itself. In heavy-ion collisions, a different $\eta$ gap means the jets cross different amounts of medium and quench differently, and the paper's own text says jet-peak modifications interfere with the wake more at small gaps. A profiler diff has no category for "the control run isn't actually a control." That is the structural weak point of this and every difference-of-differences measurement.

**Central analogy for this paper:** Moving the hot function to expose a stall.

**Supporting everyday analogy (not from computer systems):** A bus driving through a crowded platform pulls people along in its slipstream and leaves a gap right behind it. **Breaks when:** the slipstream is only momentum from air; the QGP's response involves a pressure wave (the Mach cone) as well as drag, and the two can be coupled in ways a bus slipstream cannot show.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**The data.** The CMS detector recorded lead-lead collisions ($^{208}$Pb nuclei) in 2018 at 5.02 TeV per nucleon pair, and proton-proton collisions at the same energy in 2017 as the no-plasma reference. The proton-proton sample is huge (299 inverse picobarns) compared with the lead-lead one (0.66 inverse nanobarns), but that is a unit convention; what matters is that the reference is far larger than the signal sample, so its statistical error is small. Lead-lead collisions are sorted by "centrality," the percentile of how head-on the collision was: 0-30% is most central (largest droplet), 30-50% is middle, 50-80% is peripheral (smallest droplet).

**Finding dijets.** Jets are grouped by the anti-$k_T$ algorithm with a size parameter of 0.4, a standard rule that merges nearby particles into a bundle roughly 0.4 units wide in the $\eta$-$\varphi$ plane. A "dijet" is the two highest-momentum jets: the leading jet must have transverse momentum above 130 GeV and $|\eta|<1.0$, the subleading jet above 50 GeV and $|\eta|<2.0$, and they must be back-to-back in azimuth (separated by more than 150 degrees). The dijets are then sorted by the $\eta$ gap between the two jets: below 0.5 (small gap), 0.5-1.0, 1.0-1.5, and 1.5-2.0 (large gaps). To keep the geometry consistent, the leading jet is always taken to be the one at larger $\eta$; if it isn't, the $\eta$ axis is flipped. That puts the subleading jet always on the negative $\Delta\eta$ side.

**Building the correlation map.** For each dijet, the paper counts charged particles (tracks with 1-4 GeV of transverse momentum within $|\eta|<2.4$) as a function of how far they sit from the leading jet in $\eta$ and $\varphi$. It then sums over the near-side half ($|\Delta\varphi|<\pi/2$, i.e. particles on the same side as the leading jet) to get a one-dimensional curve in $\Delta\eta$. Detector acceptance (the fact that particles at some angles are less likely to be recorded) is corrected by pairing each leading jet with particles from 30 other, randomly chosen events with similar vertex position and centrality: those pairs have no real correlation, so any structure in them is just acceptance. Tracking efficiency and misreconstruction are also corrected, following earlier CMS procedures.

**Why the geometry works (analyst-authored schematic, not to scale).**

```
Δφ (relative to leading jet)
 π | .................   subleading-jet peak   ......   (small gap: at Δη ≈ 0,
   |                                                      large gap: at Δη ≈ -1 to -2)
 0 | leading-jet peak at Δη = 0        subleading-jet WAKE (a dip) lands here:
   |                                   near side, negative Δη, for large gaps
   +------------------------------------------------------------- Δη
     -2        -1          0          +1         +2
```

The subleading jet sits at negative $\Delta\eta$ by construction. Its wake points opposite to its motion, which puts it on the near side (same azimuth as the leading jet) and at negative $\Delta\eta$. For a small gap, the subleading jet sits at nearly the same $\eta$ as the leading jet, so its wake falls right under the leading jet's own peak and is invisible. For a large gap, the wake moves out to negative $\Delta\eta$, clear of the leading jet's peak, and appears as a dip. Meanwhile the leading jet's own wake should be on the away side, and nothing wake-related is expected at positive $\Delta\eta$ on the near side. That empty region ($1.5<\Delta\eta<2.5$) is used to measure the uncorrelated "underlying event" (UE) background, so it can be subtracted from each sample before they are compared.

The observable is then a difference of differences.

$$ W(\Delta\eta) = \left[ R^{\mathrm{asym}} - R^{\mathrm{sym}} \right]_{\mathrm{PbPb}} - \left[ R^{\mathrm{asym}} - R^{\mathrm{sym}} \right]_{\mathrm{pp}} $$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $W(\Delta\eta)$ : the extracted diffusion-wake signal as a function of the particle's $\eta$ offset from the leading jet (particles per unit angle per dijet).
- $R^{\mathrm{asym}}$ : near-side charged-particle yield for dijets with a large $\eta$ gap (the "asymmetric" configuration, where wake and jet peak are separated).
- $R^{\mathrm{sym}}$ : the same yield for dijets with a small $\eta$ gap (the "symmetric" configuration, where the wake is hidden under the peak).
- PbPb, pp : lead-lead (plasma) and proton-proton (no plasma) samples.

**What this actually means:** It is a differential profile with a control build and a reference machine. The inner subtraction cancels everything the large-gap and small-gap samples share, such as the jet's own fragments and the UE shape. The outer subtraction cancels whatever a plasma-free collision does under the same selection. What is left should be a plasma-only effect that depends on the gap.

**Worked numbers: why a subtraction that leaves 1% can still be 5-sigma (illustrative, my assumptions, not the paper's error budget).**

Signal size, from the central-collision panel of the paper's Fig. 1 (my read-off): the PbPb dip reaches roughly $-0.25$ to $-0.3$ against a pp value near $-0.03$, so the plasma-driven dip is about $-0.25$. Background size, from Fig. 4 (vertical axis runs 35-50): about 35 particles per unit $\Delta\eta$ per radian per dijet. Ratio: $0.25 / 35 \approx 0.7\%$, so about one percent.

Now suppose the dip window contains 10 independent points ($\Delta\eta$ from $-2.4$ to $-0.6$ in steps of about 0.2, roughly what Fig. 1 shows), each with a statistical error of 0.05. Random errors average down as one over the square root of the number of points: $0.05/\sqrt{10} \approx 0.016$. A systematic error, like a mis-set UE baseline, shifts all points together, so averaging does nothing; take 0.03 (the paper quotes 0.01-0.04 for UE mismatch). Combined: $\sqrt{0.016^2 + 0.03^2} \approx 0.034$. A mean dip of about $-0.22$ then sits about 6.5 standard errors from zero. Equally important: even if the systematic baseline error ran as far as 0.03 in the worst direction, the dip would shrink only to about $-0.19$ from $-0.22$. That is the real crux: the quoted systematics cannot by themselves erase this dip; the open question is whether the dip is caused by the wake.

**Systematics, in plain terms.** The paper says the signal is insensitive to jet energy scale and resolution (varying them within uncertainty changes nothing) because the effects cancel between the small- and large-gap samples, so no corrections for them are applied. The main uncertainties are the track-finding efficiency (5% in lead-lead, 2.5% in proton-proton, translating to an absolute 0-0.025), the pair-acceptance correction (0-0.01), and above all the residual mismatch in UE level between the large- and small-gap samples (0.01-0.04), the dominant one. Effects of centrality calibration are negligible.

**Results.**

- **Raw picture (Fig. 1).** The proton-proton difference is flat and slightly below zero. Peripheral (50-80%) lead-lead agrees with proton-proton within errors. In the most central 0-30% bin, particles with 1-2 GeV show a clear dip across roughly $-2.4<\Delta\eta<-0.6$, strongest at the lowest particle momentum; at 2-4 GeV the dip is shallower. A mild positive bump at positive $\Delta\eta$ appears in the central 1-2 GeV panel (my read of the figure, at about $+0.1$).
- **After subtracting proton-proton (Fig. 2).** For central collisions and 1-2 GeV, the dip's mean position moves toward more negative $\Delta\eta$ as the gap grows from (0.5, 1.0) to (1.5, 2.0), which is exactly where the wake should move as the subleading jet moves out. The paper says small-gap dijets show a weaker wake because jet-peak modification and out-of-cone radiation interfere with it.
- **Integrated size (Fig. 3).** Integrated over $-2.4<\Delta\eta<-0.6$, the depletion grows with centrality, falls with particle momentum, and grows with the $\eta$ gap. My approximate read-offs (units not printed on the axis): central 1-2 GeV about $-1.3$ for the (1.0, 1.5) gap and about $-1.9$ for (1.5, 2.0); 2-4 GeV about $-0.75$ and $-1.0$; peripheral about $-0.45$ across the board. The central 1-2 GeV points deviate from zero by more than 5 standard deviations by a $\chi^2$ test, for both large-gap selections.
- **Models (Fig. 2).** PYTHIA8 (jets without a medium) shows no dip, as expected. HYBRID (a model combining perturbative jet evolution with a strongly coupled plasma response) overpredicts the depletion at 1-2 GeV, fits the trend at 2-4 GeV better, and predicts an enhancement near $\Delta\eta = 0$ from the wake of the subleading jet that is much larger than what the data show. CoLBT-hydro (a transport-plus-hydrodynamics model) agrees better with data on both the negative and positive sides, but its predictions are for 0-10% centrality, not 0-30%, so the comparison is not like-for-like. Both models capture the position of the dip.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the dip means the plasma lost particles in an absolute sense. The paper actually plots a difference of differences between two jet geometries and a proton-proton reference. The "depletion" is relative to that constructed baseline, and it is about 1% of the background level.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes ">5 sigma" means "5 sigma that the diffusion wake exists." The paper actually shows that the depletion is non-zero at that significance. The step from "non-zero dip with the right dependencies" to "the diffusion wake" rests on the argument that the wake is the only known mechanism that makes such a structure in the UE opposite to the jet, plus qualitative model agreement.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the small-gap sample is a wake-free control. The paper actually says the wake is present in both samples; at small gap it is hidden under the jet peak, and the paper itself notes that jet-peak modification interferes with it at small gaps. The subtraction therefore removes a mixture, and the paper treats the shared parts as cancelling without proving it.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "models agree" means a quantitative match. The paper actually says only that models with jet-medium interaction show qualitatively similar trends. HYBRID overpredicts the central 1-2 GeV depletion and predicts a much larger near-zero enhancement than observed; CoLBT-hydro is shown for a different centrality; and the models are plotted with statistical errors only.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**New to the field: the first dijet-based diffusion-wake measurement.** The idea of using a large $\eta$ gap to separate the wake from the jet's own fragments was proposed by Yang and Wang (ref. 28, Phys. Rev. Lett. 135, 072302 (2025), as far as the paper's reference list says), and the proposal is explicitly "background-free" by design, since the same wake enters both samples and the jet-correlated background cancels in the difference. CMS did the execution: building the near-side projection, estimating the UE separately for each sample from a region where theory says there is no wake, correcting detector acceptance with event-mixing, and showing in simulation that without medium effects the UE does not vary with subleading-jet location (paper p.3).

**New to the reader, but known in the field:** the 0-30% depletion's trend with centrality, particle momentum and gap are not unexpected given the theory; the novelty is that they are measured.

**Predictive Content Check**

1. **Falsifiable handle:** the theory proposal makes several statements that could have failed: the depletion should be absent in proton-proton (it is; PYTHIA8 is flat too), should vanish in peripheral lead-lead (it is consistent with proton-proton), should grow as the $\eta$ gap grows (it does), should sit at negative $\Delta\eta$ for the ordered jets (it does), and its mean position should move outward with the gap (it does at 1-2 GeV). The quantitative handle is the size: roughly $-0.25$ in the paper's yield units in the central bin, about 1% of background. Models attempted to hit that number and differ from it by a visible margin (HYBRID too large, CoLBT-hydro in a different centrality). So this measurement does test the models; it does not yet pin them down.
2. **Formalism load:** not triggered. This is an observational measurement whose subtraction machinery plainly generates the reported curves.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**L1. The dominant uncertainty is the UE baseline.** The residual mismatch between the large- and small-gap UE levels (0.01-0.04) is the largest systematic, at roughly 5-15% of the central dip. **(A) Consensus** — the paper states it is the dominant systematic and a mismatched baseline is a standard worry for sub-percent signals. **(paper p.3)**

**L2. Attribution rests on an argument by exclusion.** The paper says the diffusion wake is the only known mechanism that produces such a structure in the UE opposite the jet. Lead-lead-specific effects that proton-proton cannot subtract (for instance an event-by-event UE whose $\eta$ structure correlates with which dijet geometry gets selected, or collective flow modulations that differ between samples) are not fully excluded by the proton-proton reference; the paper's no-medium Monte Carlo shows the UE does not vary with subleading-jet location, but that simulation has simplified UE structure. **(C) Speculative** — this is my assessment, since I have not seen a dedicated flow-fluctuation control, and a specialist may know the checks that cover it. **(analyst inference)**

**L3. The significance procedure is thinly specified.** The Letter says "more than 5 standard deviations from a $\chi^2$ test" but does not say how correlated systematic errors and bin-to-bin correlations enter. **(C) Speculative** — the supplementary or HEPData record may spell it out; I have not read it. **(analyst inference)**

**L4. Model comparison is qualitative, and the models disagree with the data in size.** HYBRID overpredicts the central 1-2 GeV depletion and predicts a much larger enhancement near $\Delta\eta = 0$ than observed; CoLBT-hydro is shown for 0-10% centrality against 0-30% data; model bands include statistical errors only. **(A) Consensus** — the paper says so itself. **(paper p.4)**

**L5. The expected Mach-cone-like enhancement is weak or absent.** The much smaller enhancement near $\Delta\eta \approx 0$ than HYBRID predicts raises the question of whether the model's momentum transfer or its hadronization of the medium response is too strong. **(B) Contested** — the paper reports the mismatch but not a cause, and different models would give different answers. **(paper p.4, broader literature)**

**L6. Cancellation of jet-selection biases is assumed.** The paper omits corrections for jet position resolution and selection bias (jets with harder constituents, upward $p_T$ fluctuations) on the grounds that they cancel between small- and large-gap samples. If quenching differs between the geometries, the cancellation is imperfect, though the paper's jet-energy-scale and resolution variation tests suggest the effect is small. **(B) Contested** — the paper argues cancellation; the residual effect of the geometry-dependent quenching is not shown. **(paper p.3, analyst inference)**

**L7. Narrow kinematic coverage.** Only charged particles with 1-4 GeV, one leading-jet and subleading-jet $p_T$ selection, a near-side projection, and one jet size are shown; there is no scan in jet $p_T$ or quenching strength. **(C) Speculative** — more scans are easy to ask for, and the statistics may simply not allow them yet. **(analyst inference)**

**12-24 month outlook.** ATLAS (and CMS in more data) repeating the dijet geometry; larger lead-lead samples from LHC Run 3; centrality-matched, quantitative model fits, including three-dimensional wake structure (for instance the Yang et al. preprint arXiv:2509.24315); and a joint reading of dijet, Z-hadron and photon-jet results in one framework.

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

CMS studied lead-lead and proton-proton collisions at 5.02 TeV and selected dijets: two jets back-to-back in azimuth but at different $\eta$. Using the leading jet as a reference, they counted charged particles near it in $\eta$ and $\varphi$ and projected the near-side half onto $\Delta\eta$. For dijets with a large $\eta$ gap, the subleading jet's diffusion wake lands in the region of negative $\Delta\eta$, away from the leading jet's peak. For dijets with a small gap it is hidden. Subtracting small-gap from large-gap yields cancels most shared structure; subtracting the same procedure in proton-proton collisions removes the rest. Uncorrelated background is measured separately in a region where no wake can appear.

What emerges is a depletion of low-momentum charged particles (1-2 GeV) in the most central collisions, appearing at negative $\Delta\eta$, with a size and position that track the $\eta$ gap. It is smaller for peripheral collisions, smaller for 2-4 GeV, and absent in proton-proton and in a no-medium simulation. The central 1-2 GeV result is more than 5 standard deviations from zero. Models with medium response show a similar trend; one overpredicts the size and predicts a larger near-zero enhancement than seen.

**Why the summary is framed this way.** The key interpretive choice is to separate two claims that the headline merges: that a depletion exists with the right dependencies, which the data support strongly, and that the depletion is the diffusion wake, which is supported by the pattern of dependencies, the absence of alternative explanations in the models tested, and agreement of trends with theory. The first is a statistical statement; the second is an inference. The paper is careful in the body but the abstract speaks of the wake being "firmly established." A reader should take away that the result is solid as a measurement and persuasive as an interpretation, but that a quantitative theory match and an independent confirmation are still needed. The relation to the earlier STAR critical-point paper in this pipeline is instructive: there too, a statistical significance relative to an assumed baseline did not by itself settle what physical effect it measured.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the exact construction of the >5-sigma figure (how correlated systematic errors enter the $\chi^2$ test) and my read-offs of the numerical values from Figs. 1-3, which I took from plots rather than from the HEPData tables.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. A jet crossing the quark-gluon plasma should leave a tiny hole in the particle density on the side opposite to its push, and CMS now sees a dip of about one percent that behaves the way that hole should: stronger in head-on collisions, for softer particles, and for jets farther apart in angle along the beam.
2. The cleverness is geometric. Sending the two jets to different angles along the beam moves the wake out from under the jet's own debris, and a double subtraction against proton-proton collisions cancels most of what is left.
3. Five sigma proves the dip is real, not that the wake is its cause; that step rests on the lack of any other known mechanism and on models that match the trend but not yet the size, so independent confirmation and quantitative theory are the next steps.

## 9. Shorter Summary

When two lead nuclei collide at the LHC, they briefly make a droplet of quark-gluon plasma, the hot soup that filled the early universe. A fast quark or gluon ploughing through it becomes a "jet." Theory says the jet should drag some of the soup along with it, leaving a thin depletion of particles on the opposite side, called a diffusion wake. Spotting it is hard: the depletion is only about one percent of the background and lies under the jet's own debris.

CMS found a way around this by using events with two jets that are back-to-back around the beam but at different angles along it. When the jets sit at different angles, the second jet's wake lands in a clean patch of the detector. Comparing events with large and small angular separation cancels most of the background, and a further comparison against proton-proton collisions, where there is no plasma, removes what remains.

In the most head-on collisions, low-momentum particles show a clear shortfall, more than five standard deviations from zero. The shortfall is weaker in glancing collisions, weaker for higher-momentum particles, and weaker when the two jets are closer in angle, which is the pattern a wake should show. Proton-proton data and a simulation without plasma show nothing.

Two models with wake physics reproduce the trend, but one overpredicts the size and predicts a bump the data barely show; the other was run for a different range of collision centrality, so it is not a like-for-like test.

The main caution: five sigma says the shortfall is real, not that the wake is its cause. That attribution rests on no other known mechanism producing it, plus qualitative agreement with models. The result is from a single experiment, so the cleanest next steps are for ATLAS to repeat it and for theorists to fit the size quantitatively.
