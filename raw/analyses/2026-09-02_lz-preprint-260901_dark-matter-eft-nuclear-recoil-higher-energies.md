## 2. Big-Picture Context

Dark matter direct-detection experiments like LZ are built to catch a WIMP (Weakly Interacting Massive Particle) colliding with an atomic nucleus and giving it a tiny kick — a "nuclear recoil." For fifteen-plus years, the field's default assumption has been that this kick is gentle: standard spin-independent (SI) and spin-dependent (SD) interactions predict recoil spectra that fall off steeply with energy, so essentially all the signal is expected to show up below roughly 100 keV. Every major xenon experiment — LZ included — has historically tuned its analysis window (its Region of Interest, or ROI) to hug that low-energy region tightly, both because that's where the predicted signal lives and because backgrounds are messier and less well understood at higher energies.

But that low-energy assumption is a *choice*, not a law of nature. A more general theoretical framework — non-relativistic effective field theory (NREFT) for dark matter — allows the WIMP-nucleon coupling to depend on the momentum transferred in the collision, or on the relative velocity, or on both particles' spins in more complicated ways. Some of these more general operators predict recoil spectra that stay significant, or even peak, at energies of 100-300 keV — energies the standard playbook would consider uselessly rare and not worth searching. A separate and even stranger possibility is *inelastic* dark matter, where the WIMP doesn't just bounce off the nucleus elastically — it absorbs some of the collision energy to jump into a slightly heavier internal state (a mass splitting, denoted δ). This inelastic channel actively *suppresses* the ordinary low-energy recoils (a minimum collision energy is needed before the transition is even kinematically allowed) while pushing whatever signal does occur out to higher recoil energies, sometimes producing an unusual double-peaked spectrum.

This paper's contribution is straightforward to state and genuinely useful in practice: take LZ's existing, already-collected 220-day exposure (the same dataset behind the collaboration's earlier headline EFT results), and instead of stopping the search window at roughly 80 keV, push it out to 270 keV — and test it against a much larger catalog of NREFT and inelastic models (293 independently distinguishable model/mass/mass-splitting combinations after removing degenerate duplicates) rather than just the traditional SI/SD pair. The landscape before this paper: LZ (and XENONnT, PandaX-4T, and others) had already used comparable exposures to set world-leading limits on SI and SD couplings, and LZ itself had already published EFT constraints from this same run in the narrower low-energy window. This paper is best read as "mining the same rock for a different mineral" — reusing existing exposure to probe a physics hypothesis space the earlier low-energy-only analysis structurally couldn't see.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** this is an experimental re-analysis (not a new run, and not a discovery claim) that widens an existing dataset's analysis window and model catalog; the stakes are twofold — either it sets tighter, genuinely new world-leading exclusion limits on a broad swath of previously under-explored dark-matter interaction models, or (as turned out to be the case) it also surfaces one statistically middling but physically unexplained event that will need more data to resolve.

**Prior Belief Check:** nothing here contradicts mainstream dark-matter theory — NREFT and inelastic dark matter are both well-established, years-old theoretical frameworks, and extending a search's energy window to chase their higher-energy predictions is a natural, expected next step, not a surprise to experts. The one-event result, at a *global* significance of 2.6σ (after correcting for having tested 293 models), is well below the conventional 5σ discovery threshold and, crucially, is exactly the kind of result the field has learned, through hard experience, to treat with real caution — direct-detection experiments have a documented history of 2-4σ "events of interest" (and even a few higher-significance claims) that evaporated or were reinterpreted once more data arrived. Experts will read this paper as "an anomaly worth watching," not as evidence that shifts anyone's prior about dark matter's existence or properties. The paper's own language is notably restrained — it never claims "evidence for" anything, in contrast to how some other recent direct-detection and indirect-detection papers have framed comparable-or-lower-significance results.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** this is a single-experiment, single-event result with no independent confirmation, and by its nature (one anomalous event in one run of one detector) it cannot currently be "replicated" in the usual sense — there's nothing yet for another experiment to reproduce. What would count as confirmation: either LZ's own continued data-taking (the paper notes LZ has kept running under the same field conditions since 1 April 2024) turning up additional events consistent with the same interaction hypothesis at the rate implied by a 200-1000 GeV/c² WIMP, or a comparable exposure from XENONnT or PandaX-4T showing a statistically similar excess in the same high-energy NR region. Absent either, this stays exactly what LZ itself calls it: "one event of interest," not a signal.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**How a xenon TPC "sees" a collision.** LZ's detector is a dual-phase (liquid + gas) xenon time-projection chamber (TPC): 7 tonnes of liquid xenon sandwiched between electric-field electrodes, with photomultiplier tube (PMT) arrays at the top and bottom. When a particle interacts in the liquid, it produces two separate light signals: a prompt flash (S1) at the interaction site, and — because the electric field drifts freed ionization electrons up to the liquid surface, where they're pulled into the gas phase and accelerated into a much brighter secondary flash — a delayed second signal (S2). The size of S1 and S2, and the time delay between them, together pin down the interaction's 3D position and energy.

> **Analogy:** think of S1 and S2 as two independent, cross-checking measurement channels reading the same physical event — like a storage system that writes both a fast write-ahead log entry (S1: quick, low-detail, immediate) and a slower, more complete indexed record (S2: delayed, richer, position-taggable). Comparing the two tells you far more about what actually happened than either alone.
>
> **Breaks when:** you push the analogy to imply the two channels are redundant copies of the same information. They're not — S1 and S2 respond differently to *how* the recoiling particle deposited its energy (electron recoils and nuclear recoils recombine ionization differently), so the *ratio* between them, not just their agreement, is what actually carries the physics.

**Electron recoils vs. nuclear recoils.** A WIMP is expected to knock into a xenon *nucleus* (a "nuclear recoil," NR), producing a genuinely different S2/S1 ratio than the far more common background process of a gamma-ray or beta particle scattering off an atomic *electron* (an "electron recoil," ER). Plotted in the two-dimensional space of {S1c, log₁₀(S2c)} (the "c" marks position-corrected signals), ER and NR events separate into two distinct, overlapping bands — NR events sit systematically lower (less ionization survives per unit of prompt light) than ER events at the same S1.

> **Analogy:** this is exactly a two-class classification problem in a 2D feature space — like plotting spam vs. legitimate email by two measured features and finding the two classes cluster into separate, partially-overlapping regions. You classify an event as "probably NR" by where it lands relative to the calibrated NR and ER bands, the same way a simple linear classifier assigns a new point based on which cluster's region it falls in.
>
> **Breaks when:** you assume the classification is ever fully clean. The bands genuinely overlap, especially at high S1 (high energy) — which is exactly why the "event of interest" in this paper can't be assigned to ER or NR with certainty from its band position alone; it sits in an ambiguous zone the paper has to work hard to characterize with independent cross-checks (waveform shape, spatial position).

**Standard couplings vs. the bigger rulebook (NREFT).** The ordinary WIMP-nucleon interaction is often written as a simple contact interaction between the WIMP field χ and the nucleon field N:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} = \mathcal{O}\,\bar\chi\chi\,\bar N N$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}$ : the interaction Lagrangian — the mathematical object that encodes "how strongly, and in what way, two particle types are allowed to interact"
- $\mathcal{O}$ : the operator — the specific combination of momentum, spin, and velocity factors that describes *how* the interaction depends on the details of the collision
- $\chi, N$ : the WIMP and nucleon fields, respectively; $\bar\chi, \bar N$ are their Dirac conjugates (a technical bookkeeping device for relativistic fermion fields)

**What this actually means:** the *shape* of $\mathcal{O}$ is the entire ballgame. If $\mathcal{O}$ is just a constant number, you get the traditional spin-independent interaction — a fixed-strength "opcode" that doesn't care about how fast or how the particles are spinning. NREFT instead builds $\mathcal{O}$ out of four fundamental building blocks:

$$i\frac{\vec q}{m_N},\quad \vec v^{\perp} \equiv \vec v + \frac{\vec q}{2\mu},\quad \vec S_\chi,\quad \vec S_N$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\vec q$ : the momentum transferred from WIMP to nucleon in the collision (a vector — it has both size and direction)
- $m_N$ : the nucleon mass
- $\vec v$ : the relative velocity between WIMP and nucleon before the collision
- $\vec v^\perp$ : the component of that relative velocity perpendicular to the momentum transfer
- $\mu$ : the reduced mass of the WIMP-nucleon system (a standard two-body-problem quantity, $\mu = m_\chi m_N/(m_\chi+m_N)$)
- $\vec S_\chi, \vec S_N$ : the WIMP's and nucleon's spins

**What this actually means:** think of the standard SI/SD interaction as a minimal instruction set — two "opcodes" (spin-independent and spin-dependent) covering the common case. NREFT is the full instruction set: fifteen independent, dimensionless combinations built from those four building blocks (up to second order in $q$), each producing a numerically distinct recoil-energy spectrum. Some of these operators are explicitly momentum-dependent (they scale with $\vec q$ or $\vec q^2$), which means their predicted spectra don't fall off the way a standard SI spectrum does — they can stay flat, or even grow, out to much higher recoil energies. That's the physical reason a wider energy window is worth searching at all: the "opcodes" that only NREFT can express are exactly the ones whose signal would be invisible to an analysis stuck in the old low-energy window.

**Inelastic dark matter — an admission-control gate on the interaction.** Inelastic models add one more twist: the WIMP can transition to a heavier internal state (mass $m_\chi + \delta$) *during* the scatter, which means the collision has to supply at least $\delta$ worth of extra energy before the interaction is kinematically allowed to happen at all. To leading order, this sets a minimum relative velocity:

$$v_{\min} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{\mu}}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $v_{\min}$ : the minimum WIMP-nucleus relative velocity needed for the inelastic transition to be possible at all
- $\delta$ : the mass splitting — the extra rest-mass energy the WIMP must "pay" to make the transition
- $\mu$ : the WIMP-nucleus reduced mass (here for the *nucleus*, not a single nucleon — heavier than the nucleon reduced mass used above)

**What this actually means — worked numerical example:** for a 1000 GeV/c² WIMP scattering off a xenon nucleus (average mass ≈ 122 GeV/c²), the reduced mass is $\mu = (1000 \times 122)/(1000+122) \approx 108.7$ GeV. Plugging in $\delta = 200$ keV $= 2\times10^{-4}$ GeV:

$$v_{\min} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 2\times10^{-4}}{108.7}} \approx 1.92\times10^{-3}\,c \approx 575\ \text{km/s}$$

For $\delta = 300$ keV, the same calculation gives $v_{\min} \approx 704$ km/s. Compare that to the Standard Halo Model's characteristic WIMP speeds: a bulk velocity dispersion around 220-250 km/s, and a galactic escape-velocity cutoff usually quoted around 540-550 km/s (with the fast tail, boosted by Earth's own orbital motion, extending out to roughly 700-780 km/s in the lab frame). A 200 keV splitting demands speeds near the *edge* of the WIMP velocity distribution; a 300 keV splitting demands speeds at or beyond the galactic escape velocity, leaving only the thinnest sliver of the fastest-moving WIMPs able to interact at all. This is exactly why Figure 1 of the paper shows the $\delta = 300$ keV curve sitting orders of magnitude below the $\delta = 0$ keV (ordinary elastic) curve — it isn't a free theoretical choice, it's a hard kinematic gate.

> **Analogy:** picture an admission-control system that only lets a request through if it arrives with enough "energy budget" attached — like a rate limiter that silently drops any request below a minimum priority threshold instead of queuing it. Only the fastest-arriving WIMPs (the rare, high-priority requests) clear the bar; everyone else is turned away before they can even attempt the interaction.
>
> **Breaks when:** you push it to imply the gate is a hard binary cutoff with no further physics beyond it. In reality, once a WIMP clears $v_{\min}$, the resulting recoil-energy spectrum still has real structure (it isn't just "anything goes") — the full calculation (which the analyst's $v_{\min}$ formula above simplifies away) also folds in the nuclear recoil energy itself, producing the specific peaked, non-monotonic shapes seen in Figure 1's inelastic curves, not a flat plateau above threshold.

*This threshold derivation is the analyst's own simplified illustration of the standard inelastic-dark-matter kinematics (following the well-known Smith–Weiner-style leading-order relation), not a reproduction of the paper's own internal calculation — the paper computes its predicted spectra with the full WimPyDD software package rather than a simplified closed form. Flagged here and again in the Genuine Uncertainty Disclosure.*

**Profile likelihood, and why testing 293 models isn't like testing one.** The statistical machinery used to decide "is there a signal" is a Profile Likelihood Ratio (PLR) test — a standard particle-physics technique for comparing a background-only hypothesis against a background-plus-signal hypothesis while letting nuisance parameters (background normalizations) float to their best-fit values. Testing one single model gives one p-value. But this paper tests 293 independently distinguishable interaction models (after removing degenerate duplicates from an original catalog of 616) across a range of WIMP masses and, for inelastic models, mass splittings. Scanning that many hypotheses against the same one dataset means you're much more likely to find *some* model that happens to fit an accidental fluctuation reasonably well, purely by chance — the same statistical trap as running many A/B tests against the same dataset and reporting only the one that came back significant. The fix is the "look-elsewhere effect" (LEE) correction, computed here via toy Monte Carlo: simulate many fake background-only datasets, run the *entire* 293-model scan against each fake dataset, and see how often a *fake* dataset produces a result as extreme as the real one. That gives a "global" significance that's honestly corrected for the number of independent chances taken — and it is honestly lower than what a naive glance at the strongest-fitting individual model would suggest (a *local* significance of up to 3.4σ, versus a *global* one of 2.6σ once the LEE correction is applied).

> **Central analogy for this paper:** *Re-scanning old sensor logs with a much bigger rulebook.*

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**The setup.** LZ reuses the exact 220 live-day, 4.71-tonne fiducial-volume dataset (27 March 2023 – 1 April 2024) behind its earlier, narrower low-energy EFT paper. The detector, calibration, and background-rejection pipeline are unchanged from that earlier work with two exceptions: the analysis window (ROI) and the catalog of tested models. The previous ROI covered S1c from 3-80 phd (roughly up to 55-80 keV of nuclear recoil energy); this analysis extends S1c out to 600 phd, corresponding to a nuclear recoil energy reach of up to about 270 keV (with 50% detection efficiency at 269.9 keV, per the paper's own efficiency curve). The detector response — how much scintillation (S1) and ionization (S2) a given true recoil energy produces — is calibrated using tritium and ¹⁴C beta decays and ²¹²Pb-²²⁰Rn injections for the ER band, and deuterium-deuterium (D-D) fusion neutrons (covering 0-74 keV) plus americium-beryllium (AmBe) neutrons (covering 0-330 keV) for the NR band, all folded through the NEST v2.4.5 detector-response model.

**Backgrounds — old and two genuinely new ones.** Most of the background sources are unchanged from the earlier low-energy analysis: beta decays of $^{214}$Pb and $^{212}$Pb from radon-chain contamination, long-lived $^{85}$Kr/$^{136}$Xe/$^{124}$Xe, solar-neutrino electron scattering, gamma-rays from detector materials, residual tritium/$^{14}$C, and $^{127}$Xe/$^{125}$Xe activation products. The wider energy window introduces two backgrounds that weren't relevant before: residual $^{83m}$Kr (a short-lived, 1.83-hour-half-life isotope periodically injected for detector calibration, whose tail decay rate has to be separately constrained) and $^{125}$I (a daughter of $^{125}$Xe that decays via electron capture, releasing a combined 64.3-67.3 keV of energy — uncomfortably close to the energy of the anomalous event, and specifically flagged by the paper as a modeled-but-not-perfectly-verified spectral feature).

The most conceptually interesting background is MSSI — "multiple-scintillation, single-ionization" events. These happen when a standard ER interaction deposits energy at two separate locations, but one of those locations falls in a "charge-dead" region (either the 13.75 cm reverse-field region below the cathode, or a thin layer near the TPC wall) where the freed ionization electrons never make it to the gas phase and get lost. The two S1 flashes merge together in time, but only one location's worth of S2 survives — the event *looks* like a single NR-like interaction even though it's really a mismeasured double ER deposit.

> **Analogy:** MSSI is exactly a lost-write / torn-transaction bug — two separate writes happen, but one gets silently dropped before it's committed to the log, and what's left behind reads as a single, different-looking event rather than revealing that two things actually happened.
>
> **Breaks when:** you assume the "lost write" is random or unpredictable. It's actually geometrically constrained — it can only happen when one of the two deposits genuinely falls in a specific, mapped-out charge-dead region of the detector, which is exactly why the paper can simulate and constrain its rate rather than treating it as an unknown wildcard.

**The event.** After all cuts, one event stands out in the *science* sample (the cleanest sample — no coincident veto signal): a reconstructed nuclear recoil of $248 \pm 23\,(\text{stat}) \pm 23\,(\text{sys})$ keV, recorded at 21:22:39 UTC on 16 June 2023. In {S1c, log₁₀(S2c)} space it sits 1.5σ below the median of the calibrated NR band and 6.7σ below the median of the ER band at that S1c — closer to NR-like than ER-like, but genuinely in a region where hardly any calibration data exists to sharpen the call. Its reconstructed position (26.4 cm above the cathode, 26.9 cm radially inward from the reconstructed wall) is unremarkable — well inside the fiducial volume, not hugging a boundary in a way that would flag it as edge-effect-prone.

A dedicated profile-likelihood fit of the full 293-model catalog against the *science*, *prompt veto*, and *delayed veto* samples together finds this event drives a rejection of the background-only hypothesis with a p-value below $1.3\times10^{-3}$ for a substantial subset of the tested models — corresponding to local significances above 3σ, peaking at 3.4σ, typically for WIMP masses above 200 GeV/c². After the toy-Monte-Carlo look-elsewhere correction across all 293 models, the global significance is 2.6σ.

**Investigating whether the event is "real" or an artifact.** LZ runs an unusually thorough set of sanity checks specifically because a single anomalous event invites exactly this scrutiny:

- *Detector conditions:* nothing unusual in scintillation or ionization response near the event in time or space; the nearest prior muon events (127 minutes earlier in the TPC, 41 minutes earlier in the outer detector) were routine.
- *Calibration proximity:* a $^{57}$Co ER calibration source had been deployed and removed 25 minutes before the event — but on the *opposite side* of the detector, making direct calibration-source contamination implausible (though not, strictly, impossible).
- *MSSI reinterpretation:* if the event were actually a two-deposit MSSI event with the "hidden" second deposit in the wall, it would need to have deposited about 77 keV there; if in the reverse-field region, about 204 keV. Both are kinematically conceivable in isolation, but the paper argues the *geometry* required — a gamma-ray traveling more than 60 cm through liquid xenon at a shallow scattering angle before depositing a second time — is very unlikely to occur by chance.
- *Waveform/pulse-shape analysis:* the S1 pulse shape and top-bottom PMT light-sharing pattern are consistent with a genuine single-scatter interaction at the reconstructed position, and inconsistent with the specific template expected for a reverse-field-region MSSI event — though the analysis explicitly could *not* rule out a wall-MSSI interpretation on waveform grounds alone, and notes that S1c ~550 phd is a region with essentially no event-by-event ER/NR discrimination power from timing information.

No individual background process, nor any combination the paper considered, was identified as a compelling, specific explanation. The Letter's own framing is carefully neutral: "several rare background processes and detector effects were studied in detail but none could be identified as a likely explanation."

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** a reader might assume this represents *new* telescope/detector time — a fresh run specifically targeting higher energies. **The paper actually says** the exposure is the exact same 220-live-day, 4.71-tonne dataset behind LZ's earlier low-energy EFT result (the tonne-year math even checks out: 220 days × 4.71 tonnes ÷ 365.25 days/year ≈ 2.84 tonne-years, matching the paper's quoted exposure exactly). This is a second, wider-window pass over already-collected data, not additional running time.

> **Watch:** a reader might read "2.6σ global significance" and "3.4σ local significance" as escalating evidence that dark matter has been detected. **The paper actually frames** these numbers purely as a rejection strength against the background-only null hypothesis for a huge, honestly-corrected model scan — and pointedly never uses "evidence for" or "discovery" language anywhere in the text, unlike some other recent >3σ direct- or indirect-detection claims in the broader field.

> **Watch:** a reader might assume the blind bias-mitigation procedure (deliberately injecting fake "salted" signal-like events to prevent analyzer bias) covers the entire newly-widened energy range the same way it covered the original low-energy window. **The paper actually says** the salting model was built from a distribution that didn't adequately extend into the new high-energy region — three of the (total, across old and new) eleven salt events fell in the widened window with one just above it, but all fell outside the 90% NR-band containment region used for the actual analysis — so bias mitigation above roughly 55 keV explicitly "relies on the use of unchanged analysis selections" locked in before this new high-energy look, not on a dedicated blind test of the new region itself.

> **Watch:** a reader might expect a headline "3σ+ event" to be spectrally or spatially bizarre in some way that immediately marks it as exotic. **The paper actually shows** the event sits in exactly the corner of {S1c, log₁₀(S2c)} space where the *known* rare background processes (accidental coincidences, atmospheric-neutrino coherent scattering, and MSSI) are specifically expected to be capable of producing an isolated, high-energy, NR-like interaction — its rarity is broadly consistent with a statistical fluctuation of already-modeled low-rate processes, even though the paper could not pin the blame on any one of them specifically.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**1. Squeezing new physics reach out of data you already have.** Rather than requiring new beamtime or a longer exposure, this paper re-processes an existing, already-analyzed dataset through a five-fold wider energy window and a vastly larger model catalog. That's a genuinely efficient move: the marginal cost of this analysis is almost entirely computational and methodological (new background models, new NEST calibration, new statistical machinery), not new detector operating time — and it opens up sensitivity to an entire class of NREFT and inelastic models that the earlier low-energy-only analysis was structurally blind to, independent of whether any signal turns out to be real.

**2. A genuine detector-modeling fix, not just a wider window.** Extending the ROI required reconciling two calibration datasets (D-D neutrons, good to 74 keV, and AmBe neutrons, good to 330 keV) that a single power-law charge-yield model couldn't jointly satisfy — NEST v2.4.0 showed real tension between what the low-energy and high-energy calibration data separately preferred. NEST v2.4.5 fixes this with an explicit broken power law:

$$p(E) = 0.5 + a\ln\!\left[1 + b\,(E-E_0)\right]$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $p(E)$ : the power-law exponent governing how NR charge yield depends on recoil energy $E$
- $a, b$ : tuned suppression-strength and energy-dependence parameters
- $E_0$ : the threshold energy above which the correction switches on (fit here to 74.7 keV)

**What this actually means:** below $E_0$, the charge yield follows the simple, previously-calibrated behavior ($p=0.5$ flat); above it, a logarithmic correction smoothly bends the yield to match the higher-energy AmBe data instead of forcing one ill-fitting curve across the whole range. This is genuinely necessary infrastructure, not decoration — without it, the paper's own detector-response model wouldn't correctly predict *either* calibration dataset, and none of the headline recoil-energy or significance numbers would be trustworthy.

**3. Bridging back to the field's traditional units.** Because most of the field still thinks and plots in terms of the traditional spin-independent (SI) cross-section $\sigma_{SI}$, the paper provides an explicit recasting:

$$(c_1^s \times m_\nu^2)^2 = \sigma_{SI}^N\,\frac{\pi \cdot m_\nu^4}{\mu^2}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $c_1^s$ : the fitted isoscalar coupling strength for the NREFT operator $\mathcal{O}_1$ (the operator that reduces to ordinary SI scattering when $\delta=0$)
- $m_\nu$ : the Higgs vacuum expectation value (246.2 GeV/c²) — used here purely as a fixed reference mass to make $c_1^s$ dimensionless, not because the Higgs itself is doing physics in this equation
- $\sigma_{SI}^N$ : the equivalent traditional spin-independent WIMP-nucleon cross-section
- $\mu$ : the WIMP-nucleon reduced mass

**What this actually means:** this is a units-conversion bridge, not a new physical claim — it lets Figure S7 plot LZ's new $\mathcal{O}_1^s$ limits directly alongside CRESST, PICO, XENON1T, and PandaX-4T's traditional SI exclusion curves on the same axes, so the new, more general result can be compared against decades of prior SI-only literature.

**Predictive Content Check:**

1. **Falsifiable handle (always):** the paper sets concrete, numbers-attached 90% CL exclusion contours (coupling strength vs. WIMP mass, or vs. mass-splitting for inelastic models) across all 293 tested models — a genuinely checkable prediction against future data. More directly: if the event of interest reflects real new physics rather than a fluctuation, LZ's own continued exposure under the same field conditions since 1 April 2024 should reveal additional events consistent with the rate implied by a roughly 200-1000 GeV/c² WIMP interacting via one of the higher-local-significance operators; if it's a fluctuation, no such pattern should emerge as exposure grows. This is a plain, near-term, checkable prediction, not an unfalsifiable relabeling.

2. **Formalism load (fires — judged load-bearing):** the NREFT/inelastic formalism is doing real, non-decorative work here — each of the 293 tested spectra is numerically distinct, individually simulated via WimPyDD, and individually fit against the data to produce the specific exclusion contours in Figure 6 and Tables S7-S8. Removing the formalism wouldn't just simplify the presentation; it would remove the entire reason a 270 keV window (rather than 80 keV) was worth analyzing in the first place. This is the opposite of formalism dressing up a thin result — it's formalism *generating* the paper's entire search strategy.

**A second worked-numbers check, on the look-elsewhere correction itself:** converting significances to p-values, a local significance of 3.4σ corresponds to a one-sided p-value of about $3.37\times10^{-4}$, while the reported global (LEE-corrected) significance of 2.6σ corresponds to about $4.66\times10^{-3}$. If the 293 tested models behaved like fully *independent* trials, a naive Bonferroni-style correction would give $p_{\text{global}} \approx 293 \times 3.37\times10^{-4} \approx 0.099$ — which converts back to only about **1.3σ**, far weaker than the paper's actual reported 2.6σ. The fact that LZ's real (correlation-aware, toy-Monte-Carlo-based) LEE correction lands at a *stronger* result than the naive independent-trials estimate tells you something concrete: the 293 models are heavily correlated with each other (many differ only by smoothly varying a mass or coupling parameter, or by a scalar rescaling), so testing 293 of them isn't statistically equivalent to 293 independent coin flips.

> **Analogy:** this is the same lesson as running a hyperparameter grid search where neighboring configurations produce nearly identical results — a naive Bonferroni correction that treats every grid point as an independent test massively over-penalizes you, because the *effective* number of independent trials is much smaller than the raw count. A correlation-aware correction (like the toy-Monte-Carlo approach used here) gets this right where naive multiple-testing arithmetic gets it badly wrong.
>
> **Breaks when:** you conclude from this that the LEE correction is "generous" or under-strict. It isn't — it's simply *accurate* about the actual correlation structure, which a naive independent-trials assumption doesn't have access to. The 2.6σ number is the honest one; the 1.3σ naive estimate is the miscalibrated one.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**The single-event, single-experiment nature of the result caps how seriously it can be taken today.** A global significance of 2.6σ sits far below the conventional 5σ discovery threshold used across particle and astroparticle physics, and any single event's characterization (its exact recoil energy, its precise position in {S1c, log₁₀(S2c)} space) carries large individual-event measurement uncertainty that an aggregate p-value doesn't fully capture. **(A) Consensus** — this is both a field-wide convention (the 5σ bar) and the paper's own explicit framing (it calls for "increased scrutiny," not confirmation). *(paper §Discussion, broader field convention)*

**The blind bias-mitigation procedure has a real, acknowledged gap at high energy.** The salting model used to guard against analyzer bias was built from a distribution that didn't extend properly into the newly-widened energy region — none of the salt events that landed in this region fell within the 90% NR-band containment used for the real analysis. Bias mitigation above ~55 keV therefore rests on reusing the previously-finalized (and previously-locked) low-energy analysis selections, rather than a dedicated blind check of the specific region where the event of interest sits. **(B) Contested** — LZ's position is that locking those selections *before* looking at the high-energy region is itself sufficient protection against bias; a stricter reviewer could reasonably argue this specific energy range still lacks its own dedicated blind test. *(paper §Data Analysis, salting discussion)*

**The MSSI alternative-explanation argument rests on plausibility, not direct exclusion.** The paper shows the event's waveform is inconsistent with a reverse-field-region MSSI template, and argues a wall-MSSI interpretation is geometrically implausible (it would require a gamma-ray to travel over 60 cm through liquid xenon at an unusually shallow angle before a second interaction) — but explicitly frames this as "unlikely," not as a ruled-out possibility. **(B) Contested** — reasonable physicists could weight this residual possibility differently, since the argument is a physical-plausibility calculation rather than a direct experimental exclusion. *(paper, Supplementary Material MSSI discussion)*

**The look-elsewhere correction's exact strength depends on how the 293-model space was constructed.** Reducing an original 616-model catalog down to 293 independently distinguishable spectra required judgment calls about which models are "degenerate enough" to merge (e.g., pairs differing only by a scalar constant, or models whose recoil spectra become numerically indistinguishable above certain WIMP masses). A different, still-defensible way of grouping near-degenerate models could shift the reported 2.6σ figure somewhat. **(C) Speculative** — the paper's degeneracy-reduction logic is clearly documented and looks sound, but the sensitivity of the final global-significance number to plausible alternative groupings isn't quantified anywhere in the text provided. *(analyst inference)*

**The extended-range NR detector-response model is calibration-driven curve-fitting, not a first-principles high-energy prediction.** The broken power-law charge-yield correction (Eq. 5 above) exists specifically to reconcile DD (to 74 keV) and AmBe (to 330 keV) calibration data with a single unified model — a normal and well-precedented approach in the field, but one that means the detector's response above the AmBe calibration's own effective statistical reach is more extrapolation than direct calibration. **(A) Consensus** — this is the standard, openly-acknowledged state of empirical detector-response modeling across the direct-detection field generally, and the paper is explicit that this specific NEST update exists to satisfy joint DD+AmBe agreement. *(paper, Supplementary Material, Waveform/NEST discussion)*

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

LZ reused its existing 220-live-day, 4.71-tonne xenon exposure — the same run behind its earlier headline effective-field-theory result — and re-analyzed it with two deliberate changes: a nuclear-recoil energy window stretched from roughly 80 keV out to 270 keV, and a model catalog expanded from the traditional spin-independent/spin-dependent pair to 293 independently distinguishable non-relativistic effective field theory (NREFT) and inelastic dark-matter interaction models. The motivation is that several of these more general models predict recoil spectra that stay significant, or even peak, at energies the traditional low-energy-only search window was structurally incapable of seeing — inelastic models in particular, where the dark-matter particle must absorb extra energy to transition into a heavier internal state, actively suppress low-energy recoils while concentrating whatever signal exists at higher energies.

Extending the window required real supporting work: reconciling two different neutron-calibration datasets (D-D fusion neutrons, reliable to 74 keV, and americium-beryllium neutrons, reliable to 330 keV) into a single, internally consistent detector-response model via an updated NEST charge-yield parameterization, and building background models for two ER contaminants (residual $^{83m}$Kr and $^{125}$I) that only matter once the analysis window is widened. After all cuts, one event stood out: a $248\pm23\,(\text{stat})\pm23\,(\text{sys})$ keV nuclear recoil recorded in June 2023, sitting closer to the nuclear-recoil band than the electron-recoil band but in a region of parameter space with little direct calibration data to sharpen that classification. A combined statistical fit across the full 293-model catalog rejects the background-only hypothesis with a local significance as high as 3.4σ (typically for WIMP masses above 200 GeV/c²), which drops to a global significance of 2.6σ once properly corrected, via toy Monte Carlo, for having tested that many models against the same dataset. Extensive follow-up investigation — checking detector conditions, calibration-source proximity, alternative multi-deposit background interpretations, and pulse-shape/waveform consistency — found nothing that convincingly explains the event as an instrumental artifact or a specific known background, but also nothing that elevates it above "an unexplained, statistically middling anomaly." The paper sets world-leading exclusion limits across the full model catalog regardless of how the single event is ultimately interpreted, and LZ has continued taking data under unchanged detector conditions since the exposure ended, setting up a natural test of whether the anomaly persists, strengthens, or (as has happened to comparable claims elsewhere in the field before) fades with more data.

The interpretive choice worth flagging explicitly: this analysis is framed throughout as re-mining an existing dataset for previously-invisible physics, not as a new experimental run — and the paper's own restrained, non-triumphant language about the one anomalous event (never "evidence for," always "event of interest") reflects an appropriately cautious read of a 2.6σ global result, consistent with how the field has learned, sometimes the hard way, to treat single-experiment anomalies at this significance level.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the inelastic-dark-matter kinematic threshold ($v_{\min} \approx \sqrt{2\delta/\mu}$) presented in Section 3 and used in the worked numerical example is the analyst's own simplified, leading-order illustration of standard inelastic-DM kinematics — it is not a reproduction of the paper's own internal calculation, which uses the full WimPyDD simulation machinery (including the full nuclear recoil energy dependence, the detailed galactic velocity distribution, and Earth's motion through it) rather than a single closed-form threshold velocity. The qualitative conclusion (higher mass splittings push required WIMP speeds toward or beyond the galactic escape velocity, suppressing the interaction rate) is solid and consistent with the paper's own Figure 1, but the specific numbers (575 km/s, 704 km/s) should be read as an order-of-magnitude illustration, not as numbers the paper itself states.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **LZ found a second kind of signal by looking at old data differently, not by collecting new data at all** — the same 220-day, 2.84-tonne-year exposure behind an earlier result was re-analyzed with a five-times-wider energy window and a model catalog 150× larger, turning up sensitivity to physics the original low-energy-only search structurally couldn't see.

2. **One event, at 248 keV, currently has no convincing explanation** — not as a known background, not as an instrumental artifact, and not (yet) as confirmed dark matter. Its statistical weight, a 2.6σ global significance after correcting for testing 293 models, is real but well short of anything the field would call discovery-level.

3. **The math wasn't decoration here — it generated the entire search.** The elaborate effective-field-theory operator catalog is what let this analysis "see" a class of predicted signals (momentum-dependent and inelastic interactions with high-energy tails) that the traditional two-model (SI/SD) approach is structurally blind to, regardless of whether the one anomalous event turns out to mean anything at all.

## 9. Shorter Summary

*(350-word ceiling)*

The LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ) dark-matter experiment normally searches for weak nuclear "kicks" from WIMP particles only up to about 80 kiloelectron-volts (keV) of recoil energy, because the standard theoretical picture predicts almost no signal above that. But a more general theoretical framework allows the dark-matter-nucleus interaction to depend on momentum or velocity in ways that can push predicted signals out to much higher energies — and a separate possibility, "inelastic" dark matter, actively suppresses low-energy recoils in favor of higher-energy ones. This paper reuses LZ's existing 220-day dataset (no new running time) and widens the search window out to 270 keV, testing it against 293 different interaction models instead of the usual two.

After all data-quality cuts, one event stood out: a 248 keV nuclear recoil recorded in June 2023 that sits in an ambiguous zone between the expected electron-recoil and nuclear-recoil signal regions. A careful statistical scan across all 293 models found this event pulls the data away from a pure-background explanation with as much as 3.4-sigma local significance — which, once properly corrected for having tested so many models at once, becomes a 2.6-sigma global significance. That's well short of the 5-sigma bar the field requires for a discovery, but notably higher than a coincidence would typically produce.

LZ investigated hard for mundane explanations: detector glitches, a nearby calibration source, a mis-measured double-deposit event, and pulse-shape inconsistencies. None of these convincingly explains the event, but none can be fully ruled out either. The honest, careful conclusion the paper reaches is that this is an unexplained anomaly worth watching, not a discovery — and LZ has kept its detector running under the same conditions since the analysis ended specifically to see whether more data strengthens, weakens, or explains the signal away.

Separately from the single anomalous event, the analysis sets new world-leading limits on a much broader menu of dark-matter interaction possibilities than earlier searches could test, simply by looking at the same data through a wider, more general lens — a reminder that meaningful new physics reach doesn't always require a bigger detector or more running time, sometimes it just requires asking a more general question of data you already have.
