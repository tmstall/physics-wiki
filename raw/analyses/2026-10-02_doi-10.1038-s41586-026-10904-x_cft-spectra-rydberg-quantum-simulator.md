# 1. Hearing a Quantum Critical Point Ring: Reading the Fingerprint of a Conformal Field Theory off a Chain of 35 Atoms

**Hook:** At a quantum phase transition, the energy levels of a finite chain are predicted to sit in fixed ratios (like 2:4:6:8) that depend only on the *type* of transition, not on the details of the machine, and this paper is the first to actually measure those ladders directly, by shaking a chain of Rydberg atoms and watching which shake frequencies kick it into an excited state.

*Paper:* Sun, Le, Naus, Tsai, Picard, Murciano, Knap, Alicea & Endres (Caltech / Paris-Saclay / TU Munich), "Observation of conformal field theory spectra in a quantum simulator," *Nature* 657, 98–106 (3 Sept 2026), DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10904-x, open access. Companion arXiv:2601.16275.

---

# 2. Big-Picture Context

**The problem.** When you tune a quantum material to a zero-temperature phase transition (a *quantum critical point*), something remarkable happens: the system looks statistically the same when you zoom in or out. Correlations decay as power laws instead of exponentials, and the whole thing is governed by a conformal field theory (CFT), a field theory whose symmetries are so restrictive that they fix many numbers exactly. Which CFT you get (the "universality class") depends only on broad features like the symmetry of the problem. The Ising CFT, for instance, describes the transition between a disordered phase and a checkerboard-ordered phase.

**What was missing.** People have measured correlations, entanglement, and slow-ramp dynamics near such points in quantum simulators. What had not been measured is the *excitation spectrum*, the actual list of energies at which the finite system can be excited. That is a pity, because in 1984 Cardy showed that in a finite system the spectrum is a fingerprint: the *ratios* of the lowest few energy gaps are universal numbers set by the CFT, while an overall scale (a "velocity" v, set by lattice details) is not. Boundary conditions at the chain's ends also change the ratios, so the spectrum doubles as a readout of what the edges are doing.

**Paper type and stakes.** This is an experimental quantum-simulation paper. It introduces a spectroscopy technique for many-body states in a neutral-atom array and uses it to measure finite-size spectra for two universality classes (Ising and tricritical Ising, "TCI"), plus a first look at the dynamical structure factor. The stake is whether programmable atom arrays can serve as a *diagnostic* for universality classes, including ones nobody has solved in advance.

**Prior Belief Check.** For the Ising case this is confirmatory: the Ising CFT is equivalent to free (non-interacting) fermions, so the 2:4:6:8 ladder is textbook, and experts expected it. The result complicates nothing in the mainstream picture. The genuinely less-predictable parts are the tricritical Ising point (an *interacting* CFT, central charge 7/10) and the ability to switch between boundary-condition fixed points. Even there the theory predictions come from the same group's earlier work (Slagle et al. 2021, ref. 40), so this is an experimental realization of a theory-anticipated target, not a surprise. The TCI evidence is also noticeably weaker than the Ising evidence (see Section 6).

**Replication & Convergence Note.** Single group (Endres lab), single platform (alkaline-earth $^{88}$Sr tweezer arrays), no independent confirmation yet. The theory side (Cardy's results, exact diagonalization) is independently well established, so what needs confirming is the *experimental realization*. Independent confirmation would be another lab, ideally on another platform (another Rydberg array, trapped ions, superconducting qubits), reproducing the same ratios, especially the TCI ones, where the current error bars are large.

# 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

### 3.1 The Rydberg chain as a constrained bit string

Each atom is a bit: ground state |0> or Rydberg (highly excited) state |1>. Two neighboring atoms cannot both be |1> at once, because a Rydberg atom shifts its neighbor's energy so strongly (the "blockade") that a second excitation is off-resonance. **Analogy:** a row of mutual-exclusion locks. No two adjacent threads can hold the lock at the same time.

A knob called the detuning (Δ) biases each atom toward |1>. At very negative Δ everything sits at |0> (the "disordered" phase). At large positive Δ the system prefers 1010101... (a "Z2-ordered," checkerboard, or charge-density-wave phase). Between them lies a continuous phase transition, and that transition is the Ising critical point. If you also add a weaker interaction between next-nearest neighbors (V2) and make it *attractive*, the continuous transition line ends at a special *tricritical* point, beyond which the transition becomes abrupt (first-order). The TCI CFT lives right there.

### 3.2 Why a finite chain's spectrum is a fingerprint

> **Concept:** A finite chain of length L tuned to a quantum critical point has excitation energies E = (π ħ v / L) × (a fixed list of numbers). The list is determined entirely by the CFT (the "operator content") and by the boundary conditions at the two ends. The velocity v is non-universal: it depends on lattice details and can't be predicted from the CFT. So you can never read off absolute energies universally, but *ratios* between levels are exact universal numbers that identify the universality class and the boundary condition.
>
> **Vehicle:** A family of CPUs, all built to one microarchitecture but sold at different clock speeds. The clock varies from board to board and isn't fixed by the design. But instruction latencies counted in *cycles* (say 2, 4, 6, 8 cycles for four instruction classes) are fixed by the design itself. Time each instruction on an unknown-clock machine, divide by the shortest, and you recover the cycle ratios, which tell you which microarchitecture you have.
>
> **Analogy:** The clock ↔ the non-universal velocity v (and the 1/L scaling). Cycle counts ↔ the universal numbers in the list. Measuring latency ratios to identify the design ↔ measuring energy ratios to identify the universality class. Measuring on a chip of different size ↔ changing L, which rescales every energy by 1/L, like re-timing at a different clock.
>
> **Breaks when:** you ask who chose the numbers. Cycle counts are engineering choices someone could have made differently. CFT ratios are *forced* by symmetry, with no design freedom, and the vehicle has no way to say "the ratios are not a choice." Nothing about the vehicle explains why only certain families of ratios can ever occur.

### 3.3 Ising = free fermions: counting pairs gives the ladder

Tier-3 material, so here is the whole mechanism with real numbers. At the Ising critical point the chain's low-energy excitations behave like free particles (fermions) with allowed momenta stepping in units of π/L. Label the allowed levels by an integer n = 0, 1, 2, ... with energy proportional to (n + 1/2). In an odd-length chain the excited states are made by occupying an *even number* of these levels (the paper's Table 1 shows why). The simplest are two-particle states {a, b}, with energy proportional to a + b + 1, in units of π ħ v / L. Counting pairs by hand:

| Energy (units of π ħ v / L) | Two-particle states | Count | Reflection parity (paper's Table 1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | none (ground state) | 1 | even |
| 2 | {0,1} | 1 | even |
| 3 | {0,2} | 1 | odd |
| 4 | {0,3}, {1,2} | 2 | even |
| 5 | {0,4}, {1,3} | 2 | odd |
| 6 | {0,5}, {1,4}, {2,3} | 3 | even |
| 7 | {0,6}, {1,5}, {2,4} | 3 | odd |
| 8 | {0,7}, {1,6}, {2,5}, {3,4} (plus a four-particle state) | 4+ | even |

I checked this enumeration by script, and the parity pattern follows the paper's Table 1 (sum a + b odd gives even parity). The takeaway: the *even-parity* levels sit at 2:4:6:8, and the *odd-parity* levels at 3:5:7. A ladder of equal spacing with alternating parity is the Ising finite-size fingerprint. Why do the levels get more crowded as energy rises? Because there are more ways to split the energy among two particles, which is the same reason it matters later that large chains are hard to resolve.

### 3.4 Boundary conditions: pinned versus floating edges

The edges of an open chain matter. In the Ising chain, an atom at the very end has fewer neighbors, which nudges it toward |1> and catalyzes checkerboard order that bleeds into the bulk: a "fixed" boundary condition. In the TCI case two stable edge behaviors exist: "fixed" (edge order present) and "free" (edge order suppressed), separated by an *unstable* in-between "intermediate" condition. Each has its own universal ladder.

**Analogy:** a bus line pinned by a pull-up/pull-down resistor (fixed) versus a floating input (free).

**Breaks when:** you take "floating" literally. A floating input has no defined value, whereas the CFT's free boundary is a definite, stable state with its own exact, predictable spectrum.

### 3.5 Spectroscopy by shaking

The paper's measurement idea is a frequency-sweep stress test: wiggle the system's control knob at frequency f, and if hf matches the gap between the ground state and an excited state, population moves into that state. **Analogy:** sweeping the voltage on a power-delivery network to find its resonant droop frequencies. **Breaks when:** the power network is classical and every resonance shows up. Here, symmetry selection rules (parity under left-right reflection) hide whole families of levels from a given kind of shake.

**Central analogy for this paper:** Latency ratios identify the design; clock stays unknown.

# 4. Core Technical Explanation

**Step 1: Prepare.** The authors adiabatically (slowly) sweep the detuning so the chain starts disordered and ends near its critical point, close to the many-body ground state. Chains run up to L = 35 atoms.

**Step 2: Shake.** They modulate the detuning with a small oscillation at frequency f inside a Gaussian-shaped pulse. The pulse has a width in time, which means each excited state shows up as a *Gaussian bump* in frequency, not a razor line.

**Step 3: Read out cleverly (the "modulation-ramp-probe" trick).** The trouble is that the critical Hamiltonian's eigenstates are hard to measure directly. So after shaking, they ramp the detuning back into an easy phase (disordered or checkerboard) where atom counts line up with energy eigenstates. Then simply counting how many atoms are in |0> tells you how much population was transferred. The change in that count is called δn. Think of it as converting a hard-to-observe quantum state into a simple counter that increments when an excitation happened.

The resulting signal, for a weak drive, is a sum of Gaussians:

$$\delta n \;\propto\; A^2 w^2 \sum_{e \neq g} e^{-2\pi^2 w^2 \left(\frac{E_e - E_g}{h} - f\right)^2}\, |\langle g|\hat K|e\rangle|^2$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\delta n$ : change in number of atoms in |0> after the shake and ramp (dimensionless count)
- $A$ : modulation amplitude (frequency units)
- $w$ : time width of the Gaussian pulse envelope (time)
- $E_e, E_g$ : energies of an excited state e and the ground state g (energy)
- $h$ : Planck's constant
- $f$ : modulation frequency (Hz)
- $\hat K$ : the operator describing the shake (the sum of occupation numbers, a "how many atoms are excited" counter)
- $|\langle g|\hat K|e\rangle|^2$ : the transition strength, how strongly the shake couples ground to excited state e

**What this actually means:** each excited state contributes a Gaussian bump centered where hf equals its gap, with height set by the transition strength. It is exactly how a sweep of a resonance test shows up as peaks at the resonant frequencies. The widths come from the finite pulse duration; the heights tell you which states are "visible" to this particular shake.

**Worked number.** On a 7-atom chain the authors measured the first gap two independent ways: modulation spectroscopy gives 2.83(4) MHz, and a separate Ramsey interferometry run gives 2.81(3) MHz. Agreement to under 1%. At 2.83 MHz one oscillation takes 1/2.83 ≈ 0.35 µs, while the coherence time is only 0.75(4) µs, so you get about two oscillations before the signal dies. That coherence budget is the reason the technique resolves only a handful of levels in a big chain.

**Step 4: Even-parity sector (Fig. 1d–f, Fig. 2).** A uniform (global) shake respects left-right reflection symmetry, so it can only reach even-parity states. In the 19-atom chain at the Ising point (V2/Ω = 0.51) they see four peaks lining up with exact-diagonalization energies, and with the predicted ratio 2:4:6:8. Then they test *scaling*: if you multiply the frequency by chain length L, peaks from different L should land on the same universal values. For L = 19, 23, 27, 35 they do (fit reduced χ² = 0.98 against the CFT prediction); for L = 7 they do not, because at that size the gaps are comparable to the Rabi frequency and the low-energy description fails.

**Step 5: Odd-parity sector (Fig. 3a–b).** To reach odd states, they add a *spatially varying* detuning pattern (a half-cosine across the chain) that flips sign under reflection. Peaks appear *between* the even ones at the ratio 3:5:7, matching Table 1. Even-parity 2:4:6:8 plus odd-parity 3:5:7 gives the full 2:3:4:5:6:7:8 ladder.

**Step 6: Linear dispersion (Fig. 3c).** A modulation with wavevector k picks states of definite momentum. The measured threshold frequency rises linearly with k, confirming the particles are massless with a speed v, the "light cone" velocity.

**Step 7: The tricritical point (Figs. 4–5).** They switch to attractive V2 (−0.51 and −1.63 times Ω) and find the point where the spectrum matches TCI predictions by numerical search (not by theory alone). At V2/Ω = −1.63 the *edge* checkerboard order (measured by σ_edge) collapses almost completely, signaling the "free" boundary. The measured ratio of the second to the first even-parity gap, E2/E1, comes out 2.08(4) at the Ising point (CFT: 2), 1.98(6) with fixed edges at TCI (CFT: 2), and 1.45(25) at TCI with free edges (CFT: 4/3). So the ratio changes character with the edge condition, as predicted.

**Step 8: Boundary tuning (Fig. 5, Extended Data Fig. 5–6).** To get fixed edges at TCI they add ancillary atoms at each end, pinned in |1>, and apply a local detuning profile; a dial η runs from 0 (free) to 1 (fixed). Sweeping η, the first excited gap first drops and then rises. At an intermediate η ≈ 0.39 theory predicts the *unstable* fixed point with E2/E1 = 10/3. They measure 2.5(4).

**Step 9: Large systems (Fig. 6).** In big chains the levels crowd together (spacing falls as 1/L) and individual peaks can no longer be resolved within the coherence time. Instead they use a shorter "modulation-probe" sequence that measures the dynamical structure factor: the response at frequency ω for the field that sits at the heart of the CFT. For the Ising energy-density field (scaling dimension 1) theory predicts a *constant* response at low frequency. They see a nearly flat response for L = 35, supported by tensor-network simulations up to L = 85 whose curves collapse onto one universal function. The paper calls this an "encouraging first indication," not a measurement of the scaling exponent.

### Analyst checks

I recomputed a few numbers from the paper's own values to see how tightly the data pin the predictions:

| Comparison | Measured | Predicted | Offset |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ising E2/E1 | 2.08(4) | 2 | 2.0 σ |
| TCI free, E2/E1 | 1.45(25) | 4/3 | 0.5 σ |
| Same, but versus fixed value 2 | 1.45(25) | 2 | 2.2 σ below |
| E1(η=1)/E1(free) | 0.91(15) | 4/3 | 2.8 σ below |
| E1(intermediate)/E1(free) | 0.57(15) | 2/5 | 1.1 σ above |
| E2/E1 at η ≈ 0.39 | 2.5(4) | 10/3 | 2.1 σ below |
| Same, versus fixed value 2 | 2.5(4) | 2 | 1.3 σ above |

So the Ising ratio is sound, but several TCI numbers sit 2–3 σ from prediction in either direction, and the data can't separate 2.5(4) from the fixed-boundary value 2, only from the free-boundary value 4/3. The authors attribute these deviations to the ~1.7% uncertainty in V2 and show a shaded theory band in their plots, which does cover most points. That explanation is plausible and quantitative (the ratios are steep functions of V2 near TCI), but it shifts the burden: the experiment tests the TCI prediction only to the precision with which the interaction strength is known.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "observed the spectrum" means resolving a rich level structure. The paper actually says it resolves the lowest four even-parity peaks for L ≥ 19 (plus a few odd ones), as Gaussian bumps whose width is comparable to the level spacing. It extracts ratios at the ~2–4% level for Ising and ~15–20% for the harder TCI cases.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the 2:4:6:8 ladder is a distinctly CFT-specific test. The paper actually says (and the physics confirms) that the Ising ladder follows from massless fermions with linear dispersion plus fermion-filling constraints. That is strong evidence for those two ingredients, but the Ising CFT is the free-fermion case, so this spectrum alone does not test anything *interacting*. The TCI results are where interactions matter, and they are the weaker data.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the critical point was found from the experimental spectrum. The paper actually says the Ising critical point comes from finite-size scaling of a numerical order parameter, and for TCI the interaction strength V2 and detuning were set by numerically searching for parameters at which the *Hamiltonian's* low-lying levels match TCI predictions (E2/E1 → 4/3, E3/E1 → 5/3 after extrapolation). The experiment then checks whether the hardware realizes that Hamiltonian's spectrum. That validates the mapping from Hamiltonian to atoms, which is a legitimate test, but the claim "the CFT emerges" leans on the numerics for tuning.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "boundary condition" is a direct measurement. The paper actually infers it from the combination of an edge order parameter (σ_edge) and the gap ratios, with the three boundary cases distinguished by only a few data points each.

# 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**1. Modulation-ramp-probe spectroscopy.** The trick is converting a hard-to-measure many-body eigenstate question into an atom count by ramping to a phase where counts are diagonal in the energy basis. Combined with Gaussian pulse shaping, it gives parity-resolved, many-body spectroscopy on arrays of tens of atoms. This is new to the reader, and (as far as I could tell from the prior-work citations) new to the field *in this form*: modulation spectroscopy exists in cold-atom physics, but applying it to resolve finite-size CFT levels with symmetry-selective local control is the advance.

**2. Local control as a boundary-condition dial.** Using ancillary atoms and a position-dependent detuning (Eq. 5 profile) to tune edges between CFT fixed points, including probing the unstable intermediate one, is a rare example of using a simulator to turn the *boundary* of a critical system into a knob.

**Predictive Content Check.**
1. **Falsifiable handle.** The paper makes several numerical predictions that could have failed: even-parity ladder 2:4:6:8 and odd-parity 3:5:7 for Ising; E2/E1 = 4/3 (free), 2 (fixed), 10/3 (intermediate) and gap ratios 2/5 and 4/3 for TCI; a constant low-frequency dynamical structure factor. Most of the Ising predictions held; two or three TCI numbers are 2–3 σ off in either direction and are explained by the V2 uncertainty. This is a genuine prediction test (the theory numbers were fixed in advance), not a relabeling of known data, though the test is partly retrospective in that the TCI operating point was tuned numerically.
2. Formalism-load check: not triggered; the machinery plainly generates the reported numbers.

# 6. Limitations & Open Questions

1. **Few levels, small sizes.** Only the lowest four even-parity peaks are resolved for L ≥ 19, and L = 7 does not collapse onto the universal ratios. Resolving more of the tower (and degeneracies) is open. **(A) Consensus** — the paper states the density-of-states limit and the coherence-time cap directly. **(paper Figs. 2, 6; Methods)**

2. **TCI precision is thin.** The free-boundary TCI feature is a single skewed peak fit by two Gaussians, giving E2/E1 = 1.45(25); this separates 4/3 from 2 only at about 2σ, and the 10/3 intermediate prediction is measured as 2.5(4). **(A) Consensus** — the large error bars are in the paper; the σ-level translation is my own arithmetic. **(paper Fig. 4b, 5d; analyst inference for σ-levels)**

3. **Results depend on how well V2 is known.** Near TCI the predicted ratios are steep in V2, so a ~1.7% uncertainty produces the shaded band that absorbs the deviations. If V2 were pinned independently to <0.5%, the tests would become sharper (and might fail). **(B) Contested** — reasonable people would disagree about whether "consistent within the V2 band" is a confirmation or an under-powered test. **(paper Methods, Extended Data Fig. 6; analyst inference)**

4. **Tuning and test are entangled for TCI.** Operating parameters were chosen numerically so that the model Hamiltonian's low-lying levels match TCI predictions; the experiment then tests that hardware realizes that model. That is a valid test of the platform, but it is not an independent discovery of a critical point from data alone. **(C) Speculative** — my reading of the Methods; the authors may regard the numerical search as standard practice. **(analyst inference)**

5. **Dynamical structure factor is a first look.** The constant response is "encouraging," not a fit of a scaling exponent with an error bar, and a flat response could in principle have other causes. **(A) Consensus** — the paper itself uses "first indication." **(paper §Universal dynamical response)**

6. **Post-selection removes 20–70% of runs.** Criteria (rearrangement success, erasure detection, no double excitation, pulse delivery) are not obviously outcome-dependent, so bias is probably small, but the paper gives no sensitivity analysis. **(C) Speculative** — I am extrapolating from a Methods description of the filters. **(analyst inference)**

7. **Limited data access.** Data and code are available "upon request" only. **(A) Consensus** — stated in the paper. **(paper §Data/Code availability)** Note also that the senior author is a co-founder of a neutral-atom company and one author is employed there (disclosed in the paper); I am flagging this neutrally, not as a criticism of the results.

8. **Beyond Ising/TCI.** The Discussion claims the technique can diagnose *a priori unknown* universality classes, but all demonstrations here are classes whose spectra were predicted beforehand. Whether the method can *identify* an unknown class from a handful of low-resolution ratios is untested. **(B) Contested** — plausible but unproven extrapolation. **(paper Discussion; analyst inference)**

# 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

The authors ask whether a programmable neutral-atom array can reveal the hidden spectral fingerprint of a quantum critical point. They realize an open chain of up to 35 $^{88}$Sr Rydberg atoms with the blockade constraint, prepare a state near the ground state by a slow sweep, then drive the chain with a weak oscillating detuning at a scanned frequency. Wherever the drive frequency matches an energy gap, population moves to an excited state; a final ramp converts that into a countable change in atom number. The peaks in this response trace out the many-body energy levels.

At the Ising critical point, the measured peaks in 19–35 atom chains fall at 2:4:6:8 for even-parity levels and 3:5:7 for odd-parity levels (accessed by a reflection-antisymmetric local detuning), after rescaling by chain length. This collapse across lengths is what makes them *universal*. Momentum-resolved shaking shows linear dispersion, and the extracted velocity agrees with numerics. Near the tricritical point, where the underlying theory is interacting, they use local detunings to switch the chain edges between "free" and "fixed" boundary behavior and measure gap ratios that distinguish the cases (4/3 versus 2, plus an unstable intermediate case), with uncertainties much larger than in the Ising case. Finally, in bigger chains where levels can't be resolved, a second protocol measures the dynamical structure factor and finds the flat low-frequency response predicted for the Ising energy field.

**Why frame it this way:** the paper stresses *ratios* and *collapse with L* because those are the quantities independent of the unknown velocity, which is the CFT-specific, device-independent information. The interpretive choices I made: I emphasized that the Ising result confirms free-fermion physics rather than interacting physics, and that the TCI evidence should be read with its error bars, because the abstract-level phrasing ("recovering universal energy ratios") is stronger than the 1.45(25) number alone supports. What you should take away is that this is a convincing demonstration of a new measurement tool, an accurate Ising confirmation, and a promising but statistically modest TCI confirmation.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the exact statistical construction behind "reduced χ² = 0.98" (whether the overall scale/velocity was a free parameter or fixed from independent data, and how correlated systematics enter), and the TCI tuning procedure's independence from the experimental data; I did not read the Supplementary Information or the peer-review file, which may address both.

# 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **At a quantum critical point, ratios are the signal.** The overall energy scale is device-specific, but ratios like 2:4:6:8 are set by the type of transition alone, so measuring ratios identifies the universality class without knowing the machine's clock.

2. **Shaking plus a smart readout turns many-body energy levels into peaks.** By modulating the control knob, then ramping back so atom counts line up with energy eigenstates, you can read an excitation spectrum off with simple counting, and parity-selective shakes let you separate even and odd levels.

3. **The Ising case is solid; the tricritical case is a promising first look.** The free-fermion ladder is reproduced with ~2–4% precision, while the interacting tricritical numbers carry 15–20% error bars and lean on the interaction strength being known to ~1.7%.

# 9. Shorter Summary

At a quantum phase transition, a finite chain's energy levels are predicted to fall into fixed ratios set only by the type of transition, not by device details. Until now, nobody had directly measured those ladders in a quantum simulator. This paper does it with a chain of up to 35 strontium atoms in a Rydberg array, where neighboring atoms cannot both be excited.

The method is a frequency-sweep test. The team oscillates a control knob at a chosen frequency, and when that frequency matches the gap to an excited state, the chain jumps into it. A final ramp turns the jump into a simple atom count. Peaks in the count reveal the energy levels. Using a uniform shake reaches even-symmetry levels; a position-dependent shake reaches odd-symmetry ones.

At the Ising critical point, the even levels fall at 2:4:6:8 and the odd levels at 3:5:7 once frequencies are rescaled by chain length, matching theory to a few percent. This result is expected, because the Ising point behaves like free particles. The more interesting case is the tricritical point, an interacting theory. By pinning extra atoms at the chain ends and tuning local detunings, the team switched the edges between "free" and "fixed" behavior and measured gap ratios near the predicted 4/3 and 2, plus a case near the unstable 10/3, though with large uncertainties. For chains too long to resolve individual levels, a second protocol measured a flat low-frequency response, as predicted.

The honest summary: a new, general tool, an accurate Ising confirmation, and an encouraging but statistically modest tricritical confirmation, all from one lab with data available on request. Independent replication, sharper interaction calibration, and a test on a theory not predicted in advance are the next hurdles.
