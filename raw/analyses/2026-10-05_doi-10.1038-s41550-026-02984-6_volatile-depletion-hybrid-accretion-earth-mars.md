## 1. Earth and Mars were built from two kinds of brick, and the missing sodium says which

**Hook:** Earth and Mars are both short on "moderately volatile" elements such as sodium, potassium and zinc, and this paper argues that the *shape* of that shortage records how each planet was assembled: Earth mostly from pebbles that lost their volatiles in a hot gas envelope, Mars mostly from already-dried-out asteroid-like bodies.

---

## 2. Big-Picture Context

**Paper type and stakes.** This is a modelling-and-inference paper: it builds a physical model of how elements are lost while a planet grows, then uses Bayesian model comparison to ask which mixture of building blocks best reproduces the measured compositions of Earth's and Mars's mantles. At stake is a live debate about how rocky planets form at all.

There are two competing pictures of rocky-planet growth. In the classical picture, kilometre-sized bodies (planetesimals) collide and merge over tens of millions of years, ending with a few giant impacts, one of which made the Moon. In the newer pebble-accretion picture, a seed body sweeps up millimetre-to-centimetre grains (pebbles) drifting through the gas disk, which can build a large protoplanet within the few million years that the gas disk survives. For Earth and Mars, pebble accretion has met two strong objections (Morbidelli, Kleine & Nimmo, arXiv:2411.09271): their isotopes lack the outer-disk material drifting pebbles should bring, and no pebble model had reproduced Earth's gradual volatile depletion; they argued pebbles should give a sharp, step-like pattern instead.

The depletion itself has been known for half a century: plot each element's mantle abundance against how easily it evaporates, and it falls steadily as volatility rises. Mars shows the same trend, a little less depleted. Three explanations have competed: a hot early disk that never let volatiles condense; small bodies (planetesimals) that melted and degassed early, heated by short-lived $^{26}$Al and $^{60}$Fe or by collisions (the asteroid Vesta is the poster child); and loss during giant impacts. Recent work makes the hot-disk option look less likely: disks driven by magnetic winds stay near 300 K at Earth's distance, and the Ryugu and Bennu samples show that primitive, volatile-rich dust was widespread.

This paper adds a fourth mechanism and then mixes it with the second. A protoplanet that grows by pebble accretion wraps itself in a hydrogen–helium envelope, which heats up as the planet grows. Pebbles falling through it lose any element whose evaporation temperature is lower than the temperature at the envelope's base, and convection carries that vapour back out to the disk. The authors combine pebble-grown bodies (which lose volatiles this way) with Vesta-like planetesimals (which lost theirs earlier and more sharply), and ask what mixture reproduces each planet.

**Prior Belief Check.** This result *complicates* the current consensus rather than overturning it. The planetesimal-collision picture remains the mainstream for the terrestrial planets, and the paper deliberately frames its answer as a hybrid, not a win for pebbles. For the pebble-accretion community (Johansen, Lambrechts and Bizzarro are central figures in it), the outcome is expected; for skeptics, the notable claim is that a pebble model now reproduces Earth's gradual depletion trend, answering one of the specific objections above. Experts will not be surprised that a mixture of building blocks fits two depletion curves; the open question is whether the fit is *diagnostic* (only this mixture works) or merely *permissive* (this mixture is one of several that work). Much of this analysis is about that distinction.

**Replication & Convergence Note.** This is one group's model, but it is not alone: Olson, Sharp & Garai (University of New Mexico; arXiv:2601.08958, read only as an AI summary) independently model volatile loss in pebble-accreting protoplanets with a different atmosphere model and longer gas-escape timescales, and also find that Earth's depletion is best matched by merging a volatile-depleted target of about 0.7 Earth masses with smaller, less-depleted impactors. That is partial convergence within the same theoretical school (Olson & Sharp's earlier tungsten-isotope model is cited here as support). Real independent confirmation would mean either a group from the planetesimal-collision tradition testing the same data with equally flexible non-pebble mixtures, or an independent observable, such as the core sulfur content the paper predicts, landing in the predicted range.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

### What "volatility" means here, and why it is plotted on a temperature axis

Every element condenses out of a cooling gas at some temperature; heated, it sublimates (goes straight from solid to vapour) at about the same temperature. The paper uses the temperature at which half of an element has condensed, computed for the pressure at the bottom of a protoplanet's envelope, as that element's "sublimation temperature". It is a ranking more than a precise threshold: aluminium and calcium sit near 1,700–1,800 K, magnesium and silicon near 1,450–1,480 K, lithium and manganese near 1,300 K, sodium and potassium near 1,050 K, fluorine and zinc near 700–750 K, indium near 460 K. Everything between roughly 1,400 K and 450 K is called "moderately volatile": less volatile than water or carbon, more volatile than the rock-forming bulk.

The abundances are normalised twice, to the Sun's composition and to aluminium, so that "1" means "the same ratio to aluminium as in the Sun" and "0.1" means "only a tenth as much as the Sun would predict". Your chemistry background covers the rest: sodium and potassium chlorides and silicates vaporise easily; aluminium and calcium oxides do not.

> **Analogy:** It's a memory hierarchy sorted by how easily data gets evicted. The refractory elements are pinned pages that never leave; moderately volatile elements are pages with progressively shorter time-to-live. Plot "fraction still resident" against "eviction threshold", and you can read the history of how hot the system ran and for how long.
>
> **Breaks when:** you treat it as one machine with one temperature history. A planet is a merge of many bodies, each with its own heat history, so the curve you see is an average over several eviction histories. Unpicking that average is the whole job of this paper.

### Who is depleted, and by how much (the data)

*Figure 1 (from the paper): abundances in Earth's mantle (blue), Mars's mantle (red), the asteroid Vesta (yellow, from HED meteorites), the Ryugu and Bennu samples (green/cyan) and two chondrite types (crosses), plotted against each element's sublimation temperature, hotter on the left.*

**What to look at:** Ryugu and Bennu stay on the "1" line all the way across: they are undepleted. Earth and Mars slope down gently from about 1,400 K, Earth below Mars. Vesta is the opposite shape: flat at 1 until about 1,150 K, then it falls off a cliff. A gentle slope and a cliff are the two "signatures" the paper mixes.

For scale: Earth's potassium is about one-sixth of the solar ratio and Mars's about one-third.

### Planetesimals that dried out early: the Vesta curve

Vesta is a 500 km asteroid that melted, formed a core, and lost almost all its sodium, potassium and zinc. Meteorites from it (the HED group) give its composition. The authors treat Vesta as the representative of a population of early, melted, degassed planetesimals, and summarise its depletion as a smooth step: elements well above about 1,135 K are kept, those well below are gone, with the change happening over a width of about 60 K. They are agnostic about *why* Vesta lost its volatiles (radioactive heating, collisions, or a magma ocean).

> **Analogy:** Vesta is a server that went through one fast, very hot reboot: everything below a fixed priority threshold was flushed, everything above survived. The result is a clean cut-off, a step function in what survived.
>
> **Breaks when:** you assume all early planetesimals had the same threshold. Vesta is one sample. The paper itself finds that Mars fits better with a planetesimal population whose cut-off sits about 230 K hotter than Vesta's, a body type we have no meteorite for.

### Pebbles losing volatiles in a hot envelope (the new mechanism)

A protoplanet bigger than roughly the Moon holds onto a puffy envelope of disk gas, hydrogen and helium. The energy released by infalling pebbles heats this envelope, and the bigger the planet, the hotter its base: by the paper's model, the base passes indium's sublimation temperature at around a hundredth of an Earth mass, sodium's and potassium's around five to ten hundredths, and about 1,400 K at around 0.15–0.2 Earth masses. A pebble falling through the envelope loses every element whose sublimation temperature is below the base temperature at that moment; the vapour is carried up by convection and swept back into the disk by gas flowing through the outer envelope. Refractory elements (magnesium, silicon and anything less volatile) are protected: once the base is hot enough to vaporise silicates, a thick silicon-monoxide cloud layer forms a non-convecting lid that keeps their vapour trapped against the magma ocean below.

![Fig. 2](https://media.springernature.com/full/springer-static/image/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41550-026-02984-6/MediaObjects/41550_2026_2984_Fig2_HTML.png)

*Figure 2 (from the paper): (a) Fraction of each element sublimated in the envelope as a function of the protoplanet's mass (horizontal axis, log scale, up to 1 Earth mass) and the element's sublimation temperature (vertical); coloured lines mark individual elements. (b) 3D simulation snapshots of tracer particles (vapour) released in the convective envelope at 0, 0.1, 1 and 5 years.*

**What to look at:** In panel a, follow any element's line from left to right: it is safe (dark) until it crosses the dotted "<1%" curve, after which everything that element brings in is lost. Low-volatility lines (aluminium, titanium, calcium) cross only at the top right, behind the shaded "radiative zone" that protects them. In panel b, the percentages under each snapshot are the fraction of tracers still in the envelope: about 30% after one year, about 2–3% after five.

> **Concept:** A pebble-grown planet keeps an element only from the pebbles it accreted *before* its envelope got hotter than that element's sublimation temperature. After that crossing, every new pebble's share of that element is stripped and lost to the disk. Refractory elements, by contrast, are never lost, because a silicate cloud layer seals them in. So the retained fraction of any moderately volatile element equals the planet's mass at the crossing divided by its final mass, while for refractory elements the retained fraction is one.
>
> **Vehicle:** A storage system that grows by ingesting a stream of records. Its ingest buffer runs hotter as total stored volume grows. Each record field has a heat tolerance; once the buffer temperature passes a field's tolerance, that field is stripped from every subsequent record and discarded, though records already written to disk keep it. A small set of core fields is written through a separate protected path and is never stripped.
>
> **Analogy:** Planet's mass ↔ total stored volume. Envelope base temperature ↔ ingest-buffer temperature. Element's sublimation temperature ↔ field's heat tolerance. Pebbles ↔ incoming records. Escape to the disk via convection and recycling flow ↔ discarding the stripped field. The silicate cloud layer ↔ the protected write path for core fields. Final retained fraction of an element ↔ fraction of all records that were ingested before that field started being stripped.
>
> **Breaks when:** you ask about re-entry. A discarded field is gone, but stripped vapour can recondense onto grains; whether it falls back depends on grain size, which has no counterpart in the vehicle. The authors argue that recondensed volatiles land on fine dust that leaves with the gas, and that any landing on big pebbles just re-sublimates deeper down, but it is an argument, not a simulation.

### How one fits a mixture, and how one compares models

Each candidate building block has a predicted "retention curve" (fraction kept versus sublimation temperature), and a mixture inherits the mass-weighted average. The authors fit the mixing fractions to 13 elements per planet with nested sampling, a Bayesian method that also returns each model's *evidence*: how well it predicts the data averaged over all its allowed settings, which penalises unneeded components. Models are compared by the difference in log-evidence on the conventional Jeffreys scale: below 1 "not worth mentioning" (under about 3:1 odds), 1–2.5 "substantial", 2.5–5 "strong" (about 12:1 to 150:1), above 5 "decisive".

> **Analogy:** Comparing two performance models of a system by how well each predicts held-out benchmark runs, averaged over all plausible parameter settings, not just their single best tuning. A model with a knob that it doesn't need pays for it, because most of the knob's settings predict badly.
>
> **Breaks when:** the models being compared don't include the true one. Evidence ranks only the candidates on the list; a model that wins against weak rivals can still be wrong.

### Origins & Big Picture: how Earth and Mars were assembled

The Sun formed about 4.57 billion years ago inside a rotating disk of gas and dust. Within the first million years, dust grains stuck together into fluffy aggregates and then into pebbles, millimetres to centimetres across, which drifted inward through the gas as they lost orbital speed to headwind. Where pebbles piled up, clumps collapsed under their own gravity into planetesimals tens to hundreds of kilometres across. The earliest of these, formed while radioactive $^{26}$Al was still abundant, melted from the inside; iron sank to make small cores and volatiles boiled off. Iron meteorites are fragments of those cores; Vesta is a survivor of that generation, and its meteorites preserve the strongly depleted composition that this paper borrows.

From there the two growth pictures diverge. In the classical picture, planetesimals collide and merge into Moon-to-Mars-sized "embryos" within a few million years; after the gas disk dissipates, tens of millions of years of chaotic collisions among those embryos build Earth and Venus, ending with the Moon-forming impact of a body often called Theia. Mars, in this picture, is a stranded embryo that was never swallowed. Hafnium–tungsten dating supports a fast Mars: the decay of $^{182}$Hf (half-life about 9 million years) into $^{182}$W leaves a clock in the mantle, and Mars's clock says its core formed within a few million years, while the gas disk still existed. Earth's clock says it finished much later.

In the pebble picture, a planetesimal that is already large enough can grow very fast by capturing passing pebbles, because gas drag slows them down as they pass. A body can go from a Moon mass to a substantial fraction of an Earth mass while the disk survives, and that is exactly when it carries a gas envelope, which is the setting for this paper's mechanism. Pebble accretion stops when the disk disperses, after about 3–5 million years. Whatever happens after that is collisions.

The hybrid picture is the obvious compromise: both processes act, in proportions that differ by location and timing. A body growing fast in a pebble-rich region (proto-Earth here) becomes mostly pebble-made; a body whose orbit is stirred by heavier neighbours (Mars, possibly) captures pebbles inefficiently and grows mostly from planetesimals. Earth then takes one or two large impacts from other pebble-grown protoplanets after the gas is gone.

Where this sits among related systems: rocky exoplanets around other stars are probed by their host stars' compositions, and by white dwarfs whose atmospheres are polluted by shredded rocky bodies. Those polluted white dwarfs and "planet-eating" stars often show the same depletion of moderately volatile elements, which suggests that volatile loss during assembly is common, not a Solar System quirk. If the hybrid picture holds, a planet's depletion pattern carries a record of how it grew, and composition becomes a way to read formation history from the outside. This paper measures nothing new; its contribution is a model that turns two existing composition curves into proposed assembly recipes.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### Step 1 — Getting the volatility ranking right for the actual setting

**The failure it prevents:** using condensation temperatures computed for the low-pressure disk would rank elements correctly but put their thresholds in the wrong place for a planet's envelope, where pressures are hundreds of times higher.

**What the authors do:** they model the envelope's base pressure and temperature as the planet grows, then read each element's sublimation temperature off published pressure-dependent condensation curves at the pressure where the two cross. They do this for two disk-opacity settings and find the thresholds almost identical (differing by at most about 20 K, for indium), so they use the average.

**What it buys:** a volatility axis that is consistent with the envelope model it feeds. I recomputed the tabulated thresholds from the paper's own fitted formula; they match (Claim Check).

### Step 2 — Computing what a pebble-grown body keeps

**The failure it prevents:** without a physical model, "pebble accretion" has no predicted composition and cannot be tested against Earth.

**What the authors do:** a one-dimensional envelope model (outer conditions set by a 1 au disk at about 120 K, growth on an e-folding timescale of 0.7 million years) gives the base temperature at each planet mass. They assume that every moderately volatile element is lost entirely once the base passes its threshold, and that elements at or above silicon's threshold are never lost. A characteristic temperature of about 1,400 K marks the boundary. The result is a family of retention curves, one per final planet mass; see the micro-example above.

**What it buys:** a predicted depletion shape that is gentle, not step-like, because different elements are crossed at different masses. That gentleness is what answers the "pebbles should give a step" objection. The price is the sharp jump at 1,400 K, which is put in by hand.

### Step 3 — Checking that stripped vapour really leaves

**The failure it prevents:** if sublimated volatiles stayed in the envelope and later rained back onto the planet, the pebble mechanism would remove nothing.

**What the authors do:** they run 3D hydrodynamic simulations of the convecting envelope around an Earth-mass planet at 1 au, seed about 900,000 tracer particles, and delete any that reach a boundary where disk gas flows through the outer envelope (placed at twice the radius of the envelope's gravitational grip, which they call conservative). About 30% remain after a year and about 3% after five years; with the boundary placed closer in, the clearing time drops to 0.1–1 years.

**What it buys:** support for "100% loss" on timescales that are tiny compared with the hundreds of thousands of years of growth. Two caveats: the 3D run uses a warmer disk (about 250 K) and slower growth than the 1D model, and the tracers move passively with the gas, so the chemistry of recondensation is not simulated.

### Step 4 — Fitting Earth

**The failure it prevents:** guessing a formation story without checking whether it reproduces all 13 elements together.

**What the authors do:** they mix three ingredients: a proto-Earth (PE) and an impactor (Imp), both grown by pebbles and each with its own retention curve, plus Vesta-like planetesimals (Plm). The mixture's abundance of each element is just the mass-weighted average of the ingredients' retention values. This is the one equation the result rests on:

$$A_{\rm planet}(T_{\rm sub}) \;=\; \sum_i f_i \, R_i(T_{\rm sub})$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$A_{\rm planet}$ : the planet's mantle abundance of an element, normalised to the Sun and to aluminium (dimensionless; 1 = solar)
$T_{\rm sub}$ : that element's sublimation temperature (K)
$f_i$ : mass fraction of ingredient *i* in the planet (the fractions add up to 1)
$R_i$ : fraction of the element that ingredient *i* retains (1 = all of it kept)

**What this actually means:** it is a weighted blend, like computing a fleet-wide cache-hit rate from per-server hit rates weighted by each server's share of traffic. If 64% of Earth came from a body that kept 7% of its sodium and 14% from planetesimals that kept 10%, those contributions add up linearly. The model's predictive content lives entirely in the shapes of the retention curves; the fit only chooses the weights.

Before the fit, the authors correct chromium, manganese and zinc upward for the share that went into the core, using a metal–silicate partitioning factor of 1 with a wide allowed range (from one-third to three times that), and they leave out chlorine, bromine and iodine because their reference abundances in CI chondrites are disputed. The fit includes a free "intrinsic scatter" term that absorbs misfit beyond the data's error bars.

![Fig. 3](https://media.springernature.com/full/springer-static/image/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41550-026-02984-6/MediaObjects/41550_2026_2984_Fig3_HTML.png)

*Figure 3 (from the paper): (a) Earth's measured mantle abundances (black points) against sublimation temperature, with the proto-Earth curve (blue dashed), impactor curve (blue dotted), Vesta-like planetesimal curve (yellow dashed) and the fitted mixture (solid blue: 0.64 proto-Earth, 0.14 planetesimals, 0.22 impactor). The grey line is a body grown entirely by pebbles to one Earth mass. (b) Goodness-of-fit grid over impactor-to-proto-Earth mass ratio (horizontal) and planetesimal fraction (vertical), with the Bayesian 1σ and 2σ regions outlined.*

**What to look at:** In panel a, note the vertical drop at 1,400 K in all the pebble curves (the protective lid) and how the gentle slope of the blue mixture follows lithium, sodium, potassium, fluorine, zinc and indium. A fully pebble-grown Earth (grey) would be too depleted; the impactor, being smaller, is less depleted, and mixing it in lifts the curve. In panel b, the numbers in the cells barely change across the whole top band: any impactor between about 0.1 and 1 times the proto-Earth's mass, with 0–20% planetesimals, fits almost equally well.

The reference result: about 64% proto-Earth, 22% impactor, 14% planetesimals, so roughly 86% from pebble-grown bodies. Dropping the planetesimals entirely (proto-Earth plus impactor only) fits just as well (evidence difference about zero), which is why the abstract says "up to ~25%" planetesimals. Dropping the impactor costs a "substantial" amount of evidence (about 3.7:1 against). Replacing everything with planetesimals of two kinds (Vesta-like plus primitive CI-like) is "decisively" rejected. A second small impactor is allowed; a third is disfavoured.

### Step 5 — Fitting Mars

**The failure it prevents:** assuming Mars formed the same way as Earth.

**What the authors do:** they use a pebble-grown Mars (PM), which, being small, has a cooler envelope and loses only the most volatile elements, mixed with Vesta-like planetesimals. Then they let the planetesimal curve itself move: first its cut-off temperature, then both its cut-off and its width.

![Fig. 5](https://media.springernature.com/full/springer-static/image/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41550-026-02984-6/MediaObjects/41550_2026_2984_Fig5_HTML.png)

*Figure 5 (from the paper): (a) Mars's measured mantle abundances with the pebble-grown Mars curve (red dashed), the Vesta-like planetesimal curve (yellow dashed), an alternative planetesimal curve with its cut-off moved to 1,363 K (orange dashed), and the fitted mixture (solid red: 0.27 pebble-grown, 0.73 planetesimals). (b) Goodness-of-fit grid over the planetesimal cut-off temperature (horizontal) and planetesimal fraction (vertical); the brown star is the Vesta-like reference, the orange star the fitted alternative.*

**What to look at:** In panel a, the pebble-grown Mars curve stays near 1 until about 1,000 K, so here the planetesimals do most of the depleting. Look at lithium (about 1,310 K): the measured point sits well below the red mixture curve. In panel b, the best-fitting cells (lightest) lie to the right of the Vesta star, at cut-offs of about 1,300–1,400 K.

The reference result: 27% pebble-grown, 73% planetesimals. Letting the cut-off float gives 1,363 K and a 32/68 split, and evidence odds of about 3.6:1 in its favour. Letting both cut-off and width float gives the best evidence of all, about 13:1 over the reference, "strong" on the paper's own scale, with a 21/79 split. Across every Mars variant, planetesimals supply at least about 65% and pebbles at least about 10%.

### Step 6 — What it implies for sulfur

**The failure it prevents:** a mixture model that only explains the data it was fitted to.

**What the authors do:** they carry the best mixtures over to sulfur, which goes mostly into the core and so is not in the fit. Assuming planetesimal cores kept between a tenth of and all their sulfur (as iron meteorites suggest), bulk Earth would hold about 0.10–0.23 of the solar sulfur ratio and bulk Mars about 0.19–0.85.

**What it buys:** a prediction against independent data. The ranges agree with recent, higher estimates of sulfur in Earth's core and with seismically inferred Martian core compositions, rather than with older, lower estimates.

### Claim check

Done with Python (script `chk.py` in the session workspace), using the paper's Supplementary Table 1 abundances, Extended Data Table 1 thresholds, and Table 1 results. Every item is reported as a plain verdict.

**Volatility thresholds — Reproduced.** Recomputing each element's sublimation temperature from the paper's tabulated formula and pressures returns the listed values to within 1 K for every element checked. The temperature axis that everything rests on is internally consistent.

**Vesta-like planetesimal curve — Reproduced.** Refitting the smooth step to Vesta's meteorite abundances gives a cut-off of 1,136 K and a width of 63 K, matching the paper's 1,135 K and 62 K. (Without weighting by error bars the fit drifts to about 1,170 K and 85 K, so the curve leans on the precise sodium, potassium, zinc and indium points.)

**Mars, 27% pebbles and 73% planetesimals — Partly reproduced.** A simple lever-rule check (the share of the mixture that must have kept its sodium or potassium, given that Vesta-like planetesimals kept only about a tenth) gives a pebble share of about 25% from potassium and about 36% from sodium. That matches the headline. But the same reference mixture predicts nearly solar lithium for Mars, about 0.95–1.0, against a measured 0.55 with an error of 0.13: a miss of about 3σ. That single element accounts for roughly 9–11 of the reported total misfit of about 16 across 13 elements, so the reference Mars model owes most of its residual to lithium. This is why the alternative planetesimal curves, which deplete lithium, win.

**Earth, at least 75% from two pebble-grown protoplanets — Partly reproduced.** The "pebble-grown" half holds under the paper's assumptions: a lever-rule check shows that if the pebble bodies had kept their sodium and potassium, Earth's levels would allow no more than about 12% pebble material. So the fit works only because the pebble bodies themselves are strongly depleted. In my reading of the figure values, the reference mixture gives Earth's lithium as about 0.38 against a measured 0.41, consistent. The "two" protoplanets is weaker: the impactor is preferred over no impactor only at about 3.7:1, and its mass is essentially unconstrained (its posterior spreads flat over 8–43% of Earth's mass, and Fig. 3b shows nearly equal fit across impactor-to-Earth mass ratios from 0.1 to 1).

**Significant finding — the Mars headline precision is conditional, and the paper's own evidence favours a different model.** The abstract quotes Mars as "27 ± 5%" pebble material. That uncertainty applies only if the planetesimals had exactly Vesta's depletion curve. In the paper's own Table 1, the model with a freely fitted planetesimal curve is favoured over that reference by an evidence difference of 2.58 (about 13:1, "strong" on the paper's own scale), and it moves the split to 21% (range 10–30%) pebbles. The text acknowledges the range ("never less than 10%", planetesimals "over ~65%"), but the abstract and the headline numbers present the disfavoured model's ±5%. Across the paper's Mars models, the pebble share spans roughly 10–37% at 1σ. This doesn't change the qualitative conclusion (Mars is mostly planetesimals, with a real pebble share), but anyone quoting "27 ± 5%" should quote the wider range. It also means Mars's building blocks, on the paper's own fit, are not Vesta-like but a hotter-processed planetesimal type with no known meteorite sample.

**Sensitivity — which assumption decides the Earth result?** The decisive assumption is that pebble-grown bodies lose *all* of a volatile element once their envelope passes its threshold. The lever rule above shows how much hangs on it: if pebble bodies kept their volatiles, Earth could be at most about one-eighth pebble material, the opposite conclusion. The 3D tracer simulations support near-complete loss, but they are run for one planet mass, with passive tracers and a prescribed escape boundary. The Earth conclusion survives as long as envelope loss is efficient; if recondensation onto settling grains returned even a third of the stripped sodium and potassium, the pebble bodies would be too volatile-rich and the fit would need more planetesimals instead.

**Robustness (alternative fits).** The paper's variants (dropping chromium, manganese and zinc; exponential or raw-data Vesta curves; pre-depleted pebbles; primitive planetesimals; up to three impactors) all preserve its conclusions. What it does not test is an equally flexible non-pebble rival: two or more planetesimal populations with freely fitted cut-offs and widths and no pebble-grown body. The only pure-planetesimal model (Vesta-like plus primitive) has fixed curves and is rejected, yet freeing the planetesimal curve improves both planets' fits a lot. So "hybrid accretion is required" is shown against a fixed-curve rival, not a free-curve one.

Minor slips (one line): Extended Data Table 1 labels iodine as "In" and sulfur as "Si" in its last two rows; the free-planetesimal temperatures for Earth are quoted slightly differently in the Supplementary text and its figure caption (about 1,492 vs 1,489 K); neither affects any result.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the "chemical fingerprint" is a measured signature unique to hybrid accretion. The paper actually shows a *fitted mixture* that reproduces two depletion curves; the fingerprint is the model's interpretation of a gentle slope plus a sharp-cut ingredient. Other mixtures with more flexible planetesimal curves were not ruled out (see the Robustness item).

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes Earth and Mars are depleted by the same mechanism, since their curves look alike. In the paper's solution they are depleted by *different* mechanisms: Earth's depletion is carried mostly by its pebble-grown bodies (whose hot envelopes stripped them), Mars's mostly by its planetesimals (Mars's small envelope barely got hot). The resemblance is a coincidence of the mixture, which is a striking and useful point in itself.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "two protoplanets" means the data demand a giant impact. The paper's evidence for an impactor is modest (about 3.7:1), and its mass is unconstrained across roughly a factor of five. The impactor is *permitted* and consistent with a Moon-forming impact, not measured by this method.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the pebble envelope's volatile loss is observed or simulated chemically. It is modelled: base temperature from a 1D analytic envelope, complete loss assumed past each threshold, refractory loss assumed zero behind a cloud lid, and escape checked with passive tracers. The authors themselves say the transient regime before the lid forms isn't captured, and that is exactly where Earth's 10–20% depletion of magnesium and silicon would need to come from.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**1. Putting the pebble envelope into the volatility problem.** The volatility trend of Earth has been modelled for decades as a property of the disk or of planetesimals. The new idea is that a protoplanet's own atmosphere acts as a mass-dependent furnace whose threshold rises as the planet grows, so a pebble-grown body naturally ends up with a gentle, graded depletion, not a step. Combined with a silicate cloud lid that shields refractory elements, this gives a pebble-accretion model that reproduces Earth's graded depletion. That answers a specific, published objection to pebble accretion for the terrestrial planets. This is new to the field, not just to the reader (Olson, Sharp and Garai reach a similar result in parallel).

**2. Different routes to similar-looking planets.** In the paper's solution, Earth and Mars reach similar depletion curves by opposite routes: Earth through its own hot envelope, Mars by inheriting already-dried planetesimals. That reframes the trend from "a property of the inner Solar System's building blocks" to "a property of each planet's growth history", with mass deciding which mechanism dominates: only bodies above roughly a tenth of an Earth mass get envelopes hot enough to strip sodium and potassium, while below about a Moon mass, impact vapour can escape gravity instead.

### Predictive Content Check

**Falsifiable handle.** The nearest prediction that could fail is the sulfur budget: bulk Earth at about 0.10–0.23 of the solar ratio and bulk Mars at about 0.19–0.85, which the paper reports already agrees with recent higher core-sulfur estimates and Martian seismology. A second, implicit handle is the shape of the model: a pebble-grown body should show a sharp drop in depletion between the moderately refractory group (magnesium, silicon, chromium, around 1,450–1,500 K) and lithium and manganese (around 1,300 K), and bigger pebble-grown rocky planets should be more depleted than smaller ones. For rocky exoplanet material in polluted white dwarfs, that is a testable trend in principle, though the paper gives no quantitative exoplanet prediction. Beyond that, the core contribution is largely a *retrodiction*: two composition curves of 13 elements each, fitted with two to four mixing fractions plus a scatter term. The paper's fit identifies one consistent solution; it does not yet show that the solution is unique.

The formalism-load half of the check isn't triggered: the Bayesian machinery is standard and plainly does the fitting.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**The pebble retention curves are put in by assumption at both ends.** All moderately volatile material is assumed lost once its threshold is passed; all refractory material is assumed kept behind a silicate cloud lid; the boundary is set at about 1,400 K by hand. The fit is very sensitive to the first assumption (Claim Check), and the second is why the model can't produce Earth's 10–20% shortfall in magnesium and silicon. **(B) Contested** — the authors state the transient pre-lid regime isn't captured and argue loss is efficient; recondensation and retention are open questions in envelope chemistry. **(paper Methods "1D envelope model"; analyst inference on sensitivity)**

**Vesta is one sample of the planetesimal population, and Mars prefers a different one.** The planetesimal ingredient is Vesta's curve, but the best Mars fits use a planetesimal type depleted at about 1,270–1,360 K, which no known meteorite group represents; for Earth, a free planetesimal curve raises the planetesimal share to about 40% (range 26–55%). **(A) Consensus** — the paper says so explicitly and treats it as a finding ("an unidentified type of planetesimals"). **(paper Results, Discussion; SI)**

**The headline Mars precision understates the model uncertainty.** "27 ± 5%" applies to a reference model that the paper's own evidence disfavours by about 13:1 against a free-curve alternative; the reference model also misses Mars's lithium by about 3σ. **(C) Speculative** — this is my reading of the paper's Table 1 and a recomputation from its Supplementary Table 1, not something the paper discusses as a problem. **(analyst inference)**

**The impactor is weakly constrained.** Its presence is favoured at only about 3.7:1 over no impactor, its mass spans 8–43% of Earth's, and Fig. 3b's fit grid is nearly flat across impactor sizes; a dual-impactor model is equally good. The consistency with Theia is real but loose. **(C) Speculative** — the paper reports the numbers; the judgement that "two protoplanets" is more permitted than required is mine. **(analyst inference, from paper Table 1 and Fig. 4)**

**Non-pebble alternatives with the same flexibility weren't tested.** No model mixes two or more freely fitted planetesimal populations without a pebble body. Stochastic-accretion models built from variably depleted planetesimals (e.g., Sossi et al. 2022, which the Supplement cites) are the natural competitor. Until that comparison exists, "hybrid accretion is required" means "required relative to fixed Vesta-like and primitive planetesimals". **(B) Contested** — the broader field includes planetesimal-only explanations of the same trend. **(broader literature; analyst inference)**

**No isotope or timing constraints are used.** The model is purely elemental; it doesn't check its mixtures against hafnium–tungsten timing, the absence of outer-disk isotopic material in Mars, or nucleosynthetic isotope anomalies, which are the main published objections to pebble accretion. The paper notes consistency with one three-component tungsten model (Olson & Sharp) and argues isotopes are complementary. **(B) Contested** — Morbidelli, Kleine & Nimmo argue isotopes and timing disfavour pebble-built terrestrial planets; the pebble community disputes this. **(broader literature)**

**Giant impacts are assumed to lose nothing.** The model attributes no volatile loss to the impacts themselves, citing work that collisional loss of potassium matters only for bodies up to about a Moon's mass. The authors flag that loss during impacts in the gas-disk phase is poorly understood. **(A) Consensus** — paper-stated assumption and caveat. **(paper Discussion)**

**The 1D and 3D models use different settings, and the mantle compositions carry large corrections.** The 1D envelope uses a 120 K disk and 0.7-million-year growth, the 3D run 250 K and 1 million years (argued to be conservative). Core corrections for chromium, manganese and zinc carry a factor-of-three uncertainty either way, and three halogens are excluded; the Supplement shows the solutions hold without chromium, manganese and zinc. **(A) Consensus** — all stated by the paper. **(paper Methods; SI §1)**

**What should follow in 12–24 months:** a free-curve planetesimal-only comparison; envelope models that track recondensation chemistry and the pre-lid transient; a joint elemental-plus-isotopic-plus-timing fit; and an independent group (Olson, Sharp and Garai are a start) reproducing the Mars result.

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

**What the paper does.** It asks whether the familiar depletion of moderately volatile elements in Earth's and Mars's mantles can be explained by a mixture of two kinds of building block: bodies grown by pebble accretion, which lose volatiles in their own hot gas envelopes, and early planetesimals, which lost volatiles by melting, represented by the asteroid Vesta. It builds a model of the first kind, checks with 3D simulations that stripped vapour leaves the envelope within years, and then fits mixtures of these ingredients to 13 elements per planet with Bayesian model comparison.

**Key findings.** A pebble-grown body's envelope heats as it grows, giving graded volatile loss that increases with planet mass while refractory elements stay sealed in. Earth is best matched by about 86% pebble-grown material (proto-Earth plus one impactor, roughly 64/22) and about 14% Vesta-like planetesimals, a share that could reach about 40% with a freely shaped planetesimal curve; fixed-curve planetesimal-only mixtures are decisively rejected. Mars is about 27/73 pebbles to planetesimals in the reference model and about 21/79 in the paper's best-evidence model; planetesimals dominate in every variant. Earth and Mars look alike for different reasons, and the best mixtures predict sulfur contents consistent with recent higher core-sulfur estimates.

**Why I framed it this way.** The paper's title calls volatile depletion a "fingerprint" of hybrid accretion. A fingerprint implies uniqueness. What the paper shows is that a hybrid mixture *can* reproduce both planets' curves with a physically motivated pebble ingredient, which no published pebble model had done, and that pebbles can't be removed from either planet without worsening the fit, given the rival ingredients the paper offers. Whether a more flexible planetesimal-only recipe could do as well is open. So I've treated the paper as a strong demonstration of viability and a useful reframing (different planets, different depletion routes), with the precise percentages conditional on the choice of ingredients. The Mars headline is the clearest case: its ±5% is a within-model precision, while the model-to-model spread is several times larger, and the paper's own evidence favours a non-Vesta planetesimal.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** whether the Bayesian likelihood is computed in linear or logarithmic abundance. The paper says its simple goodness-of-fit is linear but doesn't state the form for the Bayesian fit, and the fitted Earth scatter term (about 0.09 in normalised units, read off Fig. 4) is comparable to the abundances of fluorine, zinc and indium themselves. If the likelihood is linear, the most volatile elements carry little weight in Earth's fit, and the fit is driven mainly by lithium, manganese, sodium, potassium and the refractory group. I couldn't check this without running the authors' code. Secondarily, the Olson–Sharp–Garai comparison rests on an AI summary of their paper, not a verbatim read.

**Revision notes:** the referee pass added the lever-rule demonstration that Earth's and Mars's depletions come from opposite ingredients, the Mars lithium misfit, and the untested free-curve planetesimal-only alternative; it also caught that the "0.35" in the Fig. 3 legend is the impactor-to-Earth mass ratio, not the scatter term.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **A growing planet's own atmosphere can boil off its volatiles.** Pebbles falling through a young planet's hot hydrogen envelope lose their sodium, potassium and zinc, and the bigger the planet, the more they lose: a natural way to make Earth's gently sloped volatile shortage.

2. **Earth and Mars look alike for opposite reasons.** In this model Earth stripped its own building material in its hot envelope, while Mars was built mostly from small bodies that had already dried out before they arrived.

3. **The recipe is a viable fit, not a unique one.** Roughly seven-eighths pebbles for Earth and three-quarters planetesimals for Mars reproduce the data, but the exact proportions shift substantially with what one assumes the dried-out bodies looked like.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Earth and Mars both have less sodium, potassium, zinc and other "moderately volatile" elements than the Sun, and the shortage grows steadily the more easily an element evaporates. This paper asks whether the shape of that shortage reveals how each planet was assembled.

The authors combine two building blocks. The first is a body grown by pebble accretion, sweeping up millimetre-to-centimetre grains in the gas disk. Such a body wraps itself in a hydrogen–helium envelope that gets hotter as it grows; incoming pebbles lose any element that evaporates below the envelope's base temperature, and convection carries that vapour away, while rock-forming elements stay sealed under a silicate cloud layer. Bigger pebble-grown bodies end up more depleted. The second block is early planetesimals like the asteroid Vesta, which melted and lost their volatiles abruptly.

Fitting mixtures of these to 13 elements, the authors find that Earth is best matched by about 86% pebble-grown material (a proto-Earth plus one impactor, consistent with a Moon-forming impact) and up to about a quarter Vesta-like planetesimals. Mars is best matched by about a quarter pebble-grown material and three-quarters planetesimals. In this picture the two planets look chemically similar for opposite reasons: Earth stripped its own pebbles, Mars inherited dried-out planetesimals.

Two caveats matter. The headline Mars figure, 27 ± 5% pebbles, assumes Vesta-like planetesimals; the paper's own best-supported model uses a hotter-processed planetesimal type with no known meteorite counterpart and gives roughly 21%, with a model-to-model range of about 10–37%. And the claim that a hybrid is required was tested only against planetesimal mixtures with fixed shapes, not equally flexible ones. The result is a strong demonstration that pebble accretion can reproduce Earth's volatile pattern, which answers a published objection, rather than a unique reading of the planets' history. A testable prediction is the bulk sulfur content of both planets, which so far agrees with recent higher estimates.
