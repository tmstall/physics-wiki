# Direct experimental test of Feynman's path integral postulates with single photons

## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Watching a Photon Take Every Path at Once**

Seventy-eight years after Feynman wrote down the two rules his path-integral formulation of quantum mechanics rests on, a South China Normal University team has directly measured — rather than simply assumed — both of them, by reconstructing the individual contribution of every one of 1.4 million possible photon paths through a tabletop optical setup.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Ordinary quantum mechanics, as most people first learn it, evolves a wavefunction forward in time using the Schrödinger equation. Feynman's 1948 alternative — the path integral — instead says: a particle going from point A to point B effectively takes *every possible route* between them simultaneously, and the final probability comes from combining a tiny complex "contribution" from each route. This picture is mathematically equivalent to the Schrödinger equation (the paper notes its propagator equation "is equivalent to" it), but it has become the working language for huge swaths of modern theoretical physics — quantum field theory, the path to quantum gravity, quantum statistical mechanics — precisely because it's often easier to reason about "sum over all the ways this could happen" than to solve a differential equation directly.

Feynman built this framework on two specific postulates, stated in his original paper, that have never been directly tested until now. Postulate I says the true, final probability comes from adding up all the path contributions *as complex numbers first* and squaring the result afterward — allowing different paths to reinforce or cancel each other (interference) — rather than squaring each path's contribution individually and then adding those already-positive numbers (which is what a naive "classical" probability sum would do and which permits no interference at all). Postulate II is more specific and, on the face of it, stranger: it says every single path, no matter how wild or physically unreasonable-looking, contributes a term of *exactly the same magnitude* — the only thing that varies from path to path is the term's phase, which is fixed by that path's classical action (measured in units of ħ). Wide swaths of theoretical physics have been built on top of these two statements for eight decades without anyone measuring, path by path, whether they're actually true of a real physical system.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** this is a full experimental Article — a from-scratch optical apparatus and measurement campaign, not a Letter or a purely theoretical proposal. The stakes are foundational-but-quiet: this doesn't threaten to overturn quantum mechanics (both postulates are guaranteed by standard QM's own internal mathematics), but it closes a genuine, long-standing gap between "we've always assumed this because the math requires it" and "we've actually watched it happen, path by path, in the lab" — and along the way demonstrates a measurement technique precise enough to reconstruct over a million individual quantum path amplitudes with useful fidelity.

**Prior Belief Check:** nothing here is surprising to experts in the sense of threatening established physics — Postulate I and Postulate II are mathematical consequences of the path integral's equivalence to the (extraordinarily well-tested) Schrödinger equation, so a genuine violation would have been a shock on the scale of overturning quantum mechanics itself, which no one in the field expected or was hoping for. What *is* genuinely uncertain going in, and what the paper is honestly testing, is whether the specific measurement technique — a weak-coupling "pointer" scheme for extracting complex path amplitudes without destroying them — is precise enough to resolve these predicted patterns against real experimental noise. The authors' own prior version of this apparatus (87.6% propagator fidelity) was, in their own words, "insufficient" for this exact task. This is best read as a high-precision confirmatory/methodological achievement rather than a paper that could have gone either way on the physics.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** single-group result, and more specifically a direct methodological continuation of the same lab's own earlier work — a 2023 *Nature Photonics* paper (ref. 44 in this paper) by an overlapping author list, which first demonstrated propagator measurement and a two-path "least action" test at 87.6% fidelity. This paper is that same apparatus, substantially upgraded (to 98.5% fidelity), pointed at a much bigger question. No independent lab has performed this specific postulate test, on this or any other platform. Independent confirmation would look like a different group — ideally using a different physical platform for the "which-path" degree of freedom (e.g., a superconducting-qubit or trapped-ion pointer instead of photon polarization) — reproducing the flat-probability-vs-action pattern of Postulate II, since weak-coupling measurement schemes carry their own platform-specific systematic-error profiles that a single lab's error budget can't fully rule out.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Postulate I — combine signed numbers first, take magnitude last.** Standard probability theory says you'd compute each contribution's likelihood separately, then add those (already-positive) likelihoods. Feynman's Postulate I insists on the opposite order.

> **Analogy:** think of a distributed reduction/accumulator pattern — like a MapReduce job where many workers each report a signed (or, here, complex-phased) partial result. Postulate I is a "combine-then-finalize" reduction: sum all the signed contributions first, and only take the magnitude (square it) at the very end — which lets contributions cancel each other out (destructive interference) or reinforce (constructive interference) before you ever look at a final number. The classical alternative is "finalize-then-combine": take the magnitude of each worker's contribution individually, throwing away its sign/phase, and only then sum those already-positive numbers — which makes cancellation permanently impossible, the same way summing floating-point magnitudes loses whatever information the original signs carried.
>
> **Breaks when:** in a real MapReduce job, "signed" is a choice of convention and either reduction order is a valid, well-defined computation — you pick the one that answers your actual question. In quantum mechanics, only the combine-then-finalize order gives the experimentally correct answer; the "classical" order isn't a different-but-valid convention, it's simply wrong once interference is present. The analogy explains the mechanics of the two computations, not why nature insists on one of them.

**Postulate II — every path gets equal weight; only a phase "tag" varies.** This is the less intuitive of the two. It says a path that looks absurd — say, one that jitters backward and forward wildly — contributes a term exactly as large in magnitude as the smooth, "obvious," classical-looking path. The only thing that distinguishes paths is a phase, fixed by that path's classical action $S_j$ (in units of ħ): $\phi_j = Ae^{iS_j/\hbar}$, with the same real constant $A$ for every path.

> **Analogy:** think of a uniform-weight brute-force search over an enormous candidate space — every candidate gets identical routing priority/resources, the way a well-designed load balancer or hash function gives every request the same expected treatment regardless of payload. The "interesting" information about each candidate isn't encoded in how much weight it gets; it's encoded entirely in a separate metadata tag attached to it (here, a phase derived from the classical action) that only matters once you start combining candidates together and their tags start reinforcing or canceling each other.
>
> **Breaks when:** a real load balancer's uniform weighting is an engineering choice you could relax (priority queues exist for a reason). Postulate II's equal weighting isn't a design choice available to be relaxed — it's asserted as an exact structural fact about how nature assembles a quantum amplitude, and the paper's whole point is to check whether that structural fact actually holds up under direct measurement rather than being taken as a given.

**The propagator — breaking one long journey into many small, individually measurable hops.** Rather than trying to measure a whole path's contribution in one shot, the experiment (following the general path-integral formalism) breaks each path into a chain of short propagation steps and represents the full path amplitude as the product of the propagators for each step — $K(x_{p_k},t_k;x_{p_{k-1}},t_{k-1})$.

> **Analogy:** like decomposing a distributed system's end-to-end behavior into a chain of separately measurable per-hop link transformations, then reconstructing the whole path's effect as the ordered product of those hops — the same logic behind measuring a multi-stage pipeline's per-stage transfer function instead of trying to characterize the entire pipeline as one opaque black box. Each hop is small and simple enough to actually calibrate; the complicated global answer is assembled afterward, in software (or here, in post-processing), by multiplying the calibrated pieces together.
>
> **Breaks when:** in a real pipeline, per-hop measurements can usually be taken with negligible disturbance to the signal. Here, every "hop" measurement is a quantum measurement, which unavoidably disturbs the very quantity you're trying to characterize — which is exactly the problem the next concept exists to manage.

**Weak-value ("pointer") measurement — reading a quantity without destroying it.** A propagator is a complex number (magnitude and phase); directly, forcefully measuring the photon's position at every intermediate step would collapse its wavefunction and destroy the very interference the experiment is trying to observe. Instead, the experiment couples the photon's polarization — a separate, "pointer" degree of freedom — only *weakly* to its position, then reads out the pointer's small average deflection (via expectation values of Pauli operators, Eq. 8) to infer the real and imaginary parts of the propagator, disturbing the position wavefunction only slightly.

> **Analogy:** exactly like attaching a low-overhead profiler to a running program instead of stopping it in a debugger. A hard breakpoint (a strong, direct measurement) gives you a perfectly precise snapshot but halts execution and changes the program's subsequent behavior; a lightweight sampling profiler (weak coupling) barely perturbs the running program, at the cost of each individual sample being noisier and needing many repeated runs to average out into a trustworthy signal. This experiment is fundamentally a profiling exercise: tap a small pointer signal at each of many space-time points, accept that each individual tap is imprecise, and rely on high measurement fidelity and averaging to reconstruct the full execution trace (the propagator landscape) without ever "stopping" the photon's wavefunction.
>
> **Breaks when:** a software profiler's overhead is a purely practical, engineering-tunable inconvenience — with enough budget you could in principle profile with zero overhead. In quantum mechanics, the disturbance-versus-precision trade-off in a weak measurement is fundamental (a Heisenberg-type limit), not an engineering limitation that better hardware could someday remove entirely — you can shift the trade-off, as this paper's improvements do, but you can never eliminate it.

**Central analogy for this paper:** A low-overhead profiler that doesn't perturb the run.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**From "postulates" to a measurement plan.** The paper's real technical move is recognizing that both postulates can be tested *if and only if* you can reconstruct the individual complex amplitude $\phi_j$ for a very large number of specific paths $j$ with enough precision to see (a) whether the coherent sum $P_q = |\sum_j \phi_j|^2$ matches what a camera actually measures, and (b) whether $|\phi_j|$ really stays constant while $\arg(\phi_j)$ tracks the classical action. Because $\phi_j$ is a product of per-step propagators (Section 3), the problem reduces to measuring many individual propagators with high fidelity and multiplying them together in software.

**Measuring a propagator without collapsing it.** The core measurement equation is

$$K(x_{p_k},t_k;x_{p_{k-1}},t_{k-1}) = \frac{\langle f_k|\hat\sigma_x|f_k\rangle + i\langle f_k|\hat\sigma_y|f_k\rangle}{\Phi(t_{k-1})\,|\psi^*(x_{p_{k-1}},t_{k-1})|}$$

**Symbol definitions:** $\hat\sigma_x,\hat\sigma_y$ : Pauli operators measured on the photon's polarization "pointer" · $|f_k\rangle$ : the pointer's final state after weak coupling and postselection at position $x_{p_k}$ · $\Phi(t_{k-1})$ : a normalization factor related to the zero-momentum component of the wavefunction · $\psi^*(x_{p_{k-1}},t_{k-1})$ : the (independently, directly measured on the camera) spatial wavefunction magnitude at the earlier time step.

**What this actually means:** the numerator — an ordinary pair of polarization expectation-value measurements, exactly the kind of thing any quantum-optics lab does routinely — gives you the real and imaginary parts of the propagator directly, like reading two dial gauges to get a single complex number. No tomography of the full spatial wavefunction is needed at every step; only the pointer's small average tilt has to be read out, which is what makes the weak-coupling ("profiler") approach practical at the scale of tens of thousands of individual propagator measurements.

**Building 1.4 million paths out of 85 measured numbers.** Crucially, the experiment does not measure 1.4 million paths one at a time. It discretizes space into $N=17$ positions and time into $M=5$ steps, directly measures the individual propagators connecting adjacent positions at adjacent time steps, and then *computes* every full path amplitude as the product of the relevant propagators (Eq. 9), $\phi_j = \prod_{k=1}^{M}[\ldots]/\mathcal{N}\prod_{k=1}^{M}|\psi^*(x_{p_k},t_k)|$ — a straightforward multiplication done afterward in software, not a separate physical measurement per path.

**Worked example — where "1.4 million" actually comes from.** With $N=17$ possible positions at each of $M=5$ time steps (and the initial point fixed), the number of distinct paths is simply the number of ways to choose one position at each of the 5 steps:

$$N^M = 17^5$$

**Symbol definitions:** $N=17$ : number of discretized spatial positions the photon could occupy at a given time step (the paper's own choice of measurement grid) · $M=5$ : number of free time steps in the path (the initial space-time point is fixed).

**What this actually means:** this is exactly the same "multiply the branching factor at each layer" logic used to count root-to-leaf paths through a layered decision tree or a lattice graph in a dynamic-programming path-counting problem. Checking the arithmetic directly: $17^2=289$, $17^3=4{,}913$, $17^4=83{,}521$, $17^5=1{,}419{,}857$ — which matches both numbers the paper quotes: 83,521 paths when *both* endpoints are pinned down (4 free intermediate steps, used for the Postulate I test in Fig. 3), and 1,419,857 paths when only the starting point is pinned and every possible ending point is included (5 free steps, used for the Postulate II test in Fig. 4). All 1.4 million numbers on the headline figure come from multiplying together a comparatively modest number of actually-measured propagators (the paper reports 85 measured propagator combinations) — the "million-path" achievement is really a measurement-and-bookkeeping achievement, not 1.4 million separate physical measurements.

**Testing Postulate I.** With every $\phi_j$ reconstructed for a fixed start and end point, the team compares three curves as a function of final position: what the camera directly sees ($P_e$), the quantum prediction from coherently summing the measured amplitudes ($P_q=|\sum_j\phi_j|^2$), and what a classical, non-interfering sum of the same amplitudes would predict ($P_c=\sum_j|\phi_j|^2$). $P_e$ tracks $P_q$ closely (mean absolute percentage error $R_q=4.45\%$, a state-vector fidelity of 94.4%) while $P_c$ visibly deviates — direct, quantitative evidence that the photon's observed statistics come from a coherent sum of path amplitudes, not an incoherent one.

**Testing Postulate II.** The team groups all 1,419,857 reconstructed paths by two quantities: total path length $L$ (Fig. 4A) and classical action $S$ (Fig. 4B). If Postulate II is right, the *mean* probability per path should stay roughly flat across both — a nontrivial, checkable claim, since naive intuition might expect paths closer to the "obvious" classical trajectory to somehow dominate. The data support flatness (mean absolute percentage error $R_p=17.4\%$, against a numerically simulated noise-floor expectation of 18.0%, meaning the observed spread is consistent with the apparatus's own measurement noise rather than a real departure from flatness). Separately, the phase $\theta=\arg(\phi_j)$ is shown (Fig. 4C) to scale linearly with $S/\hbar$ with a slope matching the predicted 1:1 relationship — direct confirmation of the action-determines-phase half of Postulate II. Combined, the team reports an overall state-vector fidelity of 94.7% between the measured and theoretically predicted amplitude structure.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** you might assume this experiment could have falsified quantum mechanics if the postulates hadn't held up. **The paper actually tests something narrower:** since the path integral is mathematically equivalent to the (already extremely well-tested) Schrödinger equation, a real failure here would most plausibly indicate a flaw in the measurement apparatus or its theoretical model, not new physics — the honest headline achievement is a high-fidelity measurement technique, not a could-go-either-way test of quantum theory itself.

> **Watch:** you might assume "1.4 million paths" means 1.4 million individual physical measurements were performed. **The paper actually measures far fewer things directly** — 85 propagator combinations across a 17-position, 5-time-step grid — and computes all 1,419,857 path amplitudes afterward by multiplying the measured propagators together in software (Eq. 9). The scale of the achievement is in reconstructing a probability landscape this large from a comparatively modest set of underlying measurements, not in performing that many separate experiments.

> **Watch:** you might assume the weak-value/pointer-based technique straightforwardly reveals "the actual path" a photon took, free of interpretational controversy. **The paper's own closing claim — that these results support paths being "more than mere mathematical artifacts"** — leans on a specific, still-contested position in the weak-value/quantum-trajectories literature (associated with Aharonov and Vaidman) about whether weak-measurement-derived trajectories represent objective physical reality or are artifacts of the particular weak-coupling-plus-postselection procedure used to define them. The paper is careful to frame this as "strong insight" toward a debate, not a resolution of it — worth noticing, since it's easy to read the framing as more settled than the authors themselves claim.

> **Watch:** you might assume "all paths have equal magnitude" (Postulate II) means all paths contribute equally to what you actually observe. **The paper's own math says otherwise:** equal magnitude applies only to the individual terms $\phi_j$ *before* they're summed. The final, physically observed probability $P=|\sum_j\phi_j|^2$ still varies enormously with position, because the equal-magnitude terms carry very different phases that interfere constructively in some places and destructively in others — that interference pattern is precisely what Postulate I (not Postulate II) is about, and the two postulates work together, not independently, to produce the final observable statistics.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**1. A weak-coupling measurement technique upgraded specifically to survive multiplication.** The paper's real advance isn't a new theoretical idea — Feynman's postulates are 78 years old, and weak-value pointer measurement is itself an established technique (cited to earlier work, including the same group's own 2023 paper). What's new is recognizing that reconstructing a *path* amplitude means multiplying together up to five separately-measured propagators in a row, so measurement errors compound multiplicatively — and then re-engineering the apparatus (stronger pointer coupling per Eq. 16, tighter 4f-imaging precision, sub-wavelength interferometer stability, and a real-time reference-beam normalization) specifically to survive that compounding, taking single-step propagator fidelity from 87.6% to 98.5% and the propagator error metric from 24.3% down to 8.17%. That's the difference between a proof-of-concept two-path demonstration and a million-path reconstruction actually being trustworthy.

**2. Testing Postulate II at all.** Postulate I (coherent superposition) is the postulate most people would think to test — it's the "interference is real" claim most textbook treatments emphasize. Postulate II (exact equal-magnitude weighting across every path, phase fixed by action) is a much less commonly probed structural claim, and this paper is explicit that no one had directly tested it before. Grouping over a million reconstructed amplitudes by path length and by action, and showing the mean magnitude stays flat while the phase tracks $S/\hbar$ exactly, is a genuinely new empirical demonstration of a specific mathematical structure that most treatments simply assume.

**Predictive Content Check.**

- **Falsifiable handle:** present and quantified, even though theoretically the outcome was strongly expected. The concrete, could-have-failed claims are: $P_e$ should match $P_q$ (not $P_c$) to within a stated error ($R_q=4.45\%$); mean path probability should stay flat across path length and action bins to within a stated error ($R_p=17.4\%$, consistent with a simulated 18.0% noise floor); and phase should scale linearly with $S/\hbar$ at unit slope. These are genuine, numerically checkable predictions the apparatus could have failed to reproduce (and, per the authors' own account, their previous-generation apparatus was not precise enough to attempt this test at all) — so while the underlying physics was never really in doubt, whether *this measurement technique* could resolve it was a real, quantified risk.
- **Formalism load:** fires, and is judged load-bearing. The weak-value pointer-coupling algebra (Eqs. 8–26 in Materials and Methods) isn't decorative — it's the specific machinery that converts an ordinary polarization measurement into a complex propagator value, and the amplification-factor analysis (Eq. 16) is what let the team quantitatively justify their improved coupling strength. Remove the formalism and there's no route from "measure some polarizations" to "reconstruct 1.4 million path amplitudes."

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**Single-group, single-lineage result.** This is a direct upgrade of the same lab's own 2023 apparatus (ref. 44), with substantial author overlap; no independent group, and no alternative physical platform for the pointer degree of freedom, has attempted this specific postulate test. **(A) Consensus that this gap exists** — this is a straightforward authorship/citation finding, not a matter of physics interpretation. **(analyst inference)**

**Nonrelativistic, one-dimensional, single-particle regime only.** The photon is treated via a paraxial, effective-mass approximation to a nonrelativistic Schrödinger-like equation (Eqs. 6–7) — a standard, previously-validated simplification for this kind of measurement, but a real distance from the relativistic, many-body, or curved-spacetime path-integral applications (quantum field theory, quantum gravity) that motivate interest in path integrals in the first place. **(A) Consensus** — the authors themselves frame extending to "more complex quantum systems, particularly those with interactions or in curved spacetimes" as future work. **(paper, Discussion)**

**The "paths are physically real" interpretive claim rests on contested ground.** The paper's closing framing — that the results "support the view that paths... reflect a physical aspect of quantum reality" — leans on the position, associated with weak-value/weak-trajectory advocates, that pointer-based reconstructions reveal something ontologically real about a system's history rather than an artifact of the specific weak-coupling-and-postselection procedure used to define them. This is a live, unresolved disagreement in the quantum-foundations literature, not a settled matter the data alone can adjudicate. **(B) Contested** — reasonable physicists working on weak values and quantum trajectories genuinely disagree about what a reconstructed "path" represents, independent of how precisely it was measured. **(broader literature)**

**A clean headline fidelity number sits on top of a noisier underlying quantity.** The reported 94.7% fidelity for Postulate II is a single aggregate number computed from many individually noisier path-probability values (MAPE $R_p=17.4\%$), each itself built by multiplying up to five separately-measured propagators together — meaning any framing of "94.7% agreement" as a clean, low-uncertainty confirmation somewhat understates how much per-path noise is being smoothed over by that aggregation. **(C) Speculative** — the paper reports both numbers accurately and doesn't itself hide the discrepancy, but doesn't foreground this tension either; the observation that the two numbers can create different impressions is this analyst's framing point, not the authors'. **(analyst inference)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

Feynman's path integral says a particle's quantum behavior comes from adding up complex-valued contributions from every conceivable path between two points, with two specific rules governing how: the contributions must be summed coherently (as complex numbers, before squaring) rather than classically (squared individually, then summed), and every single path — however improbable-looking — contributes a term of identical magnitude, differing only in a phase set by that path's classical action. Both rules are mathematical necessities of the path-integral formalism, but neither had been directly tested, path-by-path, in an experiment.

This paper closes that gap for a genuinely large case: single photons moving through a simple optical apparatus, discretized into a grid of 17 possible positions across 5 time steps. Rather than trying to measure a whole path's contribution in one shot — which would require destructively collapsing the photon's wavefunction — the team uses the photon's own polarization as a "pointer," weakly coupling it to position at each time step and reading out its small average deflection to infer the complex-valued propagator connecting adjacent grid points. Multiplying the right set of measured propagators together (in software, after the fact) reconstructs the full complex amplitude for any of the 1,419,857 possible paths from a fixed start point to any end point across the whole discretized grid. With that reconstruction in hand, the team shows that the camera's directly observed probability distribution matches a coherent sum of these amplitudes far better than an incoherent (classical) sum would predict — confirming Postulate I — and that grouping all 1.4 million reconstructed amplitudes by path length or by classical action reveals a flat average magnitude and a phase that tracks the action exactly as Feynman's second postulate demands.

The technical story behind these results matters as much as the results themselves: the same lab's previous version of this apparatus, published in 2023, could only reliably measure a two-path scenario at 87.6% fidelity — not precise enough to attempt a million-path reconstruction, where measurement errors compound every time propagators are multiplied together. Four specific engineering upgrades (stronger pointer coupling, better imaging precision, tighter interferometer stability, and real-time normalization against source fluctuations) pushed single-step fidelity to 98.5%, which is what actually made this experiment possible.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the fine mechanics of the pointer-coupling derivation in Materials and Methods (Eqs. 10–26, converting an initial Hadamard-like pointer preparation through free-space evolution to a final polarization readout) were read and reported here rather than independently re-derived step by step; I'm trusting the paper's own algebra on exactly how measurement errors in the wavefunction magnitude $|\psi^*|$ propagate into the reported propagator error budget ($R_K = 8.17\pm3.50\%$), which a specialist in weak-value metrology would be better positioned to independently audit than I am.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **A 78-year-old assumption just got directly measured instead of taken on faith.** Feynman's path integral has always required that quantum probabilities come from adding complex amplitudes (not classical probabilities) and that every possible path contributes equally in magnitude, with only its phase set by classical action — and this experiment is the first to check both claims directly, path by path, rather than trusting the math.

2. **The "1.4 million paths" is a measurement-and-bookkeeping triumph, not 1.4 million experiments.** The team directly measured a comparatively modest set of individual propagators — the small building-block "hops" between adjacent grid points — using a low-disturbance polarization-based probing technique, then multiplied the right combinations together afterward to reconstruct every one of the 1,419,857 possible full paths.

3. **The physics wasn't really in doubt; the measurement precision was.** Both postulates are guaranteed by standard quantum mechanics' mathematical equivalence to the path integral, so this result doesn't threaten to overturn anything — the genuine achievement, and the genuine risk going in, was whether a weak-value-based measurement technique could be made precise enough (up from 87.6% to 98.5% fidelity) to actually see this pattern rather than lose it in noise.

## 9. Shorter Summary

*(350-word ceiling)*

In 1948, Richard Feynman proposed that quantum particles behave as if they take every possible path between two points simultaneously, with each path contributing a small "vote" toward the final outcome. He built this idea on two specific rules: first, that these votes must be combined as complex numbers — allowing them to reinforce or cancel each other — rather than combined as plain probabilities, which would allow no such interference; second, and more surprisingly, that every single path, no matter how strange-looking, contributes a vote of exactly the same size, differing only in a "direction" (phase) set by that path's classical trajectory cost. Despite underpinning huge areas of modern physics, from particle physics to attempts at quantum gravity, neither rule had ever been checked directly, path by path, in an experiment.

A team in China has now done exactly that, using single photons traveling through a carefully engineered optical setup. Instead of trying to measure an entire path's contribution in one go — which would destroy the delicate quantum behavior being studied — they use the photon's polarization as a gentle "probe," barely disturbing its position as it travels, and read out that probe's small average tilt to infer the complex value of each short hop along the way. By multiplying the right combinations of these measured hops together afterward, they reconstructed the full contribution of any of 1,419,857 different possible paths a photon could take across a grid of 17 positions and 5 time steps.

The results confirm both of Feynman's rules with real numbers attached: the directly observed pattern on their camera matches what you get from combining path contributions coherently, not classically, and when they sorted all 1.4 million reconstructed paths by length and by classical trajectory cost, the "vote size" stayed essentially flat across the board while the "direction" tracked exactly as predicted. None of this could have been attempted with the team's own previous apparatus, which was roughly ten times noisier — the real engineering achievement here is a measurement technique precise enough to survive multiplying many small, individually imperfect measurements together without the errors piling up into nonsense. The team suggests this supports the idea that these paths are more than a mathematical bookkeeping trick — though that particular interpretive claim remains genuinely debated among physicists who study this kind of gentle, minimally disturbing measurement.

## Appendix — Deep-Dive Discussion Log (2026-09-01)

Four-question deep dive following the initial analysis, all raised by the reader and not carried over from the paper's own text — working outward from Postulate II into the general mathematical machinery it plugs into.

**Q1 — If every path has equal magnitude, how do constructive/destructive interference happen from phase alone?** Each path's contribution $\phi_j=Ae^{iS_j/\hbar}$ is a fixed-length arrow in the complex plane; only its direction (phase) varies. Summing paths is adding these arrows tip-to-tail, so the *only* lever on the resultant arrow's length — and hence the observed probability $|\sum_j\phi_j|^2$ — is how well-aligned the arrows are, never how long any individual one is. Nearby paths with similar action point the same way and reinforce (constructive); paths whose action varies rapidly relative to $\hbar$ point in effectively random directions and cancel (destructive). Mapped onto phased-array beamforming: every antenna element radiates equal power, and the entire directional pattern (main lobe vs. sidelobes) is sculpted purely by phase relationships between elements — structurally the same mechanism. This is also why the paper needed *both* postulate tests: Postulate II alone only establishes equal-length arrows with action-set direction; Postulate I is what confirms they're combined coherently (vector sum) rather than classically (scalar sum), which is what actually produces interference.

**Q2 — How does the path integral reduce to classical mechanics via stationary phase?** The classical trajectory is defined by $\delta S=0$ — the point where the action's first-order response to a path deformation vanishes. Taylor-expanding the action around any path, that linear term is what makes neighboring paths' phases diverge rapidly (fast phase change → scattered arrows → cancellation) almost everywhere; only at the stationary point does the leading correction become quadratic, so nearby paths' phases barely move and their arrows stay aligned (constructive interference concentrated near the classical trajectory). The width of that coherent "bundle" of near-classical paths scales as $\sqrt{\hbar/K}$ (a Gaussian integral over the quadratic fluctuation term) and shrinks to zero as $\hbar\to0$ — classical mechanics falls out as the limit where only the exactly-stationary path survives, not as a separately assumed principle. This is the general method of stationary phase, and the same mathematics (with the same "expand around a critical point, do the Gaussian fluctuation integral" structure) underlies Fermat's principle in optics, the WKB approximation, saddle-point/instanton methods in QFT, and Laplace's method / Laplace approximations in statistics — an optimization-landscape analogy also carries over directly: a stationary point of the action behaves like a zero-gradient critical point of a loss surface, where nearby parameter values barely change the loss.

**Q3 — How do weak-value/pointer measurements extract information without collapsing the wavefunction?** In the standard (von Neumann) pointer model, a strong system-pointer coupling shifts the pointer by an amount large enough to resolve which eigenvalue caused it — and that resolvability *is* the collapse. Weak coupling instead shifts the pointer by an amount smaller than the pointer's own intrinsic uncertainty, so no single trial can resolve which value produced it; because the induced pointer states stay nearly indistinguishable from each other, the interaction can't collapse the system's coherence either — weak information gain and weak disturbance are the same fact viewed two ways. Averaging the (statistically small but systematic) pointer shift over many trials, conditioned on postselecting a specific final state, recovers the complex **weak value** $A_w=\langle f|\hat A|i\rangle/\langle f|i\rangle$ — read out as real/imaginary parts via the pointer's position/momentum shift. This paper uses the photon's own polarization as the pointer: a Hadamard-like polarization preparation, a weak position-dependent polarization rotation during propagation, postselection on the final position bin, then $\langle\hat\sigma_x\rangle,\langle\hat\sigma_y\rangle$ readout gives the real/imaginary parts of the propagator directly (Eq. 8). The disturbance-vs-precision tradeoff is fundamental (Heisenberg-type), not an engineering limitation; the paper's four hardware upgrades push the achievable precision at fixed weak-coupling strength rather than escaping the tradeoff — necessary because up to five weakly-measured propagators get multiplied per path, so any residual disturbance/noise compounds multiplicatively along the chain.

**Q4 — What distinguishes "semiclassical" from "far-from-classical"?** The governing dial is the ratio $S/\hbar$ for the paths involved, not object size. Semiclassical ($S/\hbar\gg1$): one trajectory's stationary-phase bundle dominates and is dressed with a thin, systematically computable halo of quantum corrections (the WKB expansion in powers of $\hbar$); tunneling extends the same machinery to a complex stationary point rather than abandoning it. Far-from-classical ($S/\hbar\sim1$): the bundle width is comparable to or larger than the whole range of paths being summed, no single trajectory dominates, and there's no small parameter left to expand in — the full coherent sum is required, exactly the double-slit-style regime this paper's 17-position/5-time-step grid was deliberately built into (hence the flat mean-probability distributions across path length and action in Fig. 4, rather than a distribution sharply peaked near a "classical-looking" path). Contrast case: a macroscopic trajectory sits at roughly $S/\hbar\sim10^{34}$, deep enough into the semiclassical limit that even the leading quantum correction is unobservable.
