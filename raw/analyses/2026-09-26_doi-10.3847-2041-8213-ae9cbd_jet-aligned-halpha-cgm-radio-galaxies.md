## 1. Radio Jets Light Up Pencil Beams of Halo Gas

**Hook:** Averaged over all directions, the halos around radio galaxies look completely ordinary. Look only along the jet axis, though, and the cool gas glows in Hα roughly a hundred times brighter than a normal galaxy halo. Meanwhile the *amount* of cool gas, traced by Mg II absorption, stays exactly the same.

---

## 2. Big-Picture Context

The circumgalactic medium (CGM) is the diffuse gas reservoir around a galaxy, extending out to a few hundred kiloparsecs. It feeds star formation and holds whatever outflows have ejected. Massive "red-and-dead" galaxies are a longstanding puzzle: they keep a lot of cool (~10⁴ K) gas in their halos but stop forming stars for billions of years. That requires a heating or disruption source that runs continuously. The leading candidate is **radio-mode AGN feedback**, in which relativistic jets from the central black hole deposit mechanical energy into the surrounding gas.

The direct evidence for jet–gas coupling so far comes mostly from galaxy clusters. There, X-ray cavities and shocks show jets pushing on the hot (≳10⁶ K) atmosphere. At high redshift (z > 2), integral-field spectroscopy of powerful radio galaxies shows turbulent ionized gas lined up with the radio lobes: the classic "alignment effect" and giant Lyα halos. What has been missing is evidence for jet coupling to the *cool* 10⁴ K CGM around ordinary low-redshift radio galaxies. The emission from that gas is far too faint to image directly in individual systems.

Absorption studies had already come back empty. Y.-L. Chang et al. (2024) stacked Mg II absorption in background-quasar sight lines behind about 30,000 DESI radio galaxies and found **no significant difference** from matched radio-quiet controls. That study binned by impact parameter but not by angle relative to the jet. This Letter's move is to keep the geometry: for each of 324 radio-galaxy/background-quasar pairs, the authors record where the sight line falls relative to the projected jet axis, then stack faint Hα emission in the quasar spectra by angle and by position along the jet.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is an observational statistical-detection Letter. It uses spectral stacking to find a faint, anisotropic emission signal that no single sight line could show. At stake is direct evidence that radio jets couple to the cool CGM of typical low-z radio galaxies, and a reconciliation with earlier absorption-line nulls.

**Prior Belief Check:** The *direction* of the result matches expectations. Jet-aligned ionized gas is well established at z > 2, and jet-cocoon simulations (Wagner & Bicknell; Mukherjee et al.) predict preferential compression and shock ionization along the jet path. What is new is the demonstration at low redshift (z ≲ 0.5 for Hα, see §6), at CGM scales of ~20–800 kpc, in sight lines selected independently of the gas. The *amplitude* is surprising: 1–2 orders of magnitude above the normal-galaxy Hα baseline, in a population whose isotropic Mg II properties are indistinguishable from controls. The result complicates the simple reading of Chang et al. (2024) ("jets don't affect the cool CGM") rather than contradicting its data. Both can be true if the effect is anisotropic and weights dense clumps. Expect specialists to call this a plausible and important result that needs confirmation, not a paradigm shift.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is a single-group result on one sample (DESI DR1 × LoTSS DR2), with no independent confirmation yet. Real confirmation would be (a) a larger independent stack, for example DESI DR2 or a different radio catalog, recovering the angular dependence, including a median stack; and/or (b) direct integral-field imaging (MUSE, KCWI) of jet-aligned Hα in a nearby radio galaxy at the surface brightness implied here. Those two checks matter because a stacked mean from a few dozen sight lines is vulnerable to a handful of outliers or a subtle selection effect, and only an independent sample or direct imaging rules that out.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Hα emission and Case B recombination.** You know the Balmer series cold: Hα at 6562.8 Å is the n = 3 → 2 transition. In photoionized or shock-heated gas at ~10⁴ K, protons recapture electrons, and the cascade produces Hα at a known rate per recombination. "Case B" is the standard bookkeeping assumption that the gas is thick to Lyman-series photons, so they are reabsorbed until they degrade into Balmer photons plus Lyα. The key scaling is that the recombination rate per unit volume goes as (electron density) × (proton density), about n_e². Double the density of a clump at fixed mass and its Hα output per unit mass doubles. Compress the same gas into a smaller volume and it gets brighter even though nothing was added.

> **Analogy:** Hα emissivity behaves like a two-party transaction rate: every event needs an electron and a proton to meet, so the event rate scales as the product of the two populations, like pairwise lock contention scaling with the square of thread density.
>
> **Breaks when:** you ask what powers the emission. Contention costs throughput. Recombination *releases* energy that must have been put in earlier by ionization (photons, shocks, or mixing). Hα brightness is an energy-throughput meter, so a bright clump means something is re-ionizing it continuously. The n_e² scaling says where emission concentrates, not what drives it.

**Quasar-sight-line stacking.** The CGM is too faint to image around individual galaxies at these distances. Instead, take a bright background quasar whose line of sight passes a foreground galaxy at projected distance R⊥ (the "impact parameter"). Its spectrum picks up faint foreground Hα emission (from gas inside the spectrograph fiber's footprint) and foreground absorption (Mg II). Shift each spectrum to the foreground galaxy's rest frame, subtract the quasar continuum, and average hundreds of spectra. The foreground line adds coherently at a fixed rest wavelength, while unrelated quasar features land at random wavelengths and average down. The DESI fiber is ~1.5″ across, which at these redshifts is ~5–10 kpc. Each sight line is therefore a *pencil-beam sample* of the halo, not a map.

> **Analogy:** Sampling profiling across a fleet. Any one sample is too noisy to show a 1% hot function, but aggregate a million samples keyed to the right symbol and it pops out. Aligning to the foreground rest frame is the symbol-resolution step. Without it, samples from the same function scatter across addresses and never add up.
>
> **Breaks when:** you forget that each sample here is a *different* halo at a *different* spot. Profiling aggregates repeated draws from one binary. Stacking aggregates one draw each from many nonidentical systems. The stack measures a population mean at a given geometry, not any individual halo's structure. A mean can be driven by a few extreme members, which is exactly the caveat that matters in §4.

**Mg II absorption and covering fraction.** The Mg II λλ2796, 2803 doublet absorbs in cool (~10⁴ K), metal-enriched gas. It is essentially a **column** tracer: it responds to how much cool gas lies along the line of sight (roughly linear in density × path length), not to how dense it is squared. The **covering fraction** f_c is the fraction of sight lines with an absorber above a strength threshold (here rest equivalent width ≥ 0.3 Å at ≥3σ). The equivalent width is the integrated absorption depth expressed as a width in Å. Low f_c with strong individual absorbers means a clumpy gas distribution: sight lines either hit a cloud or they don't.

**Radio galaxies, jets, and lobes (LoTSS).** LOFAR's LoTSS survey at 144 MHz maps synchrotron emission from relativistic jets and the lobes they inflate. The sample here consists of classic double-lobed sources (FR I/FR II-like), with end-to-end size D_radio of ~20 kpc to 3 Mpc and a measured position angle PA_radio. The jet drills out from the host galaxy. At the terminus it inflates a lobe, and around it an overpressured **cocoon** pushes on the surrounding gas. The paper's angle θ is the projected offset between the quasar sight line's direction (as seen from the galaxy) and the jet axis. θ < 20° means "near the jet," and θ near 90° means "perpendicular to it."

**The central interpretive distinction: amount vs. activity.** This one needs the split format, because the vehicle vocabulary (occupancy, heat) overlaps easily with the concept vocabulary (covering, brightness).

> **Concept:** Two different properties of the same cool-gas population are measured here. (1) *Presence*: what fraction of sight lines intersect cool gas, and how much column each has, from Mg II covering fraction, equivalent width, and column density. This responds to gas amount roughly linearly. (2) *Emissivity*: how much Hα the gas along the sight line radiates. This scales as density squared times an ionization/excitation factor, so a small subset of compressed or re-ionized clouds can dominate it. The paper finds presence is the same with or without jet alignment (within errors), while emissivity along the jet is enhanced ~100×. The paper does *not* determine the excitation mechanism (photoionization by the AGN, shocks, or turbulent mixing are all left open).
>
> **Vehicle:** A cache monitored by two counters. A residency counter reports which fraction of tracked lines are present in the cache. A heat counter reports access traffic per line, and traffic concentrates heavily on a few hot lines. A workload change can leave residency unchanged while making a handful of already-resident lines extremely hot.
>
> **Analogy:** Mg II covering fraction ↔ residency counter. Hα surface brightness ↔ heat counter. The jet ↔ the workload change that doesn't bring new lines in but hammers some existing ones. The n_e² scaling ↔ the fact that heat can be dominated by a few lines, so a large heat increase is compatible with no measurable residency change.
>
> **Breaks when:** you ask *what* made the lines hot. The cache vehicle has one mechanism (access traffic). The physics has at least three candidates the paper cannot separate: compression (raising n_e), extra ionizing photons from the AGN along the jet cone, and shock or mixing-layer excitation. The vehicle also has no slot for the possibility that some of the hot gas is *new* (entrained out of the host along the jet) but too small in total column to move the Mg II counter. The paper raises this possibility and disfavors it only weakly, via Mg II kinematics.

> **Central analogy for this paper:** Same cache residency, a few lines running hot

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### 4.1 Building the sample

The authors cross-match DESI DR1 spectroscopic quasars with LoTSS DR2 radio galaxies that have spectroscopic redshifts. They keep:

- **Double-lobed sources:** 4.3 ≤ log(D_radio/pc) ≤ 6.5 and 23 ≤ log(L₁₄₄/W Hz⁻¹) ≤ 29.5, with resolved major and minor axes, so the position angle is real. That leaves 90,228 radio AGN with measured jet axes.
- **Clean foreground–background pairs:** z_QSO > z_RG + 0.08, so the quasar isn't physically associated, and z_QSO − z_RG < 2.5, so the quasar's own broad UV lines (Lyα, C IV, …) can't land on the foreground line windows.
- **Geometry:** R⊥ > R₅₀ excludes the host's own ISM (R₅₀ is the optical half-light radius), and R⊥ < 0.8 D_radio = 1.6 R_radio keeps the sight line within the radio structure's reach.

That leaves **324 pairs**: 0.05 < z_RG < 1.64, R⊥ ≈ 20–800 kpc. The jet-angle offset is the projected position-angle difference folded to 0–90°. θ < 20° contains **74 sight lines**. Sanity check: for sight lines randomly oriented relative to jets, the expected count in 0–20° of a 0–90° range is 324 × 20/90 ≈ 72. The observed 74 is consistent, so there is no hint that the selection itself favors jet-aligned sight lines.

**A coverage fact that matters later.** DESI covers ~3600–9800 Å. Hα lands in that range only for z_RG ≲ 0.49, or ≲ 0.47 once the ±150 Å extraction window must fit, and Mg II only for z_RG ≳ 0.29. The stacking code drops any spectrum without valid pixels in the window, so **the Hα stacks are built only from the z ≲ 0.47 part of the sample**, even though the full catalog runs to z = 1.64. The paper states the per-line rejection but never quotes how many of the 74 jet-aligned sight lines actually enter the Hα stack.

### 4.2 Stacking

For each pair, they shift the quasar spectrum to the radio galaxy's rest frame and cut a ±150 Å window around Hα. They mask the quasar's own strong lines, fit and subtract a second-order polynomial continuum outside ±50 Å, interpolate onto a common grid, and take an inverse-variance-weighted mean, requiring ≥5 contributing spectra per pixel. The Hα flux is integrated over |Δv| < 500 km/s, which is ±10.9 Å, to stay clear of [N II] λ6583 at +941 km/s. Noise baselines come from identical "blank" stacks centered at 6800, 5500 and 4000 Å, where no foreground line exists.

**Worked-equation box: the stacked mean**

$$\langle f_\lambda \rangle = \frac{\sum_i w_i(\lambda)\, f_{\mathrm{cs},i}(\lambda)}{\sum_i w_i(\lambda)}, \qquad w_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2(\lambda)}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$f_{\mathrm{cs},i}(\lambda)$ : continuum-subtracted flux of sight line *i* at rest wavelength λ (10⁻¹⁷ erg cm⁻² s⁻¹ Å⁻¹)
$\sigma_i(\lambda)$ : that pixel's noise
$w_i$ : its weight, 1/variance

**What this actually means:** Each spectrum votes with weight proportional to how clean it is. It is the standard minimum-variance estimator. A performance engineer would recognize it as weighting benchmark runs by 1/variance before averaging. The important property for §6 is that it is still a **mean**, not a median, so a few very bright sight lines can carry it.

### 4.3 The angular result

- **θ < 20°:** a clear Hα excess at the host's systemic velocity, F_Hα = 1.19 × 10⁻¹⁷ erg cm⁻² s⁻¹, **>5σ** above the blank-stack baseline. Leave-one-out jackknife moves the flux by only 4–7% (significance stays >4.7σ). Randomized foreground redshifts and blank-region stacks don't reproduce it.
- **20° < θ < 40°** and **θ > 40°:** no comparable feature.
- **All 324 sight lines, azimuthally averaged:** <2σ, a non-detection.

The profile is skewed red and very broad (>2000 km/s total span). The authors attribute the red wing partly to blended [N II] λ6583 and the breadth to a mix of redshift scatter, cloud-motion averaging, and possible jet-driven kinematics, so they deliberately integrate only the central ±500 km/s.

**Worked-equation box: is the azimuthal null independent evidence?** (analyst check, illustrative)

Suppose *only* the 74 jet-aligned sight lines carry signal (1.19 in units of 10⁻¹⁷) and the other 250 carry zero, all with equal weights.

- Diluted mean over all 324 = (74/324) × 1.19 ≈ **0.27**
- The jet-aligned stack's noise ≈ 1.19/5 ≈ 0.24 (since it is a ~5σ detection). Averaging 324 instead of 74 spectra shrinks noise by √(74/324): 0.24 × 0.478 ≈ **0.11**
- Expected significance of the full stack ≈ 0.27/0.11 ≈ **2.4σ**

**What this actually means:** Pure dilution predicts roughly a 2σ-ish full-sample signal, and the paper observes <2σ. The azimuthal null is therefore *what you'd expect* from the jet-bin detection alone. It confirms consistency but adds little independent evidence of anisotropy. (Real weights and Hα-coverage cuts will change these numbers somewhat, so treat the 2.4σ as order-of-magnitude.) The anisotropy evidence really rests on the **two off-axis bins (20–40°, >40°) staying empty** while the on-axis bin lights up. It is the same logic as a fleet-wide regression average: if one config class of servers has a 5% regression, the fleet mean shows ~1% and falls in the noise, which tells you nothing new. What tells you it's config-specific is that the *other* config classes are clean.

### 4.4 The radial result

Within θ < 20°, the authors bin by R⊥/R_radio (sight-line distance along the jet in units of the jet half-length):

- **R⊥/R_radio < 0.5:** strongest, F = 3.46 × 10⁻¹⁷, **>7σ**. This is near the host but outside R₅₀, where the jet first breaks out into the halo.
- **0.5–1.0:** weak or no detection.
- **>1.0:** rises again, F = 1.31 × 10⁻¹⁷, near the lobe/cocoon region. No significance is quoted.

The story is two coupling zones: the ISM–CGM breakout zone and the lobe–CGM interface.

### 4.5 Comparison to normal halos (Fig. 3)

The authors convert to cosmological-dimming-corrected surface brightness (a factor of (1+z)⁴) and plot against stellar mass alongside H. Zhang et al. (2016, 2020) stacked Hα+[N II] around normal SDSS galaxies. The azimuthal radio-galaxy upper limit sits inside the normal-galaxy band. The jet-aligned inner and outer points sit **1–2 orders of magnitude above** it at similar stellar mass (log M* ≈ 11). This is the source of the abstract's "~100×."

### 4.6 Physical scale and energetics

The emitting luminosity uses the standard inverse-square law:

$$L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha} = 4\pi D_L^2\, F_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}$ : Hα luminosity (erg/s)
$D_L$ : luminosity distance to the radio galaxy
$F_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}$ : measured stacked flux (erg cm⁻² s⁻¹)

**What this actually means:** Measured flux times the area of a sphere at the source's distance gives the power emitted *into the fiber's footprint*. It is the luminosity of one pencil beam through the halo, not of the whole halo.

The ionized mass follows from Case B:

$$M_{\mathrm{ion}} \simeq \frac{m_p\, L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}}{n_e\, \alpha^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}\, h\nu_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}} \approx 3.2\times10^{9}\left(\frac{L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}}{10^{40}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}}\right)\left(\frac{10^{-2}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}}{n_e}\right) M_\odot$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$m_p$ : proton mass
$n_e$ : electron density (cm⁻³)
$\alpha^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}$ : effective Case B Hα recombination coefficient, 1.17 × 10⁻¹³ cm³ s⁻¹ at 10⁴ K
$h\nu_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}$ : energy of one Hα photon, 3.03 × 10⁻¹² erg

**What this actually means:** Each Hα photon marks one recombination. Recombinations per second divided by recombinations per proton per second (n_e × α) gives the number of protons, and multiplying by m_p gives mass. The key feature is **M ∝ 1/n_e**: the same luminosity from denser gas means *less* mass. (Hand check: the H-only coefficient works out to 2.37 × 10⁹ M☉. The paper's 3.2 × 10⁹ equals that × 1.36, the standard helium correction, which the paper applies without stating it. Consistent.)

The density n_e ≈ 10⁻² cm⁻³ comes from pressure balance with a 10⁶ K, 10⁻⁴ cm⁻³ hot halo (P/k ≈ 10² K cm⁻³). At ⟨z_RG⟩ = 0.66 the paper gets L_Hα ≈ 2.2 × 10⁴⁰ erg/s and M_ion ≈ 7 × 10⁹ M☉. Against a mean jet power ⟨log P_jet⟩ ≈ 43.5, the ratio L_Hα/P_jet ≈ 6 × 10⁻⁴: only ~0.06% of jet power needs to come out as Hα. §6 revisits these numbers.

### 4.7 The Mg II control

In the same sight lines, Mg II covering fractions are f_c = 21.6 (+5.2/−4.6)% for θ < 20° versus 16.0 (+2.6/−2.3)% for θ > 20°, which back out to 16/74 and 40/250 detections. A two-proportion test gives p = 0.24. Equivalent widths, column densities and line widths overlap completely between the two groups (Fig. 4). Individual absorbers extend past ~100 kpc and above the LRG profiles of Lan & Mo (2018), but stacked signals are weak (Fig. 5): an extended, clumpy, low-covering-fraction phase. The inference is that jets brighten, ionize, or compress a subset of pre-existing clouds rather than creating or piling up cool gas. The lack of a Mg II velocity-width or velocity-offset change argues against large-scale entrainment being dominant.

### 4.8 SIMBA illustration (Appendix A.3)

One jet-mode AGN galaxy from the SIMBA cosmological simulation (log M* = 10.6, z = 0) gets a mock Hα map. Emissivity comes from CLOUDY with the UV background only (no AGN or shock excitation). Azimuthal bins give surface brightness of 2.5, 2.2 and 1.6 × 10⁻²⁰ erg s⁻¹ cm⁻² arcsec⁻², declining away from the jet. The per-pixel distributions are broad and skewed, with means above medians. The absolute level is ~3 orders of magnitude below the observations. The authors read this as "UV background alone is far too feeble, so the jet must boost excitation."

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "radio-galaxy halos are ~100× brighter in Hα." **The paper actually says** only *jet-aligned pencil beams* are; the azimuthally averaged halo is consistent with a normal quiescent halo. The 100× is a directional, local statement, not a halo-integrated one.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes ">5σ, jackknife-stable" means the signal is spread across the jet-aligned sight lines. **The paper actually** reports an inverse-variance-weighted *mean*. Leave-one-out jackknife guards against *one* dominant spectrum, but not against three or four. The authors' own SIMBA appendix shows the binned mean driven by a small number of bright pixels, and they invoke exactly that clumpiness to explain their result. No median stack or leave-k-out test is reported.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes L_Hα ≈ 2.2 × 10⁴⁰ erg/s and M_ion ≈ 7 × 10⁹ M☉ describe the jet-affected halo gas. **The paper actually** computes a per-fiber quantity (one ~10 kpc pencil beam) at ⟨z_RG⟩ = 0.66, a redshift that appears to lie outside the range where DESI can see Hα at all (see §6, L1–L2).

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "no Mg II difference" means jets leave the cool-gas budget untouched. **The paper actually** shows no *statistically significant* difference. The analyst power check (§6, L7) says the test would catch a doubling of f_c most of the time but cannot rule out a ~30–40% increase. The observed ratio (21.6 vs 16.0%) points in the "more gas" direction.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

1. **Keeping the geometry in a stacking experiment.** This is new to the field. CGM stacking around radio galaxies (Chang et al. 2024) collapsed every sight line to R⊥ alone. Tagging each background quasar with its angle to the projected jet, then splitting the stack, turns a null into a >5σ detection. The trick is cheap, reusable, and obvious in hindsight, which is usually a sign of a good idea. It is the fleet-debugging move of not averaging over the attribute that matters.
2. **An emission tracer plus a column tracer in the same sight lines.** Hα scales as n_e², while Mg II tracks column. Measuring both along identical pencil beams separates "more gas" from "more active gas" without needing any model. The null on one plus the detection on the other is what gives the paper its physical interpretation. The combination of these two tracers for this purpose is new to the field. The individual techniques are not.

It is new to the reader, though not to the field, that jet-aligned ionized gas is a decades-old high-z phenomenon (the "alignment effect," McCarthy 1993; Nesvadba et al.). The contribution is pushing it to low z, large radii, and an unbiased sight-line sample.

**Predictive Content Check**

1. **Falsifiable handle:** The measurement makes a concrete, re-testable claim. An independent stack of background-quasar sight lines within 20° of LoTSS-like jet axes, at R⊥ ≈ 20–800 kpc and z ≲ 0.47, should show Hα at roughly 1 × 10⁻¹⁷ erg cm⁻² s⁻¹ per DESI fiber, with nothing at θ > 20°, and a double-peaked radial profile (strong below 0.5 R_radio, a dip, a rise beyond R_radio). DESI DR2/DR3 roughly multiplies the pair count and can test this directly. A second implied prediction, supported by the paper's clumpiness interpretation though not stated by it, is that a **median** stack should be far weaker than the mean. If the median stack is comparably strong, the gas is smoother than claimed. If the mean collapses when the top few sight lines are dropped, the ">5σ population result" becomes "a few jet-intersected clumps," which is still interesting but different. The *interpretation* (compression vs. AGN photoionization vs. shocks) is not tested. The paper makes no line-ratio prediction (e.g., [N II]/Hα, [O III]/Hβ) that would discriminate among mechanisms, even though those lines are in the same spectra.
2. **Formalism load:** Not triggered. The paper has little formal machinery, and what it has (stacking, Case B conversion) is doing ordinary, necessary work.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**L1. The luminosity/mass redshift appears inconsistent with the Hα coverage.** L_Hα = 2.2 × 10⁴⁰ erg/s is computed at ⟨z_RG⟩ = 0.66 (I reproduce 2.24 × 10⁴⁰ exactly at that redshift). DESI only reaches Hα for z_RG ≲ 0.47, however, and the paper's own Mg II/Hα overlap subsample has median z = 0.38. At z = 0.38 the same flux gives L ≈ 6.0 × 10³⁹ erg/s, which is **~3.8× lower**. M_ion drops correspondingly to ~1.9 × 10⁹ M☉, and L/P_jet to ~1.6 × 10⁻⁴. The qualitative argument (a small fraction of jet power suffices) is unchanged and gets stronger, but the headline numbers look inflated by roughly 4×, unless ⟨z⟩ = 0.66 refers to a subset I've misidentified. **(C) Speculative** — this rests on my reading of the coverage limits and the unreported z-distribution of the actual Hα contributors; the authors may have a reason I can't see from the text. **(analyst inference)**

**L2. The luminosity and mass are per-fiber, pencil-beam quantities, and the mass is density-assumption-bound.** A 1.5″ fiber at z = 0.38 spans ~7.8 kpc. Putting even 1.9 × 10⁹ M☉ at n_e = 10⁻² cm⁻³ inside that cylinder needs a **~110 kpc column at 100% volume filling**, and the paper's own interpretation is *low* filling (clumpy, compressed clouds). If the emitting clumps are compressed, n_e is higher and M ∝ 1/n_e falls. The pressure-balance density is effectively a *floor* for jet-compressed gas, so M_ion is closer to an upper bound. In the other direction, comparing a single-fiber L_Hα to the *total* jet power understates the halo-wide coupling fraction. The two effects pull opposite ways and neither is quantified. **(C) Speculative** — the arithmetic is simple, but the paper doesn't frame the quantities this way and reasonable readers could weigh the effects differently. **(analyst inference)**

**L3. The detection may rest on a few sight lines.** The mean-weighted stack, leave-one-out jackknife only, no median stack, no reported N per radial bin, and the authors' own SIMBA demonstration that means are pixel-outlier-driven all leave open how many sight lines carry the signal. The inner radial bin (>7σ from what must be a fraction of the 74) is the sharpest version of this question. **(B) Contested** — the paper's jackknife and randomized-redshift tests are real robustness checks, and a clumpy-gas reading actually *predicts* few-dominator statistics, so whether this is a weakness or the expected physics depends on the reader. **(paper §3.1, §A.3 + analyst inference)**

**L4. The excitation mechanism is undetermined.** AGN photoionization along the jet/ionization cone, shocks, turbulent mixing layers, and collisional excitation all remain open. Line ratios available in the same DESI spectra ([N II], [O III], Hβ, [S II]) could discriminate, but the stack doesn't have the S/N to use them, or they weren't attempted. The AGN ionization-cone alternative deserves particular attention: ionization cones and radio jets are often co-aligned, and photoionization would brighten Hα along the jet *without* any mechanical coupling. That would undercut the paper's "mechanical feedback" framing specifically. **(A) Consensus** — the paper explicitly states its measurements cannot distinguish these channels. **(paper §4.1)**

**L5. The radial "two-zone" structure is thinly supported.** It rests on three bins within the 74-sight-line jet subsample. The outer bin's significance and all per-bin counts are unquoted, and the middle bin is a non-detection. The structure is physically plausible but statistically fragile as presented. **(C) Speculative** — this is my reading of what's reported; the underlying numbers may support it more firmly. **(paper §3.2 + analyst inference)**

**L6. The baseline comparison to normal halos is not like-for-like.** Zhang et al. bin by r_p/R₁₈₀ and include Hα+[N II]. This paper bins by R⊥/R_radio and integrates Hα only inside ±500 km/s. The paper says the samples are "not exactly identical." A secondary puzzle: Fig. 3's y-axis is labeled in erg cm⁻² s⁻¹ **Å⁻¹** arcsec⁻², a flux *density* per area. That is odd for an integrated line surface brightness, and I couldn't reconcile the plotted jet-aligned values (~3 × 10⁻¹⁸) with the quoted fiber fluxes divided by the fiber area (≳10⁻¹⁷ before the dimming correction) unless a per-Å normalization over the integration window was applied. The "1–2 orders of magnitude" is probably robust in direction, but the exact factor inherits these conversions. **(B) Contested** — the non-identical-sample caveat is the paper's own; the unit-conversion concern is mine and may just reflect a convention I'm not recovering. **(paper §4.2 + analyst inference)**

**L7. The Mg II null has limited power, and its denominators look odd.** A two-proportion test at these counts has ~80% power to detect a *doubling* of f_c (32% vs 16%). The 95% interval on the jet-aligned f_c (≈14–32%) allows a ~30–40% enhancement. The quoted fractions (16/74, 40/250) also use the *full* angular-bin counts, even though Mg II is observable only for z_RG ≳ 0.29 (the paper's stated Mg II subsample is 0.30–0.49). If some pairs lack coverage, the absolute f_c values are understated. The comparison between bins survives as long as coverage is independent of θ, which it should be. **(C) Speculative** — the power calculation is standard, but the denominator issue is my inference from the numbers and could reflect a detail the text omits. **(analyst inference)**

**L8. The SIMBA comparison is qualitative only.** It uses one galaxy, at z = 0, with emissivity from the UV background alone and an absolute amplitude ~10³× below the data. It shows that jet-mode simulations produce *angularly structured* cool-gas density, but it cannot test the excitation or amplitude claims, and its anisotropy (a factor ~1.6 between bins) is far milder than observed. **(A) Consensus** — the paper presents it explicitly as an illustrative example, not a model comparison. **(paper §A.3)**

**L9. Projection effects.** θ is a projected angle and R⊥ a projected distance. Small θ selects both truly jet-aligned gas and gas in front of or behind the jet that happens to project onto it. Jet inclination to the line of sight also foreshortens D_radio and R_radio. This dilutes a real effect rather than faking one, but it means the intrinsic enhancement and the radial profile shape are uncertain. **(A) Consensus** — standard for any projected-geometry CGM study. **(broader literature)**

**Open questions for the next 12–24 months:** a DESI DR2-scale replication with median and leave-k-out stacks; line-ratio stacks to separate photoionization from shocks; a split by radio morphology (FR I vs FR II), radio luminosity, and source age (spectral index) to see whether coupling scales with jet power; IFU follow-up of the brightest individual jet-aligned sight lines; and forward-modeled SIMBA/IllustrisTNG/FLAMINGO sight lines with AGN and shock excitation included, which the authors themselves name.

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

**Summary.** The authors pair 324 LOFAR-detected, double-lobed radio galaxies with background DESI quasars whose sight lines pass through the radio galaxies' halos at 20–800 kpc. For each pair they record the angle between the sight line and the radio jet. They stack the quasar spectra in the radio galaxy's rest frame to look for faint Hα emission from cool (~10,000 K) ionized halo gas, and they look for Mg II absorption in the same sight lines.

Sight lines within 20° of the jet show a clear Hα signal, about 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁷ erg per square centimeter per second per fiber, at more than five standard deviations above the noise. Sight lines farther from the jet show nothing, and the all-angle average shows nothing. Along the jet, the emission is strongest just outside the host galaxy and again near the radio lobes, with a dip in between. Corrected for cosmological dimming, the jet-aligned surface brightness is 10–100 times higher than stacked Hα around normal galaxies of similar mass. Meanwhile, Mg II absorption shows no significant difference with angle: a similar fraction of sight lines hit cool gas, with similar absorption strength, column and velocity width.

The authors conclude that jets do not create a large new reservoir of cool gas. Instead they brighten a subset of pre-existing clumpy clouds, by compressing, ionizing or shocking them, along their path. The brightness scales with the square of the density, so a few compressed clouds can dominate the emission while barely changing absorption statistics. A Case B conversion yields billions of solar masses of ionized gas at the pressure-balance density. Only a tiny fraction (under 0.1%) of the jet's mechanical power needs to come out as Hα. A single simulated jet-mode galaxy from SIMBA shows the same qualitative preference for gas near the jet axis, dominated by a few bright clumps.

**Explanation of the framing.** I centered the summary on the *contrast* between the two tracers rather than on the Hα detection alone, because the contrast is what makes the result physically interpretable. An Hα excess by itself could mean "more gas" or "brighter gas." The flat Mg II result is what points to "brighter gas." I also separated three layers of confidence:

1. The angular detection is robustly tested within the paper's own framework (jackknife, randomized redshifts, blank stacks).
2. The radial structure and normal-halo comparison are plausible but more thinly supported.
3. The derived luminosity, mass and energetics carry the largest quantitative caveats, including what looks like a redshift mismatch that inflates them by roughly 4× while leaving the qualitative argument intact.

The honest takeaway: this is strong evidence that jets couple *directionally* to cool halo gas at low redshift. It is not yet a measurement of *how much* gas or energy is involved, or *by what mechanism*.

**Genuine Uncertainty Disclosure:**
> **Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the L1 redshift-mismatch finding. It rests on DESI's wavelength limit implying no Hα contributors above z ≈ 0.47, set against the paper's stated ⟨z_RG⟩ = 0.66. I'm confident in the arithmetic (I reproduce their 2.2 × 10⁴⁰ exactly at z = 0.66), but the paper never reports the redshift distribution of the sight lines that actually enter the Hα stack, so the ⟨z⟩ could refer to a subset or weighting I haven't identified. The Fig. 3 per-Å unit reconciliation (L6) is a second soft spot. In both cases the authors (or the arXiv v1 vs. published-text comparison) should be the arbiter, not my inference.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **Averaging erased the effect.** Radio-galaxy halos look normal in Hα when you average over all directions, but light up about 100× along the jet axis. The anisotropy is the whole signal.
2. **Jets make gas shine without making more of it.** Mg II absorption, which counts how much cool gas is there, shows no jet dependence. Hα, which scales with density squared, lights up. Jets appear to compress or ionize a few pre-existing clouds rather than add new ones.
3. **The direction is solid, the amounts are soft.** The angular detection survives the paper's robustness tests. The quoted luminosity, gas mass and energy budget depend on a pressure-balance density assumption and appear to use a redshift above the range where Hα is observable, so treat them as order-of-magnitude until someone checks.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Giant radio jets from supermassive black holes are thought to keep massive galaxies from forming new stars by heating or stirring the gas around them. But evidence that jets affect the cool, 10,000-degree gas in galaxy halos, as opposed to the million-degree gas in galaxy clusters, has been missing at low redshift. A previous survey of absorbing gas around radio galaxies found nothing unusual.

Roy and colleagues tried a sharper test. They paired 324 radio galaxies mapped by the LOFAR radio telescope with background quasars observed by the DESI spectroscopic survey, and noted whether each quasar's line of sight passed near the radio jet or off to the side. They then averaged the quasar spectra to find faint hydrogen-alpha glow, the red emission of ionized hydrogen, coming from the foreground halo gas.

Sight lines within 20 degrees of the jet show a clear hydrogen-alpha signal, detected at more than five standard deviations and roughly a hundred times brighter than around ordinary galaxies. Sight lines away from the jet show nothing, and averaging all directions together also shows nothing. The glow is strongest just outside the galaxy and again near the radio lobes where the jets end. Yet magnesium absorption in the same sight lines, which tracks how much cool gas is present, looks the same in every direction.

The interpretation is that jets don't add cool gas. They compress, shock or ionize existing clumps along their path, and because hydrogen-alpha brightness rises with the square of the gas density, a few compressed clouds can shine brilliantly without changing the total. A computer simulation of one jet-powered galaxy shows the same directional pattern.

The directional detection is the robust part. The paper's estimates of gas mass and energy depend on an assumed density, and the redshift used to compute them appears higher than the range where DESI can actually see hydrogen-alpha, which would inflate them about fourfold. Larger samples from later DESI releases can confirm the pattern and test what heats the gas.

## Appendix — Plain-Language Walkthrough (2026-09-28)

### The question the paper is chasing

Big elliptical galaxies are "retired." They stopped making new stars billions of years ago. The puzzle is that they aren't out of raw material. Around each one sits a huge, thin atmosphere of gas, the **circumgalactic medium (CGM)**, stretching hundreds of thousands of light-years out. Some of that gas is cool enough (about 10,000 degrees, which counts as cool in space) that it should slowly rain inward and form stars. Something keeps stopping it.

The main suspect is the giant black hole at the galaxy's center. When it feeds, it can fire two narrow **jets** of particles moving at nearly the speed of light, in opposite directions. The jets punch out of the galaxy and end in two huge puffy clouds called **lobes**, which radio telescopes can see. The idea is that the jets stir and heat the gas around the galaxy. There is good evidence of this for the very hot gas in galaxy clusters. Nobody had shown whether jets touch the *cool* gas around ordinary, fairly nearby galaxies.

### Why this is hard

The cool halo gas is extremely faint, far too faint to photograph directly around a galaxy billions of light-years away. An earlier study that searched for it around radio galaxies, using the "shadow" method described below, found nothing different from normal galaxies.

### Trick 1: backlighting with quasars

A **quasar** is a distant, extremely bright galaxy core. Sometimes one sits behind a nearer galaxy on the sky, so its light passes through the nearer galaxy's halo on the way to us. It's like shining a flashlight through fog: you learn about the fog from what happens to the beam.

The halo gas shows up in the quasar's **spectrum** (its light spread into a rainbow) in two ways:

1. **A faint glow.** Energized hydrogen gives off a specific red color called **hydrogen-alpha (Hα)**. It's the same kind of light a neon sign makes, just from hydrogen. If glowing gas is in the patch of sky the telescope is looking at, that red color gets added to the quasar's light.
2. **A shadow.** Magnesium in cool gas absorbs a specific ultraviolet color (**Mg II**), leaving a dark notch in the spectrum. The notch shows that cool gas sits in front of the quasar, and roughly how much.

The shadow counts **how much** cool gas is there. The glow shows **how energized** that gas is. That difference is the heart of the paper.

### Trick 2: stacking

Even with a quasar behind it, the red glow from one halo is buried in noise, so the authors average many spectra together. Each galaxy is at a different distance, so its colors are shifted by a different amount. Before averaging, they shift each spectrum so the galaxy's red line lands at the same spot in every one. Real signal then piles up at that spot. Noise and unrelated quasar features land in different places each time and cancel out. It's the same idea as stacking dozens of noisy astrophotos to reveal a faint nebula, or averaging many benchmark runs to expose a small real speedup.

One consequence: each spectrum comes from a small spot about 5,000–30,000 light-years across, inside a halo roughly a million light-years across. Each quasar is like one core sample drilled through the halo. The stack describes the average halo, not any single galaxy.

### The new idea: sort by direction

The earlier study averaged everything together regardless of direction. This team used radio maps from the LOFAR telescope, which show which way each galaxy's jets point. They sorted their **324 quasar–galaxy pairs** by angle:

- sight lines passing close to the jet direction (within 20°)
- sight lines in between
- sight lines off to the side

If jets only affect gas along their path, averaging all directions dilutes the effect until it disappears. It's like averaging one hot spot on a CPU with the rest of the cool chip.

### What they found

1. **The glow appears only along the jets.** The 74 sight lines within 20° of a jet show a clear red Hα glow. It is strong enough to be very unlikely as a fluke; the paper quotes more than 5 sigma. Sight lines off to the side show nothing. All directions averaged together also show nothing, which explains why the earlier study came up empty.
2. **There are two hot zones along the jet.** The glow is strongest just outside the galaxy, where the jet first bursts into the halo. It is weaker in the middle and rises again near the lobes at the jet's end. Those are the places where you'd expect the jet to push hardest on its surroundings.
3. **The glow is roughly 10–100 times brighter than normal.** The comparison is with the faint glow around ordinary galaxies of similar size, measured in earlier SDSS studies.
4. **The shadow doesn't change with direction.** The magnesium shadow appears in a similar fraction of sight lines: about 1 in 5 along the jet versus 1 in 6 elsewhere, which is not a meaningful difference. Its strength and the gas motions it reveals are also similar everywhere.

### What it means

Put findings 3 and 4 together. There isn't more cool gas along the jet, but the gas there glows far more brightly. **The jet isn't adding gas; it's energizing gas that was already there.**

A useful picture is flying over a region at night. The buildings (the gas) are spread evenly everywhere, but the lights are on only along one highway (the jet). Counting buildings shows no difference. Looking at the lights shows a bright stripe.

How can a jet make gas glow much brighter without adding any? The red glow happens when an electron and a hydrogen nucleus meet and join up. Squeeze a cloud into half the space and its particles meet much more often, so the same gas glows much more. A jet, or the pressure bubble around it, can do this in several ways:

- squeeze clouds
- shock them
- hit them with extra radiation

Any of these would work, and the paper can't tell which one is happening.

The overall picture is a halo full of cool, patchy clouds. The shadow data already suggest patchiness, since most sight lines miss them. The jet plows through and lights up the clouds in its path.

### Energy and mass, roughly

The authors estimate several billion Suns' worth of glowing gas along a typical jet-aligned sight line. They also estimate that making this glow takes less than a tenth of a percent of the jet's energy. Jets have far more than enough power. These numbers are rough; see below.

### A simulation check

They also examined one jet-hosting galaxy in the SIMBA computer simulation of the universe. Gas near the simulated jet direction is also brighter, and a few bright clumps dominate it, the same pattern as the observations. It is much fainter overall, because the simulation leaves out the jet's own energizing effect on the glow. This is an illustration, not a proof.

### How solid is it?

**Solid: the direction effect.** Several checks support it:

- removing any single spectrum barely changes the result
- scrambling the galaxy distances makes the signal vanish
- the same processing on random stretches of spectrum finds nothing

**Less solid:**

- **The mass and energy numbers are rough.** They depend on an assumed cloud density. The authors also used an average distance for the whole sample, but the red glow is only visible in the nearer part of the sample. Using the right distance would lower those numbers by about four times. The conclusion that jets have more than enough energy survives either way.
- **Nobody knows yet how the glow is made.** Squeezing, shocks, or light from the black hole shining along the jet would all brighten the gas. If it's mostly light, then the jet's physical push isn't the key factor.
- **Small sample, one team.** The result rests on 74 sight lines, and the "two hot zones" rest on just three distance bins. No one has repeated it yet. Later DESI data releases contain many more quasars and can test it directly.
- **Everything is flattened onto the sky.** "Along the jet" includes gas in front of or behind the jet. That tends to water down a real effect, not create a fake one.

### Why it matters

This is the first clear evidence that jets from ordinary nearby radio galaxies reach far out into their halos and change the cool gas there. It also explains why earlier searches missed it: the effect lives in a narrow direction and vanishes when you average over all directions. It supports the idea that jets help keep big galaxies retired, not by removing their gas but by keeping part of it stirred up and energized.

### Quick glossary

- **CGM:** the thin gas atmosphere surrounding a galaxy.
- **Hα (hydrogen-alpha):** red light given off by energized hydrogen.
- **Mg II:** magnesium that absorbs a specific ultraviolet color; used to detect cool gas in front of a quasar.
- **Quasar:** a very bright, distant galaxy core, used here as a backlight.
- **Stacking:** averaging many spectra so a faint signal rises out of the noise.
- **Sight line:** the path of a quasar's light through the foreground halo.
- **5 sigma:** statistical shorthand for "very unlikely to be random noise."
