## 1. The Hook

Astronomers have spent 70 years pricing every galaxy in the universe using a single, unverified assumption: that the recipe by which gas clouds turn into stars — how many small stars you get for every big one — is the same everywhere and everywhen. This paper opens that box for the first time using resolved, individual stars inside our own Galaxy, and finds the recipe has been quietly drifting for billions of years.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

### Why this matters

Almost everything astronomers know about galaxies beyond the Milky Way — their stellar mass, their star-formation rate, their contribution to cosmic history — is inferred, not measured. You can only *see* the rare, massive, luminous stars in a distant galaxy; they account for as little as one part in a million of the galaxy's stellar mass. To get from "light we can see" to "mass that's actually there," every analysis pipeline has to assume a stellar initial mass function (IMF) — a mass distribution telling you, for every luminous heavy star you counted, how many invisible faint ones came with it. Change the assumed IMF, and every derived galaxy property changes with it: stellar mass, star-formation rate, inferred history of the universe's star formation, even claims about anomalously massive early galaxies seen by JWST.

The default assumption for decades has been that this recipe (the IMF) is *universal* — the same in the Milky Way, in a starburst galaxy, and in the early universe. That assumption has never been well tested, because testing it directly requires resolving individual faint, low-mass stars — something only possible in the relatively small volume of our own Galaxy that current instruments can resolve star-by-star. Everywhere else, astronomers only ever see the integrated light of many stars blended together, which is not enough to independently pin down both the IMF and everything else at once.

This Letter uses the European Space Agency's Gaia mission — which has precisely measured positions, motions, and brightnesses for well over a billion Milky Way stars — to directly compare the stellar populations of open star clusters (loose, gravitationally bound groups of stars, typically hundreds to a few thousand members, born together from the same cloud and slowly dispersing over time) of different ages. Because Gaia can resolve individual low-mass stars in nearby clusters, this is one of the only places in the universe where the IMF question can currently be settled by direct observation rather than by inference.

### Paper type and stakes

This is a **primarily empirical** paper built on a **pre-existing theoretical framework** (the idea that IMF shape should depend on the sound speed of the parent molecular cloud has existed since the 1970s–80s). The stakes are high but not maximal: this is not a "new law of physics" claim, it's a "a long-assumed simplification in a load-bearing piece of astronomical infrastructure turns out to be wrong, and here's the first direct proof" claim. If it holds up, it doesn't overturn any fundamental physics — it forces a correction factor into essentially every extragalactic mass/star-formation-rate estimate ever made, which is a big practical deal even though it's a small conceptual one.

### Prior belief check

Contrary to how the abstract reads ("this evolution is consistent with... long-standing theoretical and numerical predictions"), this result should **not** land as a bolt from the blue if you've been following this specific research thread — because it mostly wasn't landing on strangers. A large fraction of the citations bolstering the physical model (the sound-speed-dependent IMF, the claim that most unresolved galaxies have "bottom-lighter" IMFs, the claim this matters most for JWST high-redshift masses) are **the same author group's own prior papers** (C. L. Steinhardt et al. 2020, 2022a, 2022b, 2023a, 2023b). Those were indirect, population-synthesis/template-fitting arguments built from unresolved, integrated-light photometry of distant galaxies — exactly the kind of evidence that's historically been distrusted because it's degenerate with other assumptions and prone to overfitting (the paper says as much in its own introduction). What's genuinely new here isn't the *claim* — it's the *evidence class*: this is the same research program's first shot at testing itself against resolved, star-by-star ground truth, inside the one place (the Milky Way) where that's currently possible.

Meanwhile, to the broader field that has defaulted to a universal IMF as standard practice for pipeline convenience (the paper cites Brammer et al. 2008 and Speagle et al. 2014 as examples of that default), this result is a legitimate, non-trivial challenge — it's just not a surprise to the people who wrote it.

### Replication and convergence note

Two different things are bundled together in this paper, and they have different replication statuses:

- **The raw observational pattern** — that a cluster's present-day mass function "break point" shifts to higher mass in older open clusters — is *not* new and *not* single-group. It had already been independently reported by several different teams using different methods (Bastian et al. 2010; De Marchi et al. 2010; Offner et al. 2014; Moraux 2016; Jiang et al. 2024). That part is solid, multiply-replicated ground.
- **The interpretation** — that this shift reflects a genuinely varying IMF rather than just old clusters having dynamically lost more low-mass stars — is new, and rests on a single analytical trick from this one paper (explained in Section 4 below). Those earlier studies, per this paper, generally attributed the same pattern to dynamical stripping instead. Independent confirmation of *this specific claim* would look like another group applying the same degeneracy-breaking parameterization to an independent catalog (e.g., a future Gaia data release, or a non-Gaia cluster survey) and getting the same answer.

## 3. Background Crash Course

You have strong general-science and CS/systems fluency but no assumed background in stellar astrophysics, so here's everything load-bearing, built from the ground up.

**What a stellar population's mass function actually is.** When a giant molecular cloud collapses and fragments, it doesn't produce one star — it produces hundreds to thousands, spanning a huge range of masses, from stars a tenth the mass of the Sun up to ones tens of times more massive. If you histogram "how many stars formed with mass between *m* and *m* + d*m*," you get the **initial mass function (IMF)**: the "as manufactured" size distribution of a stellar population, fixed the moment star formation finishes.

> *Analogy:* think of the IMF as the output size-distribution of a batch manufacturing process — like the distribution of file sizes a write-heavy service produces the instant a batch job finishes writing, before anything gets deleted, compacted, or resized. It's a property of the *production process*, not of what happens to the files afterward.
> *Breaks when:* pushed to imply stars fall into hard, discrete "size classes" — real IMFs are smooth, continuous distributions; the "classes" used below (low/intermediate/high mass) are just convenient bins for describing where the *slope* of that smooth curve changes, not literal categories nature respects.

**Why you never observe the IMF directly.** You can only ever look at a cluster's stars *now*, not at the moment of formation. What you measure is the **present-day stellar mass function (SMF)** — sometimes called the **current mass function (CMF)** in this paper. Three separate physical processes push the SMF away from the original IMF as a cluster ages, and the paper's whole analytical strategy is built around telling these three apart:

1. **Massive stars die young.** The most massive stars burn through their fuel fastest and explode/fade first, so older clusters are simply missing their highest-mass members. (This is also how astronomers date clusters in the first place — the highest surviving stellar mass tells you the age.)
2. **Dynamical stripping preferentially removes low-mass stars.** Inside a cluster, gravitational encounters push toward *energy equipartition* — lighter stars pick up more velocity per encounter than heavy ones (same rough logic as billiard balls of different masses colliding: momentum transfer accelerates the lighter body more), so low-mass stars are more likely to be flung to velocities that escape the cluster or get stripped away by the Milky Way's tidal field. Over time this preferentially thins out the low-mass end.
3. **The molecular cloud environment itself changes over cosmic time.** This is the one the paper actually cares about: the *IMF itself* is theorized to depend on the physical conditions (specifically the sound speed) of the parent cloud, and those average conditions have evolved across the Galaxy's history.

> *Analogy (this is the paper's central organizing idea, worth carrying through the whole analysis):* think of a cluster's mass function like a log file under a retention policy. The **IMF is the initial write pattern** — what got logged the moment the batch job ran. Effects (1) and (2) above are two different kinds of **later eviction**: (1) is a hard TTL expiry (massive stars have a fixed, mass-dependent "shelf life" and disappear on schedule), and (2) is more like LRU-style cache eviction under pressure (lower-priority/lower-mass entries get evicted first as the system experiences churn). Effect (3) is different in kind — it's not eviction at all, it's a change in **what got written in the first place**, because the *initial* write pattern for an older cluster was determined under different upstream config than a younger cluster's. Confusing "different initial write pattern" with "different eviction policy" is precisely the confound this paper is built to resolve.
> *Breaks when:* pushed to imply the "config" driving effect (3) is a single deliberate setting someone tuned — it's an emergent property of Galactic chemical and thermal evolution, not a dial anyone turns.

**Why cloud temperature (sound speed) should set the IMF's shape.** A collapsing cloud fragments into individual star-forming clumps when gravity locally overcomes internal pressure support. The mass scale at which that happens (the *Jeans mass*, not named explicitly in this paper but the physics underlying the citations to Low & Lynden-Bell 1976 and Larson 1985) scales with the cloud's thermal pressure — hotter gas, faster sound speed, more pressure resisting collapse into small clumps, so fragmentation stops at a *larger* minimum mass. The practical prediction: hotter/higher-sound-speed clouds should produce IMFs whose transition point sits at *higher* mass, all else equal.

> *Analogy:* like a memory allocator whose minimum block size scales with a system-level fragmentation-resistance threshold — under more "back-pressure," you stop subdividing into ever-smaller allocations sooner, so you end up with fewer, larger blocks instead of many tiny ones.
> *Breaks when:* pushed to imply one clean, single threshold — real cloud fragmentation is a messy, hierarchical, turbulence-driven cascade, not one deterministic cutoff; sound speed sets a statistical tendency, not a hard rule.

**The Kroupa functional form and "break mass."** Rather than fitting an arbitrary smooth curve, this paper (following Kroupa 2001) models the IMF as three separate power laws stitched together, each covering a different mass range, with fixed transition points. The transition between the "intermediate-mass" and "high-mass" power-law segments — at 0.5 solar masses in the standard Kroupa form — is the **break mass**, and it is the single number this entire paper is built around measuring and tracking. (See the equation box below.)

**Open clusters and the Gaia/Hunt & Reffert catalog.** An *open cluster* is a loosely bound swarm of stars born together and (unlike a globular cluster) not tightly enough bound to survive indefinitely — it slowly dissolves into the general Galactic field over tens to hundreds of millions of years. Gaia, a space astrometry mission, has measured precise positions, distances, and brightnesses for these stars; Hunt & Reffert (2023, 2024) used that data to catalog thousands of open clusters and derive selection-corrected mass functions for each one. This catalog is the paper's entire data source.

**Selection-bias correction via a GAM.** Because more distant clusters can only have their low-mass members detected if the whole cluster happens to have an unusually high break mass (faint low-mass stars simply drop below Gaia's detection floor at large distance), naively correlating break mass with age is contaminated by a distance-dependent selection effect. The paper corrects for this using a **generalized additive model (GAM)** — a flexible, non-parametric regression technique that fits the joint dependence of break mass on *both* age and distance simultaneously, then reports the age-relationship with the distance-driven component statistically removed.

> *Analogy:* this is the same move as correcting a software performance benchmark for a confound in your sampling — e.g., if your profiler only captures stack traces for requests that exceed a latency threshold, you'll see a spurious correlation between "code complexity" and "latency" that's partly just an artifact of your capture floor. A GAM here plays the role of a bias-correction model that separates the real signal (age) from the sampling artifact (distance-dependent detectability).

### Central analogy for this analysis

**A cluster's mass function is a snapshot of an initial write pattern, later reshaped by eviction — and this paper's whole trick is proving the write pattern itself changed, not just the eviction.**

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### The equation the whole paper hinges on

The Kroupa (2001) IMF has more moving parts than a single scalar, so here it is broken down piece by piece before being read as a whole.

$$
\xi(m) = \frac{dN}{dm} \propto
\begin{cases}
m^{-0.3}, & m < 0.08\, M_\odot \\
m^{-1.3}, & 0.08\, M_\odot < m < 0.50\, M_\odot \\
m^{-2.3}, & m > 0.50\, M_\odot
\end{cases}
$$

**What each symbol means:**
- $\xi(m) = dN/dm$ — the number of stars per unit mass interval (how many stars you'd count in a thin mass slice around mass $m$)
- $m$ — stellar mass, in solar masses ($M_\odot$; one $M_\odot$ = one Sun's worth of mass)
- The exponents ($-0.3$, $-1.3$, $-2.3$) — the power-law slope in each regime; more negative means the count falls off faster as mass increases
- $0.08\,M_\odot$ and $0.50\,M_\odot$ — the two **break points** where the slope changes; the second one (0.50 $M_\odot$) is the "break mass" this paper tracks (Gaia can't see below the first break, so only the middle and top segments are ever in play here)

**What this actually means:** it's a piecewise power law — three separate straight lines on a log-log plot, joined end to end, each with its own steepness, switching at two fixed mass thresholds. Think of it as a lookup table with different scaling exponents in different size buckets, the way a caching system might apply different eviction-cost scaling below and above some size threshold.

### The clever trick: why the *choice* of Kroupa vs. Chabrier functional form determines whether this analysis is even possible

This is the paper's actual novel contribution, and it's a clean piece of function-fitting logic rather than new physics.

**The problem:** dynamical stripping (effect 2 above) also changes a cluster's mass function shape over time — specifically, both simulations and semi-analytic models find it acts like multiplying the mass function by a single power-law "depletion factor" that removes proportionally more low-mass stars ($D(m) \propto m^{-d}$ for some depletion exponent $d$). If you fit the result with a functional form that changes shape under that kind of multiplication, you can no longer tell whether an observed shift came from IMF variation or just from dynamical aging — the two effects become **degenerate**.

**Why Kroupa breaks the degeneracy and Chabrier doesn't.** Multiply a piecewise power law by another (single, uniform) power law, and you get another piecewise power law — the slopes shift, but the *location* where the slope changes stays exactly where it was, because that location is a property of where the *original* function's exponent switches, not something the multiplying factor can move. A Chabrier IMF, by contrast, is a log-normal curve below a mass and a power law above it — multiplying a log-normal by a power law does **not** produce another log-normal, so when you force-fit the result with a log-normal model anyway, its "characteristic mass" (the peak location) drifts, purely as a fitting artifact, even when nothing about the true underlying IMF changed.

The paper's own simulated numbers make this concrete (Figure 2). Starting from a true Kroupa IMF and progressively depleting it dynamically (μ = remaining mass fraction, so smaller μ = more depletion):

| Remaining fraction (μ) | Best-fit Kroupa break mass | Best-fit Chabrier characteristic mass |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 (undepleted) | 0.500 $M_\odot$ | 0.107 $M_\odot$ |
| 0.7 | 0.500 $M_\odot$ | 0.107 $M_\odot$ |
| 0.5 | 0.504 $M_\odot$ | 0.252 $M_\odot$ |
| 0.3 | 0.510 $M_\odot$ | 0.408 $M_\odot$ |

Same underlying dynamical depletion, applied to the same true IMF. The Kroupa break mass drifts by about 2% across the entire depletion range; the Chabrier characteristic mass nearly quadruples. That gap is the entire methodological justification for the paper: **fit the SMF with a Kroupa functional form, and any observed shift in break mass can only be coming from the IMF itself, not from cluster aging** — which is precisely the ambiguity that made previous studies (using Chabrier-like or empirical forms) attribute the same break-mass-vs-age pattern to dynamics instead.

### Assumption audit

- **Watch for:** assuming "the IMF varies" means the whole *shape* of the distribution differs cluster to cluster. What's actually shown is narrower: only the **break-mass location** correlates significantly with age. The high-mass slope (Spearman ρ = 0.055, p = 0.57) and intermediate-mass slope (ρ = −0.153, p = 0.06) show **no** statistically significant age dependence in this sample — the paper explicitly separates "the break moved" from "the slopes changed," and only claims the former with confidence.
- **Watch for:** assuming cloud temperature/sound speed was *measured* for each cluster and directly linked to its break mass. It wasn't. Age is the only per-cluster quantity actually measured; the link from age → sound speed runs through a separately modeled, population-level Galactic sound-speed-evolution trend (from a different paper, Steinhardt et al. 2023c), applied as a plausibility check on the *shape* of the age–break-mass trend, not as a per-object physical measurement.
- **Watch for:** reading the JWST/extragalactic implications in the discussion section as something this paper measured. It didn't — the direct result is entirely internal to the Milky Way (age vs. break mass in Galactic open clusters). Everything about high-redshift galaxy masses, star-formation rates, or reconciling anomalously massive early JWST galaxies is an extrapolation discussed in Section 5, not data presented anywhere in the paper.

## 5. What's Genuinely New

The paper's real contribution is narrow and specific, which is worth stating precisely rather than inflating: it is the **first direct, resolved-stellar-population evidence, within a single galaxy, that a cluster's IMF break mass depends on the age (and by inferred extension, the birth-cloud conditions) of the population that produced it** — evidence that survives a specific, previously overlooked degeneracy between "the IMF changed" and "the cluster dynamically aged," by exploiting a mathematical property of the Kroupa functional form that most prior studies didn't have available to them because they used different (Chabrier-like or empirical) parameterizations.

**Falsifiable handle:** the underlying physical model makes a specific, checkable quantitative prediction — break mass should track a modeled Galactic sound-speed-evolution curve, and a roughly 37% increase in sound speed (per the cited theoretical relation) should produce the observed magnitude of break-mass increase (Figure 1, left panel). This would be falsified by, e.g., an old, low-metallicity cluster with an anomalously *low* break mass inconsistent with that curve, or by future higher-precision data revealing that the intermediate/high-mass *slopes* (not just the break) also correlate significantly with age in a way inconsistent with pure dynamical-depletion behavior. The extragalactic extension is separately falsifiable in principle: it predicts that JWST-inferred stellar masses and star-formation rates for very-high-redshift galaxies are systematically overestimated under a fixed-IMF assumption — a claim future reanalyses with variable-IMF templates could directly test.

## 6. Limitations and Open Questions

- **Isochrone-derived stellar masses may carry a low-mass systematic bias.** The paper explicitly flags (§3.2, footnote 3) recent work (Wang et al. 2025; discussed further in Kroupa et al. 2026) suggesting the PARSEC isochrones used to convert Gaia brightnesses into stellar masses could bias masses at the low-mass end. **(A) Consensus-adjacent, self-flagged** — the authors argue this would shift specific break-mass *values* systematically without erasing the *trend*, but that argument is asserted rather than independently demonstrated in this Letter. *(Source: paper §3.2, footnote 3.)*
- **The causal chain from age to birth-cloud sound speed is modeled, not measured, and rests on an assumption about the Milky Way's star-formation history that the authors themselves flag as uncertain.** The paper notes the Milky Way appears to have had an atypically *flat* star-formation rate compared to a "typical" galaxy of its mass, which they say means "significantly improved modeling would be needed to test the break mass evolution quantitatively" — their words, not an inference. **(B) Contested/acknowledged-open** — this is a genuine, author-acknowledged gap between "the trend is qualitatively consistent with theory" and "the theory is quantitatively confirmed." *(Source: paper §4, discussion of SFR history and $T_{\rm IMF}$ decline.)*
- **The final analysis sample is small relative to the parent catalog** — 110 of 3,530 high-quality catalogued clusters survive all quality cuts (332 of 417 under loosened cuts). The authors do run a real robustness check (Appendix Table 1: removing each cut individually, one at a time, and re-testing), which is good practice and shows no single cut is driving the result — but the appendix doesn't test removing *combinations* of cuts simultaneously beyond the one "all cuts loosened together" case. **(A) Consensus concern, partially addressed** *(Source: paper Appendix, Tables 1–2.)*
- **The unresolved-binary-star correction is explicitly called out by the authors as methodologically unsettled**, with the choice of correction "significantly affect[ing] inferred mass functions, particularly at low mass" (§3.2), citing an active disagreement in the literature (Kroupa 1995, 2025; Dabringhausen et al. 2022; Wirth et al. 2024). **(B) Contested, author-acknowledged** — this bears directly on exactly the mass range (near the 0.5 $M_\odot$ break) this paper's central claim depends on. *(Source: paper §3.2.)*

## 7. Detailed Summary

Determining the stellar initial mass function (IMF) has historically only been possible for the very local stellar neighborhood, because it requires resolving individual low-mass stars — everywhere else, astronomers see only integrated starlight and must *assume* an IMF to interpret it. Standard practice treats the IMF as universal. Theory has long predicted otherwise: fragmentation physics ties the IMF's shape to the sound speed of the parent molecular cloud, so different star-forming environments — and different cosmic epochs, as the Galaxy's typical gas conditions evolved — should produce measurably different IMFs.

This paper tests that prediction directly, for the first time, using resolved Gaia observations of Milky Way open clusters (via the Hunt & Reffert 2023/2024 catalog). Its key methodological move is choosing to fit cluster mass functions with a Kroupa (2001) piecewise-power-law functional form rather than a Chabrier-like or empirical form, because — as demonstrated with the authors' own simulated depletion tracks — a Kroupa fit's break mass (the point where the power-law slope changes, nominally 0.5 $M_\odot$) is essentially invariant under dynamical mass-loss, while a Chabrier fit's characteristic mass shifts substantially under the exact same depletion. This breaks a degeneracy that had confounded earlier studies of the same break-mass-vs-age pattern, which typically attributed it entirely to dynamical stripping rather than IMF variation.

After quality cuts (110 of 3,530 catalogued clusters survive, chosen to ensure both low- and high-mass stars are well sampled and break masses are robustly measurable) and correcting for a distance-dependent detection-threshold selection bias using a generalized additive model, the paper finds a statistically robust positive correlation between cluster age and break mass (Spearman ρ = 0.449, p = 8.7 × 10⁻⁷ after bias correction), while neither the intermediate- nor high-mass power-law slopes show a significant age dependence. This pattern survives when any single quality cut is individually removed (Appendix), and strengthens in statistical significance (though not effect size) when the full sample is expanded to 332 clusters under loosened cuts. The direction of the trend — older clusters having higher break masses — is consistent with a Galaxy whose average molecular-cloud sound speed has been declining over cosmic time, in line with the same author group's prior indirect (extragalactic, population-synthesis) evidence for an evolving, environment-dependent IMF.

The authors argue the implications extend well beyond the Milky Way: because unresolved galaxies are analyzed with fixed-IMF pipelines, and because the effect should be largest for the most physically distant conditions (i.e., high-redshift galaxies observed by JWST), correcting for IMF evolution could resolve some claims of implausibly massive early-universe galaxies. That extension is presented as a discussion point and prior-work connection, not as new evidence in this Letter.

### Genuine uncertainty disclosure

Two structurally different kinds of open questions remain, worth keeping distinct. First, an *observational/methodological* residue: whether the specific numerical mapping from age to break mass is quantitatively right (versus just qualitatively right) is explicitly unresolved by the authors' own admission, pending better modeling of the Milky Way's actual star-formation history, and pending resolution of the unrelated isochrone-bias and binary-correction disputes referenced above — both of which happen to bite hardest in exactly the mass range this paper's central number lives in. Second, a *conceptual* residue that the paper doesn't fully resolve: the analysis isolates *break-mass* variation cleanly, precisely because the Kroupa parameterization makes that one number robust to dynamical confounds — but that same choice is why the paper is much less confident about whether the power-law *slopes* also vary with environment (a longer-standing, harder question the authors explicitly flag as requiring "additional advances" beyond what open-cluster analysis alone can currently deliver). The clean result here is clean partly because it was engineered to isolate the one quantity this method *can* cleanly isolate — which is a legitimate and clever use of functional-form choice, but it also means the parts of "IMF universality" this paper doesn't test (slope variation) remain exactly as open as they were before.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **The IMF's break mass — not necessarily its overall shape — measurably increases with the age of Milky Way open clusters, and this can no longer be explained away as dynamical aging**, because the analysis was specifically designed (via the choice of Kroupa functional form) to make that confound mathematically impossible to hide behind.
2. **The trick that makes this work is a piece of function-fitting logic, not new physics**: a piecewise power law's break point is invariant under multiplication by another power law (dynamical depletion), while a log-normal-based fit's peak is not — so the *choice of parameterization* determines whether an old confound can or can't be resolved with the same data.
3. **This is confirmatory evidence for a specific research program's own prior claims**, now upgraded from indirect (unresolved-galaxy template fitting) to direct (resolved-star, within-Galaxy) evidence — a meaningfully stronger evidence class, but for a hypothesis this same group had already been arguing for since 2020, not a finding that came out of nowhere.

## 9. Shorter Summary

*(For quick reference or sharing — full nuance lives above.)*

For decades, astronomers have assumed the stellar initial mass function (IMF) — the size distribution of stars produced by a collapsing gas cloud — is the same everywhere in the universe, mostly because directly testing that assumption requires resolving individual faint, low-mass stars, which is only possible nearby. This paper uses Gaia data on Milky Way open star clusters to test it directly for the first time. By fitting cluster mass functions with a specific mathematical form (the Kroupa 2001 piecewise power law) whose "break point" is provably insensitive to a cluster's subsequent dynamical aging, the authors isolate a genuine, statistically robust trend: older clusters have systematically higher break masses, consistent with the idea that the Milky Way's typical star-forming gas has cooled over cosmic time and that hotter gas produces more "bottom-light" IMFs. The paper doesn't show the whole IMF shape is universally variable — only the break-mass location — and the physical chain from cluster age to birth-cloud temperature is modeled rather than directly measured, with the authors themselves flagging that quantitative (not just qualitative) confirmation will need better modeling of the Milky Way's specific star-formation history. The result strengthens, with direct resolved-star evidence, a case the same research group had previously only been able to make indirectly from unresolved galaxy photometry — with stakes that extend well beyond the Milky Way, since essentially every extragalactic stellar-mass and star-formation-rate estimate implicitly assumes the IMF this paper shows isn't fixed.
