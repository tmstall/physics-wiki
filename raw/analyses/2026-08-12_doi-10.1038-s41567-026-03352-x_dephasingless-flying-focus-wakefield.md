## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**"Outrunning the Speed Limit: Making the Accelerating Field Move at the Speed of Light Itself"**

By reshaping a laser pulse so its *focal point* — not the pulse itself — travels at the vacuum speed of light through plasma, this team got trapped electrons to gain more than twice the energy the textbook "dephasing limit" says should be possible, from the same few millimeters of plasma.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Laser wakefield acceleration (LWFA) is one of the most promising routes to a new generation of ultracompact particle accelerators. Fire an ultrashort, ultra-intense laser pulse into a puff of gas turned into plasma, and the light's radiation pressure shoves electrons out of its path, leaving behind a bare-ion cavity — a "bubble" — that trails the pulse like a wake behind a speedboat. Electrons at the back of that bubble get pulled back in by the exposed positive charge and can ride the resulting electric field, which can exceed 1 gigavolt per centimeter — a thousand times stronger than the fields inside a conventional radio-frequency accelerator cavity. In principle, that means accelerator stages measured in centimeters instead of kilometers.

In practice, LWFA has run into a stubborn wall for two decades: **dephasing**. The plasma wave (the wake) can only move as fast as the laser pulse's group velocity, which — because plasma is a dispersive medium — is always somewhat slower than the vacuum speed of light, *c*. But once an electron is trapped and accelerated, it very quickly becomes ultra-relativistic and moves essentially at *c* itself. The electron is faster than the wave carrying it. Eventually it slides all the way through the accelerating region of the wake and out the other side, into the *decelerating* phase — capping how much energy any single plasma stage can deliver, no matter how strong the field is locally.

The standard workaround is to keep lowering the plasma density, which slows the dephasing problem down but also weakens the accelerating field itself (field strength scales as roughly the square root of density) and demands longer, more elaborate plasma channels to reach useful energies. This paper attacks the problem from a completely different angle: instead of trying to make the *wave* keep pace with the electrons, make the *thing driving the wave* — the focal point of the laser — move at *c* by design, using a specially shaped optic (a "flying focus"). If the drive point moves at *c*, dephasing is eliminated by construction, at least for as long as that engineered focal region can be sustained.

**Paper Type & Stakes.** This is an experimental proof-of-concept demonstration: the first time a flying-focus-driven wakefield has been shown to accelerate electrons well beyond the conventional dephasing limit — by a factor of two in this case — rather than merely being predicted in theory or simulation. What's at stake is whether "dephasingless" laser wakefield acceleration (DLWFA) is a viable engineering path toward 100-GeV-class electron beams from meter-scale (rather than kilometer-scale, RF-cavity-based) accelerators.

**Prior Belief Check.** This result does not contradict the field's consensus — the DLWFA concept was proposed theoretically by some of the same authors back in 2020, and the basic physics (an engineered focal velocity sets the wake's phase velocity, independent of the pulse's group velocity) was already well accepted. What was genuinely uncertain, and what experts would consider non-trivial, was whether it would *survive contact with a real experiment*: a flying focus produced by an axiparabola has a messier transverse structure than an ordinary Gaussian laser pulse (concentric ring-like sidelobes that can drive their own parasitic "secondary" wakefields, and self-steepening effects that can distort the pulse). Whether those real-world complications would quietly sabotage dephasingless acceleration before it could beat the traditional limit was an open, and reasonably contested, question until this paper. So: theoretically expected, experimentally novel and non-obvious in execution — an important confirmatory result rather than a paradigm shift.

**Replication & Convergence Note.** This is a single-group result (University of Rochester's Laboratory for Laser Energetics, on the MTW-OPAL laser) with no independent replication yet. Independent confirmation would look like another flying-focus-capable high-intensity laser facility — ideally with a different axiparabola fabrication and/or full echelon-based timing control — reproducing energy gain beyond the traditional dephasing limit. That matters because the current result depends on this specific optic's fabrication tolerances, this facility's laser characterization, and this group's particle-in-cell (PIC) modeling assumptions all lining up.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Concept A — The plasma bubble (blowout regime).** When the laser pulse is intense enough, it doesn't just nudge electrons — it violently ejects nearly all of them from its path, leaving a bubble of bare, immobile ions behind (ions are ~2,000× heavier and barely move on this timescale). The displaced electrons curve back around and slam into the back of the bubble, both re-populating it and building up a sharp electric field there.

> **Analogy:** Think of the laser pulse as a fast, forceful "write-and-evict" operation sweeping through a cache — it invalidates every electron ("cache line") it touches, leaving a hole (the ion bubble) with only the read-only background data (ions) intact. The evicted electrons don't just vanish; they get pulled back and "refill" the hole from the edges, creating a sharp, moving boundary of activity right where the refill happens.

> **Breaks when:** you ask what the "refill" actually is. In a cache, the refill restores a passive, pre-existing value. Here, the electrons rushing back into the bubble carry real kinetic energy, and some of them get trapped and *become* the particle beam being accelerated — the refill isn't a passive reset, it's the literal source of the physics payload.

**Concept B — Dephasing (the problem this paper solves).** The bubble, and the accelerating field inside it, travels at the laser pulse's group velocity in the plasma, $v\_g$, which is always less than $c$ because plasma is a dispersive medium (it has an effective refractive index below 1). But a trapped electron rapidly becomes ultra-relativistic and travels at essentially $c$. The electron is faster than the accelerating structure carrying it.

> **Analogy:** This is a prefetcher/consumer mismatch. Imagine a prefetch thread trying to stay one step ahead of a consumer process, feeding it data just before it's needed. If the consumer's throughput increases past what the prefetcher can sustain, the consumer eventually catches up to — and then runs past — the prefetch pointer, hits data that hasn't been prepared correctly, and stalls or corrupts its own pipeline. That's dephasing: the electron ("consumer") runs past the front of the accelerating field ("prefetch pointer") and out the far side, into the decelerating region.

> **Breaks when:** you ask "why not just prefetch faster?" You can't simply crank up a laser pulse's group velocity in underdense plasma the way you might widen a prefetch window — $v\_g$ is set by real dispersion physics tied to the plasma density, not a tunable software knob. That constraint is exactly why this paper doesn't try to speed up the pulse; it engineers something else entirely to move at $c$.

**Concept C — The flying focus (the paper's actual trick).** An ordinary lens or mirror focuses all wavelengths/radii of a pulse to the same point at the same time. An axiparabola is a specially shaped mirror whose *focal length depends on radius* — outer rings of the beam focus farther away than inner rings. Because light from different radii arrives at its own focal point via a different path length, the point of peak intensity can be made to arrive at successive positions along the beam axis at a chosen, engineered velocity — independent of how fast the pulse's energy itself is moving.

> **Analogy:** Picture a manycore chip where each core is deliberately given its own dispatch offset so their results land in a downstream buffer in a precisely engineered sequence — not because the cores compute at different underlying clock rates, but because each one's *output timing* is scheduled. The axiparabola does the geometric equivalent: each radial "zone" of the mirror is figuratively "dispatched" to complete its contribution to the focus at a scheduled position and moment, so the *point of peak intensity* sweeps along the axis at whatever velocity the mirror's shape was designed to produce.

> **Breaks when:** you ask where the "new work" is being computed at each stage. In a real pipeline, each stage processes new data. Here, it's the *same original wavefront* of a single pulse, split by radius, each part just taking a different path length. Nothing is being computed anew at each position — it's a purely optical resequencing trick (radius-dependent path length), not staged computation.

> **Central analogy for this paper:** *A prefetch pointer re-clocked to match the consumer.*

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

The experiment (done at the University of Rochester's MTW-OPAL laser: 930 nm center wavelength, ~21 fs pulses, up to 4.8 J) fired a flying-focus pulse — shaped by a custom axiparabola alone (no echelon, the second optic that would normally add fine timing control) — into a 6.6-mm-long gas cell filled with a 95:5 mix of hydrogen and argon. Hydrogen supplied the bulk plasma via straightforward field ionization; argon's inner-shell electrons (ionized from the Ar⁹⁺–Ar¹⁶⁺ charge states) require much higher intensity to ionize, so they get liberated only right at the peak of the laser intensity — a well-established technique ("ionization injection") that seeds fresh electrons directly where trapping is most favorable, rather than relying on background plasma electrons that may be poorly positioned.

The key design choice: without an echelon, the axiparabola alone still produces a focal point whose velocity depends on the plasma's group velocity at a given density (through the relationship between the mirror's radius-dependent focal length and the plasma's optical properties). The authors found — and this was the linchpin of the whole result — that there exists a *specific* plasma density (around $n\_e = 5\times10^\{18\},\mathrm\{cm^\{-3\}\}$) at which this naturally-produced focal velocity happens to equal $c$ almost exactly. Rather than building full independent spatiotemporal control (which would need the echelon), they exploited this one resonant point in parameter space.

The wakefield's actual phase velocity combines that focal velocity with a small correction for how the size of the plasma bubble itself changes along the propagation direction:

$$v\_w(z) = v\_f - \frac\{dR\_b\}\{dt\}$$

**Symbol definitions:** $v\_w(z)$ : the wakefield's (bubble's) phase velocity at position $z$ along the beam $v\_f$ : the velocity of the laser's peak-intensity point, set by the axiparabola's shape $R\_b$ : the blowout radius (size of the ion bubble) $t$ : time

**What this actually means:** it's not enough to make the focal point move at $c$ in vacuum — the bubble itself is also breathing slightly in size as the pulse propagates through changing plasma conditions, and that breathing adds its own small velocity correction. Think of it like a delivery truck (the focus) driving at a fixed target speed while the loading dock it's delivering to (the bubble) is also slowly sliding — you need to account for both to know whether the delivery actually arrives on time.

Whether dephasing is actually eliminated over a given stretch of plasma is captured by the dephasing length — the distance an electron can travel before it outruns the accelerating phase:

$$L\_d = \frac\{c R\_b\}\{c - v\_w\}$$

**Symbol definitions:** $L\_d$ : dephasing length — the maximum useful acceleration distance in traditional LWFA $c$ : vacuum speed of light $R\_b$ : blowout radius $v\_w$ : wakefield phase velocity

**What this actually means:** the denominator, $c - v\_w$, is the size of the "speed gap" between the electron and the wave. In traditional LWFA that gap is small but nonzero, so $L\_d$ is a finite, usually short (few-millimeter) distance. As $v\_w \to c$ — which is exactly what the flying focus is engineered to do — that denominator shrinks toward zero and $L\_d$ blows up toward infinity. This is the mathematical statement of "dephasingless": at the resonant density, the traditional cap on acceleration length formally disappears (Fig. 3 of the paper shows this as a sharp spike in $L\_d$ right at $n\_e \approx 5\times10^\{18\},\mathrm\{cm^\{-3\}\}$).

**Worked numerical micro-example (does the theory match what was actually measured?).** The plasma in this experiment extended roughly 7 mm (0.7 cm), and particle-in-cell simulations predicted an average accelerating field of about $\langle \varepsilon\_z \rangle \approx 1.5,\mathrm\{GV,cm^\{-1\}\}$ if velocity matching were maintained across the *entire* 7 mm:

$$1.5\ \mathrm\{GV,cm^\{-1\}\} \times 0.7\ \mathrm\{cm\} \approx 1.05\ \mathrm\{GeV\ (idealized\ full\text\{-\}length\ prediction)\}$$

The electrons born from near-rest ionization actually measured a maximum energy of $396 \pm 5$ MeV — roughly 0.4 GeV, well over double the traditional dephasing-limited prediction of $185^\{+40\}\_\{-39\}$ MeV at that density, but only about 38% of the idealized 1.05 GeV. Working backward, the *effective* average field actually realized was:

$$\frac\{0.396\ \mathrm\{GeV\}\}\{0.7\ \mathrm\{cm\}\} \approx 0.57\ \mathrm\{GV,cm^\{-1\}\}$$

— roughly a third of the peak field the simulations show was available. The paper's own PIC simulations explain this gap: velocity matching between the flying focus and the plasma wake was imperfect over roughly the first half of the 7 mm, with periods of "stalled acceleration," and only in the final few millimeters did the phase velocity properly lock to the strong-field condition. This single piece of arithmetic is the cleanest way to see both halves of the story at once: the scheme unambiguously beat the traditional limit, and it unambiguously fell well short of its own idealized ceiling in this first attempt.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** A reader might assume "dephasingless" means acceleration is no longer limited by *anything*. The paper actually only removes the *dephasing* limit specifically — acceleration here was still capped at 7 mm by how far the engineered flying focus itself could be sustained (a property of the axiparabola design and available laser energy), and other physics (pump depletion, secondary wakefields from the axiparabola's ring-like sidelobes, self-steepening) still constrain how far the scheme can be pushed.

> **Watch:** A reader might assume that because $v\_w = c$ was engineered at the resonant density, the experiment should have realized close to the full idealized field-times-length energy gain. As the worked example above shows, actual velocity matching was imperfect over roughly half the plasma length; the achieved energy (396 MeV) was well below the idealized ~1 GeV even though it decisively beat the traditional dephasing limit.

> **Watch:** A reader might read the paper's headline scaling projections — 100-GeV electrons from a ~0.66 m accelerator, a ~20× length reduction versus traditional LWFA — as demonstrated results. They are scaling-law extrapolations assuming an *optimized*, fully velocity-matched DLWFA (using the echelon this experiment didn't include), and the paper explicitly states these projections have not yet been experimentally validated.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

1. **First experimental demonstration that a flying-focus-driven wakefield survives contact with reality well enough to beat the dephasing limit.** The DLWFA concept was already established in theory (2020, some of the same authors); what was genuinely open was whether the messier real-world structure of an axiparabola-produced flying focus — concentric ring sidelobes, potential secondary wakefields, self-steepening — would quietly degrade performance below the traditional limit rather than beyond it. This paper resolves that uncertainty in DLWFA's favor for the first time. This is new to the field, not just new to a non-specialist reader.

2. **Getting there with a deliberately minimal optical setup.** Rather than building the full spatiotemporal control apparatus (axiparabola + echelon) that the theory calls for, the team found and exploited a single plasma density at which an axiparabola *alone* naturally produces a near-luminal focal velocity. That's a clever "credit where it's due" simplification — proof-of-concept physics achieved with less hardware complexity than the complete theoretical prescription requires, at the cost of only working well over the final stretch of the plasma rather than the whole length.

**Predictive Content Check — Falsifiable handle:** The paper's clearest near-term falsifiable claim is the projected scaling law, $L\_\mathrm\{acc\},[\mathrm\{m\}] \approx 0.66,(E\_\mathrm\{max\}/100,\mathrm\{GeV\})(a\_0/2.0)^\{-1/2\}(n\_e/5\times10^\{18\},\mathrm\{cm^\{-3\}\})^\{-1/2\}$ — i.e., with proper velocity matching (via an echelon) and near-future multi-petawatt lasers, ~100 GeV electrons should be achievable from roughly 0.66 m of plasma. A nearer-term, sharper prediction: adding an echelon to this same setup should recover most of the idealized ~1 GeV/7 mm field-limited gain (rather than the ~0.4 GeV observed here) by fixing the velocity-matching gap identified above. Both are genuine predictions — not restatements of what's already been observed — and both are testable at existing or near-future facilities (NSF-OPAL, Vulcan 20-20, SEL), so this is a case where the contribution predicts rather than merely relabels.

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

Laser wakefield acceleration promises accelerating fields a thousand times stronger than conventional radio-frequency accelerators, but for two decades has been bottlenecked by dephasing: the accelerating plasma wave can only move as fast as the drive laser's group velocity in plasma, which is always somewhat below the vacuum speed of light, while the electrons being accelerated quickly approach light speed themselves and outrun the wave. This paper demonstrates a different fix than the usual density-lowering workaround: reshape the laser with a custom axiparabola mirror so that the *focal point* — the location of peak intensity, which is what actually drives the plasma wave — sweeps along the propagation axis at an engineered velocity, independent of the pulse's own group velocity. Choosing a plasma density (~5×10¹⁸ cm⁻³) where that engineered focal velocity naturally lands at the vacuum speed of light removes the dephasing limit by construction, at least in principle.

Firing this flying-focus pulse into a hydrogen-argon gas cell, with argon supplying freshly ionized electrons right at the intensity peak for trapping, the team measured electrons reaching up to 396 ± 5 MeV — more than double the 185⁺⁴⁰₋₃₉ MeV that traditional (non-flying-focus) LWFA theory predicts should be the ceiling at that density over that plasma length. Particle-in-cell simulations, run with the actual measured laser and plasma parameters, reproduce this trend across a range of densities and additionally reveal *why* the measured energy fell well short of the idealized ~1 GeV that a perfectly velocity-matched 7 mm stage should deliver: the velocity match between focal point and wake was imperfect over roughly the first half of the plasma, with genuinely accelerating conditions only locking in during the final few millimeters.

The interpretive choice underlying the whole paper is to frame this explicitly as a *proof of concept* rather than a performance record: the headline number isn't the absolute energy (396 MeV is modest by LWFA standards) but the *ratio* — that a flying-focus wake demonstrably outran the traditional dephasing ceiling by a factor of two, confirming that this fundamentally different way of structuring the driving laser does what the 2020 theory said it should, despite the added real-world complexity of the axiparabola's non-Gaussian focal structure. From there, the paper leans on scaling laws (grounded in, and roughly consistent with, both the theory and this experiment's trend) to argue that a properly optimized version — using the echelon this experiment omitted, at near-future multi-petawatt laser facilities — could put 100 GeV electrons within reach of well under a meter of plasma, an order-of-magnitude reduction in accelerator length compared with traditional single-stage LWFA scaling.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the precise physical mechanism behind *why* velocity matching failed specifically over roughly the first half of the plasma and only converged in the final few millimeters. The paper explains this largely through the particle-in-cell simulation's post-hoc account (citing "imperfect velocity matching of the wake" and pointing to Fig. 4's density/injection-position dependence) rather than a fully worked-through analytic derivation of $v\_w(z)$ across the whole 7 mm using equations (1)–(3) in the Methods. My plain-language translation of that mechanism — how the axiparabola's radius-dependent focal length interacts with the plasma's density profile at each $z$ to produce a *time-varying*, not constant, wake velocity early in the propagation — is inference from the compressed Methods equations rather than an independent re-derivation, and is the part of this analysis most likely to have lost real nuance in translation.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. Instead of trying to speed up the laser pulse (which physics won't allow in plasma), this team made the laser's *focal point* — the thing actually driving the accelerating wave — sweep along at the speed of light by reshaping the mirror that focuses it, sidestepping the two-decade dephasing bottleneck entirely.

2. The electrons gained more than double the energy the traditional physics says should be the ceiling at that plasma density and length — real proof the trick works — but still landed at less than 40% of the idealized best-case energy, because the speed-matching between focus and wake only really locked in over the last stretch of the plasma.

3. This is a proof of concept, not yet a record-breaking accelerator: the charge produced is far too low to be useful, and the tantalizing claim of 100-GeV electrons from under a meter of plasma is a scaling projection for a more complete (echelon-equipped) version of this setup, not something that's been built and tested yet.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Laser-driven plasma accelerators can, in principle, replace kilometers of conventional accelerator with centimeters of ionized gas, because the electric fields inside a laser-generated plasma wave are far stronger than anything a radio-frequency cavity can sustain. The catch, for two decades, has been "dephasing": the plasma wave can only move as fast as the driving laser pulse travels through the plasma, which is always a little slower than light speed, while the electrons being accelerated quickly reach light speed themselves and outrun the wave, capping how much energy any one stage can deliver.

This paper demonstrates a way around that limit. Instead of speeding up the laser pulse itself — not physically possible in plasma — the team reshaped the beam with a specially curved mirror (an axiparabola) so that its point of sharpest focus sweeps forward at the speed of light, independent of how fast the pulse's energy is actually moving. Because it's the focal point, not the pulse, that drives the plasma wave, tuning the plasma density so that focal sweep speed lands exactly at light speed removes the traditional dephasing cap by design.

In the experiment, electrons reached energies more than double what traditional theory predicts should be the ceiling at that density and plasma length — direct proof the approach works, and the first time anyone has shown a flying-focus-driven wake surviving real-world complications well enough to beat the dephasing limit rather than being spoiled by them. That said, the electrons only reached about a third of the energy an ideal, fully speed-matched version of this setup should deliver, and the amount of usable beam charge produced remains far too small for practical use. The paper's more ambitious claim — that an optimized version of this scheme could deliver 100-GeV electrons from well under a meter of plasma, roughly a twentyfold reduction in accelerator length versus today's approach — is a calculated projection for future upgraded hardware, not a demonstrated result. Still, as a proof that the core physics survives contact with a real laser and real plasma, this is a genuine, non-trivial step toward compact, ultra-high-energy particle accelerators.

### Q1 — What is group velocity, and why is it "always somewhat slower than $c$" in plasma?

A single, perfect sine wave — one exact frequency, going on forever — has a well-defined speed for its crests: the **phase velocity**. A real laser pulse isn't one frequency; being short in time means (unavoidably, by Fourier transform) it's built from a range of frequencies added together, which interfere constructively in one localized region — the pulse envelope — and destructively everywhere else. The speed of *that envelope* — the actual lump of energy, the thing that drives the plasma bubble — is the **group velocity**, $v\_g$.

In vacuum, phase and group velocity are both $c$, because vacuum doesn't discriminate by frequency. Plasma does: it's a sea of free electrons with a natural sloshing frequency, the plasma frequency $\omega\_\{pe\}$, set by electron density $n\_e$. A laser frequency $\omega\_0$ far above $\omega\_\{pe\}$ barely disturbs the electrons (nearly vacuum-like); closer to $\omega\_\{pe\}$, the electron response drags on the wave more. This gives the dispersion relation:

$$\omega^2 = \omega\_\{pe\}^2 + c^2 k^2$$

**Symbol definitions:** $\omega$ : laser angular frequency (rad/s) · $\omega\_\{pe\}$ : plasma frequency, set by density (rad/s) · $c$ : vacuum speed of light · $k$ : wavenumber (rad/m)

Differentiating ($v\_g = d\omega/dk$) gives:

$$v\_g = c\sqrt\{1 - \omega\_\{pe\}^2/\omega\_0^2\}$$

**What this actually means:** $\omega\_\{pe\}/\omega\_0$ measures how strongly the plasma pushes back on that laser color. Small ratio (dilute plasma) → $v\_g$ just under $c$. Large ratio (dense plasma, approaching $\omega\_\{pe\}=\omega\_0$) → $v\_g$ drops sharply, and above that density the plasma stops transmitting the light at all — this is why LWFA requires *underdense* plasma in the first place.

**Worked number, at this paper's own operating point** ($n\_e = 5\times10^\{18\}\,\mathrm\{cm^\{-3\}\}$, $\lambda\_0 = 930$ nm → $\omega\_0 \approx 2.03\times10^\{15\}$ rad/s):

$$\omega\_\{pe\} \approx 1.26\times10^\{14\}\ \mathrm\{rad/s\} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega\_\{pe\}/\omega\_0 \approx 0.062 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v\_g/c \approx 0.998$$

Only a ~0.2% speed deficit — but plugged into this document's own $L\_d = cR\_b/(c-v\_w)$ (§4), even that tiny gap, divided into a ~10 μm blowout radius, gives a dephasing length of just a few millimeters. That's exactly the traditional 185 MeV limit this paper is racing against: a relativistic electron accumulates that per-cycle slip relentlessly, and a few millimeters is enough distance for it to add up to a full half-wavelength of phase — the difference between "accelerating" and "decelerating."

**One clarifying point:** phase velocity in plasma is actually *greater* than $c$ ($v\_p = c/\sqrt\{1-\omega\_\{pe\}^2/\omega\_0^2\} > c$). Not a relativity violation — an idealized infinite single-frequency wave carries no energy or information at that speed; it's a crest-tracking speed, not a transport speed. Group velocity is the physically meaningful one here, since it's the speed of the actual energy-carrying envelope — i.e., "where the bubble is."

### Q2 — How does the axiparabola's "natural" (echelon-free) focal velocity actually depend on plasma density, and what does the $dR\_b/dt$ bubble-breathing term in $v\_w = v\_f - dR\_b/dt$ physically mean?

**Part A — why $v\_f$ depends on density at all.** The axiparabola's shape fixes a mapping from *radius* to *focal position* — outer rings focus farther out — and that mapping never changes, vacuum or plasma. What *does* change is how long light takes to travel from the mirror to each focal position, because that transit time depends on how fast light moves through whatever's in the way. In vacuum, every radius's light travels at $c$, so the sweep timing is set purely by geometric path-length differences. In plasma, light moves at $v\_g(n\_e) < c$ (Q1 above) — a speed that depends on density. So the *observed* sweep velocity of the focal point is geometry combined with propagation speed, roughly:

$$v\_f(z) \approx v\_g(n\_e)\times G(z)$$

**Symbol definitions:** $v\_f(z)$ : focal point's sweep velocity at position $z$ · $v\_g(n\_e)$ : plasma group velocity at the chosen density · $G(z)$ : a purely geometric "amplification factor" fixed by the axiparabola's shape (radius, extended focal length, nominal focal length) — independent of the plasma

*(Flagged for confidence: this is reconstructed from general axiparabola/flying-focus physics and an outside source, not derived from this paper's own Methods equations (1)–(3), which §7's Genuine Uncertainty Disclosure above already notes weren't independently re-derived. Treat as a supplementary mechanism sketch, not a confirmed result of this analysis.)*

**What this actually means:** $G(z)$ isn't always 1 — near and beyond the mirror's nominal focus, geometric path-length differences can make the *apparent* sweep of the peak run faster than light traveling any single path, even faster than $c$ in vacuum (relativity-safe: this is the "prefetch pointer re-clocked" sequencing trick from Concept C, §3 — nothing physical exceeds $c$). So $G(z)$ can genuinely amplify a subluminal $v\_g$ back up toward, or past, $c$.

**Worked number, tied to Q1:** at $n\_e = 5\times10^\{18\}\,\mathrm\{cm^\{-3\}\}$, $v\_g \approx 0.998c$. For $v\_f = c$ at the relevant $z$:

$$G(z) \approx c/v\_g \approx 1/0.998 \approx 1.002$$

Only ~0.2% geometric amplification needed — entirely plausible for a real mirror figure. **The whole trick in one line:** density sets $v\_g$, geometry sets $G(z)$, their product is $v\_f$ — so there's exactly one density where a fixed, already-built mirror's fixed geometric amplification cancels that density's group-velocity deficit and lands on $c$. This is closer to root-finding (sweep $n\_e$, find where $v\_g(n\_e)\times G(z)$ crosses $c$) than to a driven-oscillator resonance — "resonant point" (§4 above) is a loose label for that crossing, not literal resonance. The full 2020 theory wants two independent knobs (axiparabola = spatial/geometric; echelon = independent temporal) so *any* density could be forced to $v\_f=c$. Without the echelon, density is the only lever, so the authors solved it in reverse: found the one density where the correction is unnecessary — consistent with §5's framing of this as a deliberately minimal optical setup.

**Part B — deriving $v\_w = v\_f - dR\_b/dt$.** Two positions move along the axis: the bubble *front* (where the laser's focal peak currently is — moves at $v\_f$) and the bubble *back* (the sheath boundary where trapped electrons actually sit — the position that matters for the wake physics). The back sits a distance $R\_b(t)$ behind the front:

$$x\_\{\text\{back\}\}(t) = x\_\{\text\{front\}\}(t) - R\_b(t)$$

Differentiate with respect to time:

$$v\_w = v\_f - \frac\{dR\_b\}\{dt\}$$

— exactly the relation in §4. **What this actually means, as a two-pointer sliding window:** a `front` pointer and a `back` pointer where `back = front - windowSize`. If the window is growing ($dR\_b/dt > 0$, bubble expanding), the back pointer falls further behind than it would at fixed window size, so its effective forward speed is *less* than the front's — hence subtract. If the bubble is shrinking ($dR\_b/dt < 0$), the back pointer catches up faster than the front is moving, so subtracting a negative number *adds* to $v\_w$.

**Why this matters for the "imperfect matching over the first half of the plasma" finding (§6, §7):** $v\_f=c$ at the resonant density is a statement about the bubble *front*. The wake velocity an electron at the *back* actually experiences also depends on how fast the bubble is breathing — and bubble size responds to local laser intensity, which itself evolves along $z$ (self-focusing, energy loss, whatever's driving the flying-focus intensity profile). So $v\_w=c$ isn't automatic just because $v\_f=c$; it also needs $dR\_b/dt \approx 0$ at the same time and place — an extra condition on top of the density-tuning trick in Part A. That's consistent with velocity matching only really locking in once the bubble's breathing stabilizes, over the final few millimeters of the 7 mm plasma.
