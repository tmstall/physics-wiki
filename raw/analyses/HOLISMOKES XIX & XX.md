## HOLISMOKES XIX & XX: SN Winny --- The Universe\'s Accidental Gift

****A supernova 10 billion light-years away appeared five times in our sky, and it may be the cleanest shot at resolving the Hubble tension we\'ve ever found.****

### 1. Big-Picture Context

The Hubble tension isn\'t a rounding error --- it\'s a 5-sigma fissure in cosmology. The cosmic distance ladder (Cepheids → Type Ia supernovae → recession velocities) spits out an expansion rate of roughly 73 km/s/Mpc. The CMB-based ΛCDM early-universe calculation returns roughly 67 km/s/Mpc. Those two numbers should agree if our model of the universe is complete. They don\'t, and the disagreement has held up under extreme methodological scrutiny. What\'s needed is a third, completely independent measurement path --- one that shares no systematic errors with either of the other two.

Gravitationally lensed supernovae offer exactly that. By measuring the time delays between multiple copies of a supernova and knowing the mass distribution of the lensing galaxy, scientists can directly calculate the Hubble constant in a single step, with fewer and completely different sources of systematic uncertainties than the cosmic distance ladder. The technique was proposed by Sjur Refsdal in 1964 but has remained tantalizingly out of reach because galaxy-scale lensed supernovae with usable time delays are extraordinarily rare. For decades, only lensed quasars existed in sufficient numbers --- but quasars evolve, vary stochastically, and are hard to use as precise time-domain probes. [Tum](https://www.nat.tum.de/en/nat/latest/article/measuring-the-expansion-of-the-universe-with-cosmic-fireworks/)

Enter SN Winny. SN 2025wny is the first galaxy-scale strongly lensed supernova that turns out to be a superluminous supernova (SLSN), with spatially resolved multiple images and time delays that make it suitable for high-precision cosmography. This is the HOLISMOKES program paying off --- six years of cataloging gravitational lens candidates and waiting for a transient to light up behind one. The event that fired was a one-in-a-million cosmic alignment, and they were ready for it. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21694)

The two papers together form a rapid-response observational campaign: Paper XIX (Taubenberger et al.) discovers and classifies the supernova via imaging and spectroscopy; Paper XX (Ecker, Schweinfurth et al.) delivers the first high-resolution AO imaging from the Large Binocular Telescope (LBT), confirms a fifth lensed image nobody expected, and builds the first mass models of the binary lens system. Together, they lay the observational foundation for what could become a genuine new rung in the cosmic distance measurement game.

### 2. Background Crash-Course

****Strong gravitational lensing and time delays.**** When a galaxy (or two) sits nearly perfectly between us and a distant light source, the galaxy\'s gravity bends the light into multiple distinct images. Think of the galaxy as a wine glass distorting the candle behind it into a ring or multiple arcs. Because light takes different path lengths to reach us through different images, it arrives at different times. That time delay, combined with the lens mass model, encodes the Hubble constant --- essentially because the delay depends on the ratio of angular diameter distances, which are inversely proportional to H₀. Nail the delay and the masses, you get H₀ from geometry, no distance ladder needed.

****Superluminous supernovae (SLSNe-I).**** Type I SLSNe are hydrogen-free stellar explosions that are 10 to 100 times more luminous than ordinary supernovae --- powered likely by a rapidly spinning newborn neutron star (magnetar) injecting spin-down energy into the expanding ejecta, or possibly by ejecta slamming into dense circumstellar material. SLSNe are by far the most luminous supernovae, and they are also extremely UV-bright before and around peak --- meaning that at high redshifts, the observed optical light corresponds to the rest-frame UV regime, making them preferentially detectable by optical surveys at those distances. Critically, their light curves evolve slowly, and time dilation at z=2 stretches them even further, making them hard to miss even through bad weather windows. Compared to a normal SN Ia at the same location, a SLSN is almost comically bright --- detectable where a Type Ia would be invisible. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21694)

****The HOLISMOKES program.**** HOLISMOKES (Highly Optimised Lensing Investigations of Supernovae, Microlensing Objects, and Kinematics of Ellipticals and Spirals) aims to measure the Hubble constant through lensing time delays of SNe, and to constrain SN progenitor properties by using lensing time delays as a cosmic time machine enabling early-phase observations of distant explosions. The strategy: build a master catalog of strong-lens candidates from imaging surveys, cross-match against real-time transient alert streams, and flag coincidences. This is exactly the pipeline that caught SN Winny. [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.21694)

****Singular isothermal sphere/ellipsoid (SIS/SIE) lens models.**** Galaxies aren\'t point masses. The standard parametric shorthand is to assume the mass density falls off as 1/r² in 3D (isothermal), which gives a convergence profile (the dimensionless projected surface mass density κ) that\'s proportional to 1/r in projection. An SIE adds ellipticity. These models are well-tested against lensed quasar systems and give robust Einstein radii --- the angular radius at which a point source would form a perfect ring --- which directly convert to enclosed mass. Analogy: it\'s like fitting a Gaussian to a diffraction peak to extract the slit width. You\'re not modeling every atom in the slit, just the dominant shape that controls the observable.

### 3. Core Technical Explanation

#### Paper XIX: Discovery and Classification (Taubenberger et al.)

The team had compiled a list of strong-lens candidates from the Pan-STARRS 3π survey using a convolutional neural network classifier. Their team identified the transient as a possible lensed SN by cross-matching their lens-candidate list with Lasair on September 21, 2025 --- SN 2025wny being the first detection of a wide-separation, galaxy-scale strongly lensed SN resulting from the HOLISMOKES search approach. [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.21694)

ZTF had first detected the source on August 27, 2025, but GOTO reported it to the Transient Name Server on September 1 as a generic optical transient. It wasn\'t until the HOLISMOKES cross-match that anyone realized it was behind a known lens candidate (PS1 J0716+3821). This 3-week lag between detection and identification underscores how important the pre-compiled lens catalog was --- you need to know where the lenses are before the SN fires.

Photometric monitoring from Lulin and Maidanak observatories confirmed multiple point-like images. Then the team turned to spectroscopy. Optical spectroscopy of the brightest image with the Nordic Optical Telescope and the University of Hawaii 88-inch Telescope allowed them to determine the redshift to be z = 2.008 ± 0.001, based on narrow absorption lines originating in the interstellar medium of the supernova host galaxy. These narrow lines come from the ISM of the host, not the supernova itself --- they\'re sharp, well-defined, and essentially act as a precision ruler at the host galaxy distance. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.21694v1)

The spectral classification as SLSN-I is based on the broad, UV-bright continuum and the pattern of absorption features (primarily O II, C II, Si II at rest-frame UV/optical). The team finds a high ejecta temperature and depressed spectral line depths compared to other SLSNe-I. The depressed lines are interesting: they could indicate higher-than-typical ejecta velocities (which smear and blend spectral features) or the observation is catching the SN at an unusually early, hot phase --- the photosphere is so hot that the same ions that produce absorption features are fully ionized and therefore invisible. Think of it like trying to read a transistor\'s characteristic curves when your operating point is so far into saturation that everything looks flat. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.21694v1)

The team also nails down the redshift of the \"perturber\" --- the secondary, fainter lens galaxy G2. They measure the redshift of the fainter of the two lens galaxies to be z = 0.375 ± 0.001, fully consistent with the DESI spectroscopic redshift of the main deflector at z = 0.3754 --- confirming both galaxies are at the same redshift and physically constitute a binary lens system. This coplanarity is crucial: it means the two galaxies form a single, well-defined lens plane rather than two independent lensing events along the line of sight (which would massively complicate the mass modeling). [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251021694T)

The estimated time delays are days to weeks, inferred from the image separations. For comparison, lensed quasar delays typically run weeks to years; for lensed SNe Ia the delays targeted for cosmography are months. Days to weeks is operationally very convenient --- you can monitor the same event through a single observing season.

Crucially, the team addresses the probability argument head-on. SLSNe are roughly 10⁻⁴ times as common as core-collapse SNe at similar redshifts. But the UV brightness advantage at high z, the slow light curve evolution, and the fact that HOLISMOKES had the right lens in its catalog make this find not a miracle, but the result of a systematic, patient search --- the discovery validates the entire HOLISMOKES methodology.

#### Paper XX: High-Resolution Imaging and Lens Mass Models (Ecker, Schweinfurth et al.)

The LBT is a beast: two 8.4-meter mirrors on a single mount in Arizona, with adaptive optics correcting for atmospheric turbulence in real time using a natural guide star. The team observed in J and K bands (near-infrared, roughly 1.2 and 2.2 microns) on November 25--26, 2025 --- three months after discovery. They selected 29 J-band and 30 K-band frames that delivered the sharpest PSF, achieving full-width at half-maximum of 0.30 arcseconds and 0.22 arcseconds in J and K respectively. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16620)

The critical first step is lens light subtraction. You can\'t measure the supernova image positions if the galaxy glow underneath swamps them. They fit Multi-Gaussian Expansion (MGE) profiles --- basically decomposing each galaxy\'s light distribution into a sum of 2D Gaussians with shared axis ratios and centroids, fitting 30 Gaussian components per galaxy in two independent sets. The two galaxies\' isophotes don\'t significantly overlap, so they can be modeled independently, which is a lucky break.

PSF reconstruction is equally critical, and done with a technique that isn\'t obvious: because there are no stars in the field of view, the PSF was reconstructed directly from 0.75 × 0.75 arcsecond cutouts of the four brightest supernova images (A--D), using the STARRED package, which models the PSF through a regularized, multi-scale approach combining an analytical Moffat profile with a pixelated correction grid. You\'re using the very thing you\'re trying to measure to calibrate your instrument response --- an iterative bootstrap that requires very careful regularization. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16620)

The fifth image, E, is the bombshell of this paper. Its existence was hinted at in CFHT follow-up before the LBT observations. The LBT imaging confirms the presence of a fifth point source whose color is consistent with that of the other SN images at similar phases, while lens modelling robustly supports its interpretation as an additional image of SN Winny. Normally, a single elliptical galaxy lens produces 2 or 4 images. Five images is unusual --- it requires the binary lens geometry (G1 + G2) to put a \"saddle point\" in the lensing potential that generates an extra image, which ends up faint (image E) and located close to the G2 galaxy. The color check is the smoking gun: image E has the same near-infrared K/J flux ratio as images B and C (within 2σ), confirming it\'s the same source at the same phase. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16620)

The mass models use two independent codes --- GLEE and lenstronomy --- to cross-validate. They explore three model classes: SIS (spherically symmetric isothermal) for both galaxies; SIE (adds ellipticity) for G1, SIS for G2; and SIE for G1, SIS for G2, plus external shear. The Bayesian Information Criterion selects the third as optimal. The optimal model class uses a singular isothermal ellipsoid for G1, a singular isothermal sphere for G2, and an external shear, with this choice preferred by both lenstronomy and GLEE. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16620)

The resulting Einstein radii encode enclosed masses: the enclosed masses within the Einstein radius are inferred as 4.61 × 10¹¹ solar masses for G1 and 1.01 × 10¹¹ solar masses for G2. G1\'s stellar velocity dispersion from DESI is 298 ± 37 km/s --- a very massive elliptical. The model fits image positions to an RMS of 0.0012--0.0025 arcseconds, which is tight. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16620)

There\'s one significant red flag the team is honest about: the predicted relative lensing magnifications of the multiple images do not match the observed magnifications within 2σ uncertainties, and the differences could be due to millilensing and microlensing effects. Microlensing --- compact objects (stars, dark matter substructure) in G1 and G2 independently magnifying individual images --- is a known contamination source for lensed SNe. It\'s not catastrophic, but it means the magnification ratios can\'t yet be used at face value for precision cosmography. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16620)

The predicted magnifications span roughly 1.6× for image E to 10× for image A, so even the faintest image is genuinely brightened. The total effective magnification is likely 20--50× for the brightest image, which is why a SLSN at z=2 is detectable with ground-based photometric surveys at all.

### 4. What\'s Genuinely New or Clever

****Trick 1: The binary lens producing 5 images is the key enabler.**** Single galaxy lenses normally give 2 or 4 images. The additional fifth image, forced by the G2 galaxy\'s perturbation of G1\'s lens potential, gives an extra positional constraint that would otherwise be unavailable. More constraints → tighter mass model → more precise time-delay cosmography eventually. And the two galaxies being at the same redshift keeps the model tractable: single lens plane, not a compound system. This is luckier than it sounds, and the team is right to emphasize it.

****Trick 2: Using the SN images themselves as PSF calibration stars.**** In the absence of field stars in the LBT AO field, they bootstrap the PSF from the brightest SN images (A--D). This is methodologically bold --- you\'re fitting the PSF and the source positions simultaneously --- but they validate it carefully with systematic PSF variants (8 different regularization configurations), marginalizing over that uncertainty in the final error budget.

****Trick 3: The HOLISMOKES pipeline as operational infrastructure.**** The fact that SN Winny was identified as a lensed SN candidate within 25 days of first detection --- because it matched a pre-compiled lens candidate catalog during a live transient alert cross-match --- is not luck. It\'s industrial-scale gravitational lens astronomy done correctly. This is the template for LSST-era science: with the imminent start of LSST, the team anticipates finding \~10 lensed Type Ia SNe per year useful for cosmography, with the Euclid+LSST combination being especially powerful. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21694)

### 5. Limitations & Open Questions

****The magnification mismatch is a real problem.**** Microlensing by stellar-mass objects within the lens galaxies can independently boost or suppress individual SN images by 0.1--0.5 magnitudes on timescales of weeks to months. Since different images pass through different parts of the lens galaxy, they sample different microlens populations. Until you model or correct for microlensing, you cannot trust magnification ratios --- and magnification ratios feed directly into constraints on the source model and, eventually, H₀.

****These mass models are explicitly \"not cosmography grade.\"**** The current model is not yet of cosmography grade, since that requires measuring the lens radial profile slope (instead of the isothermal assumption) which needs more observational constraints. Isothermal is a good first guess but real galaxies have mass profiles that deviate by 5--15% from perfect isothermal, and that deviation propagates directly into H₀ uncertainty. Pinning down the profile slope requires either stellar kinematics (velocity dispersion maps of the lens galaxies from integral field spectroscopy) or additional arc morphology constraints --- both of which require significant additional observing. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16620)

****The SN\'s own physics is uncertain.**** The high ejecta temperature and depressed spectral features are unusual compared to other SLSNe-I. Without better photometric phase coverage, they can\'t determine exactly where in the light curve they caught the explosion, which affects spectral template matching and therefore any attempt to use spectral indicators to constrain the lens magnification independently.

****External convergence (line-of-sight mass).**** Every time-delay cosmography measurement faces the \"mass-sheet degeneracy\" --- structures along the line of sight contribute to the convergence κ in a way that\'s degenerate with H₀. Correcting for this requires either deep mass mapping of the environment or statistical priors from line-of-sight structure surveys, neither of which exists yet for this system.

****Time delays not yet measured.**** The time delays are estimated from image separations to be days to weeks, but aren\'t measured yet. Measuring them will require continuous photometric monitoring of all five images through the light curve peak and decline --- an observation program that is actively underway globally, but whose results aren\'t in these papers.

### 6. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1.  ****SN Winny is the first galaxy-scale lensed supernova with the right properties for time-delay cosmography**** --- accessible time delays, a well-constrained binary lens, and five positional constraints --- making it potentially the cleanest new shot at measuring H₀ independently of both the distance ladder and the CMB.
2.  ****Five images from a binary lens is the lucky break that makes precision modeling tractable****: a single lens galaxy would give a messier, less constrained system; a galaxy cluster would be hopelessly complex. Two smooth, non-interacting ellipticals at the same redshift is almost optimal.
3.  ****The HOLISMOKES methodology --- pre-catalog strong lenses, then cross-match live transient alerts --- is now validated**** as capable of catching once-per-decade events in near-real time, and it\'s a template for what LSST will do at industrial scale.

### 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

#### What the papers did and found

The HOLISMOKES collaboration spent six years building a catalog of strong gravitational lens candidates identified in wide-field imaging surveys using machine learning. Their strategy was to wait for a transient source to explode behind one of these catalogued lenses, and then to immediately mobilize follow-up resources. That moment arrived in August 2025.

The Zwicky Transient Facility --- a wide-field camera on Palomar Mountain that scans the sky every few nights --- first detected the transient on August 27, 2025. GOTO independently flagged it days later. When the HOLISMOKES automated cross-match checked the transient\'s coordinates against their pre-built lens catalog, it found a match with a known lens system (PS1 J0716+3821). Within weeks, the team had confirmed via photometry from observatories in Taiwan (Lulin) and Uzbekistan (Maidanak) that multiple point-like images of the same source were present --- the unmistakable signature of strong gravitational lensing.

Spectroscopy with the Nordic Optical Telescope (in the Canary Islands) and the University of Hawaii 88-inch Telescope on Mauna Kea then fixed the redshift. The narrow interstellar absorption lines from the host galaxy\'s gas registered at a cosmological redshift of approximately 2.008 --- meaning the supernova\'s light has been traveling for about 10 billion years. At that redshift, the optical telescope is actually sampling the rest-frame ultraviolet output of the explosion. The spectral shape and features --- broad, hot, hydrogen-free --- are characteristic of a Type I superluminous supernova. SLSNe-I are thought to be powered primarily by a \"magnetar engine\": the rapidly spinning, ultra-magnetized neutron star remnant of the stellar core collapse injects its rotational energy into the expanding ejecta over weeks to months, sustaining the luminosity far beyond what simple radioactive decay of nickel-56 could accomplish. The ejecta temperature in SN Winny appears unusually high, and the spectral line depths unusually suppressed relative to other known SLSNe-I --- possibly because the observation caught the explosion at a very early, hot phase, or because of higher-than-typical ejecta velocities, though the team acknowledges uncertainty here due to incomplete phase coverage.

The two foreground lens galaxies (G1 and G2) are both confirmed at redshift 0.375. G1 was already known from the DESI spectroscopic survey; Paper XIX contributed the first spectroscopic redshift of G2. Finding both at the same redshift is physically significant --- they\'re a gravitationally bound binary galaxy system at the same cosmic distance, forming a single lens plane. This binary geometry is unusual for galaxy-scale lensing (most involve a single dominant lens) and is directly responsible for the existence of a fifth lensed image.

Paper XX focuses on the follow-up imaging campaign using the Large Binocular Telescope on Mount Graham, Arizona. With two 8.4-meter mirrors and an adaptive optics system that corrects the blurring caused by atmospheric turbulence, the LBT delivered images in the near-infrared (J and K bands) with angular resolution around 0.22--0.30 arcseconds --- sufficient to separate and measure all five images and the two lens galaxies with milliarcsecond-level positional precision after careful PSF reconstruction and lens light subtraction.

The confirmation of image E as a genuine fifth lensed image of SN Winny (rather than a background galaxy or noise artifact) rests on two independent lines of evidence: its color (near-infrared K/J flux ratio) is consistent with the other SN images at similar phase, and the best-fit lens mass models predict a fifth image at that location. Its existence is a direct consequence of the binary lens geometry --- the second galaxy G2 perturbs the gravitational potential of G1 enough to create an additional saddle-point image that is fainter (about 6× lower flux than the brightest image A) and located near G2. Standard single-galaxy lenses produce two or four images depending on the source alignment; the fifth image is a distinctive binary-lens signature.

The mass models built in Paper XX use parametric profiles --- primarily singular isothermal ellipsoids and spheres, with an external shear term to capture any contribution from the surrounding large-scale structure. The Bayesian Information Criterion, which penalizes model complexity, selects the combination of an ellipsoidal profile for G1 and a spherical one for G2, both embedded in an external shear field. Both independent modeling codes (GLEE and lenstronomy) agree on this preferred model class, which is a good sign of robustness. The inferred enclosed masses --- roughly 460 billion solar masses within G1\'s Einstein radius and 100 billion solar masses within G2\'s --- are consistent with the known stellar velocity dispersion of G1 from DESI (298 ± 37 km/s), providing a physical sanity check.

The predicted magnifications range from about 1.6× for image E to about 10× for image A. However, the observed brightness ratios of the images don\'t quite match the model predictions. The most likely explanation is microlensing --- individual stars and compact objects within the lens galaxies act as additional tiny lenses, independently magnifying or demagnifying each SN image on timescales comparable to the SN light curve evolution. Microlensing is a known nuisance for lensed SN cosmography and will need to be accounted for statistically in any future H₀ measurement.

The papers collectively set up what will likely become a years-long observational and modeling program: measuring the actual time delays between images (which requires monitoring all five images through the SN light curve peak and decline), obtaining integral field spectroscopy of the lens galaxies to constrain their mass profile slope beyond the isothermal assumption, characterizing the line-of-sight mass structure, and eventually combining all of this into a cosmographic Hubble constant measurement.

#### Why this matters beyond the Hubble constant

SN Winny also opens a new window on distant superluminous supernovae themselves. At z=2, gravitational lensing has amplified the source by a factor of 20 to 50 in the brightest image, turning an otherwise unobservable event into one bright enough for detailed spectroscopic characterization. This means researchers can study the physical properties of a SLSN in the epoch when the universe was young, star formation rates were high, and galaxy metallicities were lower than today --- the conditions thought to favor SLSN progenitors. The host galaxy is presumably a compact, metal-poor dwarf, whose properties can be studied in the magnified arc of lensed host light surrounding the lens galaxies.

### 8. Shorter Summary

SN Winny is a superluminous supernova that exploded 10 billion years ago and whose light passes through two foreground galaxies sitting at roughly one-third of that distance. The gravity of those two galaxies bends the light into five separate copies spread across a roughly 5-arcsecond region of sky. The explosion was first detected by a robotic survey telescope and identified as lensed within weeks because the research team had precompiled a list of known gravitational lenses and cross-matched the transient against it. Spectroscopy confirmed the source redshift at about 2, and the spectra match the hydrogen-free, ultraviolet-bright template of Type I superluminous supernovae --- the most luminous class of stellar explosions known.

The follow-up high-resolution imaging with the Large Binocular Telescope confirmed all five images, modeled and subtracted the lens galaxy light to precisely locate each image to milliarcsecond accuracy, and built the first mass models of the two-galaxy lens system. The masses of the lens galaxies --- about 460 and 100 billion solar masses enclosed within their respective Einstein radii --- and the model configuration (one ellipsoidal galaxy, one spherical, plus an external shear) are well constrained and self-consistent across two independent modeling frameworks.

The scientific goal is to measure the Hubble constant by combining the time delays between the five images (expected to be days to weeks, and currently being monitored) with the lens mass model. This would give a single-step, geometry-based distance measurement independent of every other method currently used in the Hubble tension debate. The current mass models are a solid starting point but not yet precise enough for cosmography --- that will require measuring the actual mass profile slope beyond the isothermal assumption and correcting for microlensing contamination. Both of those steps require additional observations, which are actively in progress.

Got it --- the ****Point Spread Function**** reconstruction in Paper XX.

This is the genuinely tricky part of the whole analysis. Here\'s what makes it interesting:

****The bootstrap problem.**** Normally you calibrate your instrument\'s PSF using known point sources --- stars --- in the same image. No stars in the LBT field means you have to use the SN images themselves as your PSF reference. But those SN images are also what you\'re trying to measure. It\'s like trying to calibrate your oscilloscope probe using the signal you\'re trying to characterize.

****How STARRED handles it.**** The PSF model has two components:

- An analytical ****Moffat profile**** --- a bell-shaped function with heavier wings than a Gaussian, which captures the seeing-limited core well. This is the \"coarse\" component with only a few free parameters.
- A ****pixelated correction grid**** on top of that, regularized in wavelet space --- this soaks up the non-analytic asymmetries from the AO system, mirror aberrations, and atmospheric residuals that the Moffat can\'t capture.

The regularization in wavelet space is the key trick --- it penalizes high-frequency noise in the pixelated component, keeping it from overfitting to the photon noise in the SN cutouts, while still allowing real PSF structure through.

****Why the error budget is subtle.**** They ran 8 PSF variants (circular vs. elliptical Moffat, different regularization strengths for high-frequency and low-frequency wavelet scales) and marginalized over all of them. The result is that the systematic PSF uncertainty ends up comparable in size to the statistical MCMC positional uncertainty --- meaning PSF reconstruction is not a subdominant error, it\'s roughly half the total error budget on image positions. That 1--14 milliarcsecond range in position uncertainties you saw in the paper is largely driven by this.

The ****Point Spread Function (PSF)**** is the response of an imaging system to a perfect point source of light --- it describes how a single infinitely small point of light gets smeared out into a finite blob by the time it hits your detector.

In a perfect, diffraction-limited optical system the PSF is an ****Airy disk**** --- a bright central spot surrounded by faint rings, dictated purely by the wave nature of light and the diameter of your aperture. A larger mirror gives a tighter Airy disk. In practice, the PSF is always worse than the diffraction limit because of atmospheric turbulence, optical aberrations, mirror surface imperfections, and detector effects.

****The core concept.**** Every image you take is mathematically the convolution of the true sky with the PSF. Think of it like this: every point source in the sky gets replaced by a copy of the PSF, scaled by the source brightness, and all those copies pile on top of each other. The image you record is the sum of all those smeared copies. If you want to know what the sky actually looks like --- or measure positions and fluxes precisely --- you have to either deconvolve the PSF out, or forward-model it directly.

****How it\'s used in practice:****

In astrometry (measuring positions, like in Paper XX), you fit a PSF model to each source to find its centroid. The accuracy of the position depends entirely on how well you know the PSF shape --- a wrong PSF model biases the centroid.

In photometry (measuring brightness), you integrate the PSF fit to get the total flux, which is how they measured the K/J flux ratios of images A through E to check whether image E matched the other SN images in color.

In source separation, knowing the PSF lets you subtract the lens galaxy light (the MGE model convolved with the PSF) cleanly, leaving just the SN point sources behind --- which is exactly what Paper XX had to do before it could measure anything.

****The AO complication.**** Adaptive optics systems partially correct the atmospheric blurring in real time, which dramatically sharpens the PSF core --- but they also introduce complex, asymmetric structures in the PSF wings that vary across the field and change with atmospheric conditions. An AO PSF is much harder to model than a simple seeing-limited Moffat profile, which is why the pixelated correction grid on top of the Moffat is necessary in STARRED rather than just fitting a clean analytic shape.

****H₀ (the Hubble constant)**** is the present-day rate at which the universe is expanding, expressed as a velocity per unit distance --- how fast a galaxy is receding from us per megaparsec of distance between us and it. The units are km/s/Mpc.

Concretely: a galaxy 1 megaparsec away recedes at H₀ km/s, a galaxy 2 megaparsecs away recedes at 2×H₀ km/s, and so on. It\'s the proportionality constant in Hubble\'s Law --- recession velocity scales linearly with distance.

The current measured values cluster around ****67--73 km/s/Mpc**** depending on method, and that 6-unit gap is the Hubble tension --- the two best-measured values (CMB at \~67, distance ladder at \~73) disagree at the 5-sigma level, which is why it\'s a genuine crisis rather than a rounding dispute.

****Why it matters beyond just an expansion rate:**** H₀ sets the overall scale of the universe. It determines the age of the universe (roughly 1/H₀), the size of the observable universe, and the distances to everything in it. If H₀ is wrong, all cosmological distances are wrong by the same fractional amount. It also tightly constrains the energy content of the universe --- how much is dark matter, how much is dark energy --- because those components control how the expansion rate has evolved over time.

In the SN Winny context, the time-delay cosmography method gives H₀ directly from geometry: the time delay between images depends on the path length difference, which scales as 1/H₀. Measure the delay, know the lens mass, get H₀ --- no distance ladder rungs required.
