They work in flat Minkowski spacetime written in advanced null coordinates (v, r, angles), where v is advanced time. There is a thin spherical shell of charge that collapses radially at the speed of light and reaches the origin at advanced time v = 0.

- For v \< 0 the space is completely empty (vacuum). 

- For v \> 0 a Coulomb field suddenly appears (like a point charge at the origin). 

A massless probe particle that carries charge q feels this background through the Lorentz force (or, equivalently, through a simple worldline action that includes an interaction term a · ż, where a\_μ is the effective potential felt by the probe).

Because the shell is infinitely thin, most of the particle’s motion is “free” (straight null lines in the coordinates they use), except exactly at the moment it crosses v = 0.

### 2. The classical problem they solve: what happens when the probe crosses the shell?

Before the shell arrives (v \< 0) the probe has some conserved energy parameter ℰ (essentially its frequency or energy measured by a distant observer).

After it crosses the shell the space has a Coulomb field, so the probe’s energy is again conserved, but with a *different* value.

The jump in energy, Δℰ, occurs instantaneously as the probe crosses v = 0. From the action or the equations of motion they find:

Δℰ = 2 g² q Q / v   (with appropriate sign conventions for attractive charge)

Here v is the advanced-time coordinate at which the particle is coming in from past null infinity (I⁻). The important point is that there **is** a sudden energy kick whose size depends on the charges and on how “early” the particle crosses (the v value).

This is a purely classical, flat-space effect — there is no horizon, no curvature, nothing relativistic in the geometric sense.

### 3. The double-copy translation to gravity (real Vaidya spacetime)

The double copy is a systematic rule that turns certain gauge-theory solutions into gravitational solutions. In this case the rules are roughly:

- Replace the gauge coupling factor g² with 2G (Newton’s constant). 

- Replace the product of charges q Q with something involving the probe’s own energy ℰ and the mass M of the collapsing shell: q Q → M ℰ (or more precisely M ℰ′ where ℰ′ is the *outgoing* energy after the jump). 

When you apply these replacements you get the corresponding energy jump in the *gravitational* Vaidya spacetime:

Notice the crucial difference: on the gravity side the energy shift is **proportional to the probe’s own energy ℰ**. That is the seed of the redshift.

They also introduce a small shift in the time coordinate itself: v → v − v₀ with v₀ = −4 G M. This v₀ sets the characteristic scale of the horizon that will form (the horizon radius is 2 G M, and v₀ is directly related to that scale).

### 4. Why this captures “the redshift factor that depends on how close it passes to the would-be horizon”

In the real gravitational Vaidya spacetime a black hole forms after v = 0, with an event horizon at r = 2 G M.

Imagine two different probe particles (or, later, wave modes):

- One that crosses the shell far from the center (large impact parameter, never gets close to r = 2 G M). It barely feels the forming black hole; its energy barely changes. 

- One that crosses the shell on a trajectory that would have skimmed right next to where the horizon is forming (very small effective impact parameter relative to 2 G M). In gravity this particle (or the wave packet) suffers enormous **gravitational redshift** as it tries to climb out. Its energy at future infinity ℰ′ is *much smaller* than the energy it had near the shell (ℰ′ ≪ ℰ). The closer it was to the would-be horizon, the stronger this redshift. 

On the gauge-theory side there is no horizon and no redshift — the only thing that happens is the classical energy kick from the Coulomb field.

But because the double-copy rule replaces q Q with M ℰ (i.e. the shift becomes proportional to the probe energy itself) and introduces the time offset v₀ ~ G M, the *functional form* of the accumulated phase (the eikonal phase χ(v) ~ constant × log(−v)) on the gravity side ends up depending on the *outgoing* energy ℰ′ and on how close the trajectory was to the horizon scale set by v₀.

In other words, the mapping “imports” the horizon proximity information into the phase even though the original gauge-theory calculation never knew about a horizon.

### 5. Why they care (the bridge to Hawking radiation)

Later in the paper they promote this classical phase to a quantum wavefunction (via the worldline formalism or eikonal scattering). So the seemingly innocent classical energy jump of a probe particle crossing a charge shell, once double-copied, automatically encodes the gravitational redshift that depends on proximity to the forming horizon — and that is ultimately what makes the radiation thermal.

**Simple analogy**

That is the deep reason the classical double copy works so cleanly here: it maps the simple flat-space kick into the curved-space redshift in one stroke.
