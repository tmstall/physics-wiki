## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**The Proton's Uninvited Guest: Charm Quarks That Were There All Along**

For 40 years, physicists have argued about whether the proton is *born* with charm quarks baked into its own structure, or only ever borrows them briefly from the vacuum's radiation — and this paper closes the argument with a bookkeeping trick, not a new machine.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

The proton is officially "two up quarks and a down quark," but that's the accountant's answer, not the physicist's. Quantum field theory says the proton's wavefunction also contains an unbounded sea of virtual quark–antiquark pairs, popping in and out of existence, on top of the three "valence" quarks that carry its net charge and quantum numbers. Since the 1970s, deep-inelastic scattering experiments (electrons or muons fired at protons) have mapped out exactly how much of the proton's momentum each type of quark and the gluons carry, as a function of how hard you look — the *parton distribution functions* (PDFs) that every LHC prediction is built on.

For the three *light* quarks (up, down, strange — all lighter than the proton itself), nobody doubts they're part of the proton's intrinsic makeup: standard energy–mass reasoning says they must be. The controversy is about *heavy* quarks — charm, bottom, top — whose mass exceeds the proton's own rest mass (charm alone weighs about 1.6 times what the whole proton weighs). Everyone agrees these heavy quarks show up inside the proton at high enough collision energy, because the collision itself radiates them into existence via ordinary QCD processes — that part is textbook and calculable. The open question, live since 1980, is whether heavy quarks are *also* present as a genuine, non-radiative part of the proton's own bound-state structure — so-called **intrinsic** heavy quarks. For the charm quark specifically, whose mass sits closest to the proton's own mass scale, this has been the most fought-over case. Prior dedicated searches landed all over the map: some excluded an intrinsic charm momentum fraction above 0.5% at high confidence, others found room for up to 2%. Forty years, no consensus.

This paper claims to settle it — not by building a new detector, but by finding a clean, unambiguous *definition* of "intrinsic" that lets a state-of-the-art global QCD fit isolate the signal from the radiative background it's always been tangled up with.

**Paper Type & Stakes.** This is a data-driven QCD structure paper: a global PDF fit (NNPDF4.0) combined with a scheme-based extraction technique, aimed at resolving a specific 40-year-old open question in nucleon structure with direct consequences for LHC precision phenomenology and for the design of future facilities (Electron–Ion Collider, Forward Physics Facility, high-energy neutrino telescopes) that care about the proton's heavy-quark content.

**Prior Belief Check.** The qualitative *existence* of intrinsic charm is not, by itself, shocking to experts — it's been a live theoretical possibility since 1980, and several previous analyses already leaned toward a nonzero result. What *is* genuinely notable to specialists is that this paper claims to have resolved a question the field could not resolve for four decades, and does so with a level of statistical rigor (an unambiguous, scheme-independent definition of "intrinsic," plus two independent empirical cross-checks) that earlier claims on either side of the debate generally lacked. This is best read as a confirmatory-but-decisive result: it doesn't overturn the mainstream view so much as finally adjudicate a real, long-running expert disagreement in favor of "yes, it's there, and it's small."

**Replication & Convergence Note.** The core determination is single-group: one collaboration (NNPDF), one global fit, one software framework (NNPDF + EKO). However, the paper itself builds in two forms of *data* cross-validation that function as partial, internal replication: brand-new LHCb measurements of Z-boson-plus-charm-jet production (not part of the baseline fit) and decades-old EMC charm-production data, from a completely different process and energy regime, each independently push the significance up toward or above 3σ. That's meaningfully stronger than a single unchecked fit, but it is not the same as true independent replication — another PDF collaboration (CTEQ-TEA/CT, MSHT) applying this same scheme-based disentangling method to their own independently-built global fit. As of this paper, that hasn't happened.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**What's actually "in" a proton.** Picture the proton's valence quarks (two up, one down) as a program's persistent global variables — always present, defining the object's identity (its charge, its quantum numbers). Layered on top is a sea of quark–antiquark pairs that are constantly created and absorbed by the strong force, the population of which depends on how finely you "resolve" the proton — the higher the collision energy, the more of this transient population becomes visible, in a well-understood, calculable way (perturbative QCD radiation).

> **Analogy:** The sea of light quark–antiquark pairs is like short-lived memory allocations that spawn and vanish under the hood as a program runs — invisible at low "resolution," increasingly numerous the more finely you profile the system.
>
> **Breaks when:** you push it to ask whether these are "real." Unlike genuinely disposable allocations, the sea-quark population isn't noise to be garbage-collected away — it's a real, measurable, persistent statistical population (a probability density in momentum fraction) present at every snapshot, just one whose *size* depends on the resolution scale you probe it at.

**The actual controversy: is charm *only* radiative, or also intrinsic?** Because charm is heavier than the proton, in principle it shouldn't need to be part of the proton's *low-energy* bound-state structure at all — ordinary QCD radiation is enough to explain why you see charm quarks in high-energy collisions. The question is whether there's *also* a genuine, non-radiative charm component baked into the proton's own wavefunction at any energy, however low — this is what "intrinsic" means. Finding a nonzero charm PDF at some scale is *not* enough to prove this: radiative QCD alone predicts nonzero charm at any energy above the charm mass. You have to disentangle the two contributions, and that's the paper's central technical move.

**The trick: change your bookkeeping scheme, then subtract.** QCD calculations come in different "flavour number schemes" depending on how many quark types you treat as active radiative degrees of freedom. In the **4-flavour scheme (4FNS)**, charm is treated as one of the actively-radiating quarks, so the 4FNS charm PDF mixes an intrinsic piece (if any) with a purely radiative piece generated by known, calculable QCD processes. In the **3-flavour scheme (3FNS)**, only up, down and strange are treated as active — charm is *not* a radiative degree of freedom in this scheme at all. Perturbative QCD provides an exact, invertible recipe (the "matching conditions") for converting a PDF between these two schemes. Crucially: if there is truly *no* intrinsic charm, then the entire 4FNS charm PDF must be fully explainable — fully *recomputable* — from the light-quark PDFs via these matching conditions alone. Converting it back to the 3FNS should then give exactly zero, by construction.

> **Analogy:** This is a "recompute and subtract" move straight out of memoization. If a value can be recomputed in full from other known inputs via a known, deterministic function, then any input value's genuine information content is only what's left over *after* recomputing that derivable part and subtracting it out. Here, the "known deterministic function" is the matching-condition machinery; the "derivable part" is the radiative charm content generated purely from the light quarks; and whatever survives the subtraction — the 3FNS charm PDF — is by definition the part the light quarks and radiation alone can't explain: intrinsic charm.
>
> **Breaks when:** you assume the "recompute" step is exact. The matching-condition function is only known to a finite order in perturbation theory (here, NNLO, cross-checked at one order higher, N3LO) — like a memoized function whose implementation has residual imprecision. That imprecision is exactly the source of the paper's dominant theoretical uncertainty at small momentum fractions.

**Central analogy for this paper:** Recompute the derivable, subtract, keep the residual.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**Step 1 — fit the ordinary (4FNS) charm PDF from data, with no assumed shape.** The NNPDF4.0 global analysis fits all PDFs — including charm — to 4,618 measured cross-sections spanning fixed-target and collider experiments: deep-inelastic scattering structure functions (notably from HERA), Drell–Yan and W/Z production (ATLAS, CMS, LHCb), jets, top-quark pairs, and more (Extended Data Fig. 2 shows the huge kinematic reach this buys, from x ~ 10⁻⁴ up to x ~ 1, and Q² from a few GeV² to 10⁷ GeV²). Rather than assuming a functional form for the charm PDF's shape (as essentially every prior intrinsic-charm search did — fit a specific model like BHPS and let only its overall size float), NNPDF parametrizes it with a neural network:

xc⁺(x, Q₀) is built from two neural-network output channels (the flavour "singlet" and a specific non-singlet combination called T₁₅), each multiplied by preprocessing exponents that enforce known small- and large-x behaviour, then combined and evolved with NNLO QCD to any energy scale.

**Symbol definitions:** *x* : the fraction of the proton's momentum carried by the charm-plus-anticharm pair · *Q* : the energy/resolution scale of the probe (GeV) · *Q₀* : the scale at which the fit is parametrized (1.65 GeV) · NN(x, θ) : neural-network output as a function of momentum fraction and trained weights θ.

**What this actually means:** this is a curve-fitting neural network in the plainest sense — like training a flexible model to match a scatter of data points without pre-committing to "it's a quadratic" or "it's an exponential." The physics doesn't live in the network architecture; it lives in the QCD evolution equations and factorization theorems that relate the fitted shape at Q₀ to predictions at every other scale and process. The network's only job is to not bias the shape.

**Step 2 — transform to the 3FNS and read off the residual.** Having fit the 4FNS charm PDF at the charm-mass scale (Q = 1.51 GeV), the authors apply the matching-condition transform (via an open-source evolution code, EKO) to get the 3FNS charm PDF. The result (Fig. 1 of the paper) has a distinctive "valence-like" bump peaking around x ≈ 0.4 — meaning that when intrinsic charm shows up, a single charm quark tends to be carrying a large fraction, roughly 40%, of the whole proton's momentum, which is a striking, specific shape (not a flat or featureless residual). Repeating the transform at one perturbative order higher (N3LO instead of NNLO) leaves this peak essentially unchanged, which is the paper's way of demonstrating the result isn't an artifact of stopping the calculation too early — though the same comparison shows the uncertainty band exploding for x ≲ 0.2, exactly where the "recompute" step is least trustworthy.

**Step 3 — cross-validate with two independent, out-of-sample measurements.** The strongest piece of the argument isn't the fit itself, it's what happens when the authors check it against data the fit never saw.

First, very recent LHCb measurements of Z bosons produced alongside charm-tagged jets, in LHCb's forward-rapidity acceptance — a kinematic region that is specifically sensitive to *large*-x charm, i.e. exactly the region where intrinsic charm should dominate over radiative charm. The observable is a simple ratio:

R꜀ʲ(yZ) = N(c-tagged jets; yZ) / N(jets; yZ) = σ(pp → Z + charm jet; yZ) / σ(pp → Z + jet; yZ)

**Symbol definitions:** R꜀ʲ(yZ) : ratio of charm-tagged to all-jet Z+jet events, as a function of Z-boson rapidity · yZ : how forward (close to the beam direction) the Z boson is produced — higher yZ probes higher charm momentum fraction x · σ(...) : the cross-section (production rate) for each process.

**What this actually means:** think of yZ as a dial that lets you zoom the "probe" into different momentum-fraction windows of the proton, the way changing a query's filter zooms a database scan into a specific index range. At the highest-yZ bin — the one most tightly correlated with the x ≈ 0.45 region where the intrinsic peak sits — the prediction that *assumes zero intrinsic charm* undershoots the actual LHCb data by about 3σ, while the prediction that includes the fitted intrinsic component matches well. This is a genuine before-the-fact comparison: the intrinsic-charm shape was fixed by the earlier fit, and only then compared against a measurement it hadn't seen.

Second, decades-old EMC fixed-target data on charm production in deep-inelastic scattering — historically the *original* main evidence for intrinsic charm, but explicitly flagged by the authors as imprecise, of contested accuracy, and taken at low scales where radiative corrections are large (which is exactly why it was left out of the baseline fit). Folding it in anyway nudges the significance up by a comparable amount to the LHCb data, despite the two measurements coming from utterly different processes, energies, and decades.

Combined, the local statistical significance goes from about 2.5σ (baseline fit alone) to roughly 3σ or higher (with either or both cross-checks included) in the peak region 0.3 ≲ x ≲ 0.6.

**A worked numerical check — how "2σ" gets read off a χ² table.** The Methods section reports that forcing intrinsic charm to vanish makes the global fit noticeably worse: the total χ² per data point rises from 1.162 to 1.198. With roughly 4,618 data points in the fit, that's a total χ² increase of about (1.198 − 1.162) × 4,618 ≈ 166 — which matches the paper's own reported number almost exactly. Converting a χ² excess into an equivalent number of standard deviations uses σ ≈ Δχ² / √(2 × n_dat):

σ ≈ 166 / √(2 × 4,618) = 166 / √9,236 ≈ 166 / 96 ≈ 1.7–2

**Symbol definitions:** Δχ² : the total worsening of fit quality (166) when intrinsic charm is forced to zero · n_dat : number of data points in the global fit (~4,618) · σ : the resulting significance in standard deviations.

**What this actually means:** this is the classic move from computer-systems A/B testing — if disabling a feature (here, "intrinsic charm") measurably degrades an aggregate error metric across thousands of independent test cases, and the degradation is big compared to the expected noise (√n scaling), that's evidence the feature was doing real work, not just adding decorative flexibility. The arithmetic above lands close to, if not exactly matching, the paper's own quoted "2σ effect" — the small gap is a reasonable byproduct of rounding and of exactly which subset of data points enters the reported χ² breakdown, which the paper doesn't fully spell out.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** Reader might assume "the fit finds a nonzero charm PDF at some energy" is itself the evidence for intrinsic charm. The paper explicitly rejects this — ordinary QCD radiation guarantees a nonzero charm PDF at any scale above threshold even with *zero* intrinsic content, which is precisely why the whole 3FNS/4FNS matching apparatus exists: to separate the guaranteed-nonzero radiative piece from any genuine intrinsic residue.

> **Watch:** Reader might assume this is a first-principles calculation of proton structure (e.g., something computed from the QCD Lagrangian via lattice methods) that either proves or disproves intrinsic charm outright. It's not — it's an empirical, data-driven global fit. Theory supplies only the perturbative matching/evolution machinery that relates PDFs across scales and schemes; the actual *shape and size* of the charm PDF is 100% determined by fitting real cross-section data, with the neural network explicitly built to avoid injecting a theoretical shape assumption.

> **Watch:** Reader might read "2.5–3σ" the way particle physics headlines usually use significance numbers — i.e., as approaching "discovery" territory (the 5σ convention used for things like the Higgs boson). This paper's own title says "evidence," which in standard particle-physics convention specifically denotes the 3σ-ish tier, a full two orders of magnitude in false-positive probability short of "observation"/"discovery" at 5σ. The paper doesn't overclaim this distinction, but a casual reading easily could.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**New idea #1 — turning a scheme choice into a measurement tool.** Previous searches for intrinsic charm generally worked by fitting a specific theoretical model (e.g., the 1980 BHPS shape) to data with only its overall normalization left free — meaning the result was only ever as good as the assumed model shape, and different models gave you different answers to compare. This paper instead defines "intrinsic charm" operationally and model-independently: it's whatever charm PDF survives after transforming a *data-fitted, shape-unconstrained* charm PDF into a scheme where the purely radiative part is guaranteed to vanish by construction. That's new to the field, not just new to the reader — it turns a formal bookkeeping device (factorization scheme choice) that QCD practitioners normally treat as an arbitrary convention into the actual extraction mechanism.

**New idea #2 — triangulating with a measurement designed to fail if the effect were fake.** Deliberately picking a completely independent, very recent LHCb measurement whose forward kinematics are known in advance to be maximally sensitive to exactly the momentum-fraction region the fit's own intrinsic peak occupies is a strong piece of experimental design-after-the-fact: it's not just "more data agrees," it's "we predicted this specific ratio would be enhanced in this specific kinematic bin, and it was."

**Predictive Content Check**

1. **Falsifiable handle (always):** Yes, genuinely predictive rather than a relabeling. The fit produces a specific, checkable shape (valence-like peak at x ≈ 0.4) and a specific momentum-fraction scale, (0.62 ± 0.61)% including all theoretical uncertainty — and it makes an out-of-sample, quantitative prediction for the LHCb R꜀ʲ ratio in the forward-rapidity bin, which was then checked against real, independently-collected data and found consistent (while the zero-intrinsic-charm alternative was found inconsistent at ~3σ). That's about as close to a textbook falsifiable prediction as this kind of global-fit paper gets.

2. **Formalism load (conditional — fires here):** The paper leans heavily on perturbative-QCD matching machinery and a neural-network parametrization, so this is worth asking directly: is any of it decorative? No — both pieces are load-bearing. The matching-condition transform isn't just supporting evidence, it *is* the operational definition of "intrinsic" used throughout; remove it and there's no way to separate intrinsic from radiative charm at all. The neural network is likewise load-bearing rather than decorative: its entire purpose is to avoid assuming a model shape in advance, which is the paper's explicit point of departure from every prior model-fit approach to this question.

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

For four decades, particle physicists have argued over whether the proton's own bound-state structure includes a genuine, low-energy population of charm quarks — "intrinsic charm" — on top of the charm quarks that ordinary high-energy collisions are known to radiate into existence. The trouble was never really about data quality; it was that nobody had a clean way to prove a measured charm signal was intrinsic rather than radiative, since radiative QCD alone guarantees some nonzero charm PDF at any energy above threshold.

This paper's resolution rests on a single conceptual move: treat the choice of QCD "flavour number scheme" not as bookkeeping trivia but as a diagnostic tool. Fit the proton's charm content with maximal freedom (a neural network, unconstrained by any assumed model shape) in the standard 4-flavour scheme, where charm is an actively radiating parton. Then apply the well-known, calculable transformation into the 3-flavour scheme, where charm is *not* an active radiative degree of freedom at all. If intrinsic charm doesn't exist, this transformation is guaranteed to return exactly zero — because in that scenario the entire 4-flavour charm content would, by hypothesis, be nothing but a computable function of the light quarks. Whatever survives the transformation is, by construction, the part that computable function can't explain: the intrinsic component.

The result is a valence-like distribution peaking near x ≈ 0.4 — meaning intrinsic charm, when present, tends to carry a substantial chunk of the proton's momentum on a single quark — matching the qualitative shape predicted by 40-year-old theoretical models, at roughly the 2.5σ level from the global fit alone. The authors then do something a purely theoretical result can't: they check it against two experiments the fit never saw. A very new LHCb measurement of Z-boson-plus-charm-jet production, chosen specifically because its forward kinematics are sensitive to exactly the high-momentum-fraction region where intrinsic charm should dominate, independently favors the intrinsic-charm prediction over the no-intrinsic-charm alternative by about 3σ. Decades-old EMC deep-inelastic charm data, historically the original (if shaky) evidence for intrinsic charm, gives a similar boost despite coming from a wholly different process, energy scale, and era. Combined, the local significance in the peak region reaches or exceeds 3σ — comfortably into the particle-physics convention for "evidence," though not "discovery."

The interpretive choice worth flagging: this analysis leans hard on the idea that "intrinsic" is best understood as *whatever a well-defined recompute-and-subtract operation leaves behind*, rather than as a directly observed particle population. That's a faithful reflection of how the paper itself frames the result — the abstract explicitly describes the goal as "disentangling" the intrinsic component from radiative pairs — but it's worth being honest that the entire concept of "intrinsic charm" is, at bottom, defined relative to a particular perturbative scheme and matching-condition calculation, not an experimentally direct, scheme-independent observable in the way that, say, a particle's mass is.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the neural-network global-fit machinery itself — the Monte Carlo replica method for propagating experimental uncertainties, exactly how the preprocessing exponents and network architecture in equation (1) constrain the fit without secretly reintroducing model bias, and precisely how the Bayesian reweighting procedure folds the new LHCb data back into the PDF ensemble. The paper compresses what is, in practice, a large and technically dense piece of NNPDF-collaboration-specific fitting software and methodology into a few paragraphs of Methods; I've translated the high-level logic faithfully, but I can't independently verify the numerics of the fit itself, and that's the part of this paper where the plain-language translation is most likely to have lost real technical nuance.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. After 40 years of disagreement, there's now solid — though not "discovery-grade" — evidence that charm quarks are genuinely built into the proton's own structure, not just radiation from a collision; when this intrinsic charm shows up, a single charm quark tends to carry a hefty chunk (roughly 40%) of the whole proton's momentum.

2. The breakthrough wasn't a new experiment — it was a bookkeeping trick: recompute what the light quarks alone can explain, subtract it from the data-fitted total, and whatever's left over is, by definition, the intrinsic signal, immune to the "you just found ordinary radiation" objection that sank many earlier claims.

3. Two completely unrelated pieces of evidence — a 1980s beam-dump measurement and a brand-new 2022 LHC forward-detector result — independently corroborate the same intrinsic-charm signal, which is a meaningfully stronger argument than any single high-precision measurement could make alone.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Since 1980, physicists have debated whether the proton's own structure includes charm quarks — particles heavier than the proton itself — baked in from the start ("intrinsic" charm), on top of the charm quarks that ordinary high-energy collisions are known to radiate into existence. The debate stalled for decades because nobody had a clean way to prove a measured charm signal was intrinsic rather than radiative: standard QCD guarantees some radiative charm at any sufficiently high energy, so finding "some charm" proves nothing on its own.

This paper resolves the question with a bookkeeping trick rather than a new experiment. Physicists fit the proton's charm content from a huge dataset (over 4,600 measurements) using a flexible, unbiased curve-fitting method (a neural network, so no theoretical shape is assumed in advance). They then convert that result into an alternative mathematical scheme in which charm isn't allowed to be generated by radiation at all. If there's truly no intrinsic charm, this conversion must return exactly zero. It doesn't: a real signal survives, shaped like a bump peaking where a single charm quark carries roughly 40% of the proton's momentum — matching decades-old theoretical predictions for what intrinsic charm should look like.

On its own, this result reaches about 2.5 standard deviations of confidence — solid, but short of the "discovery" bar particle physicists typically use. The authors strengthen it by checking two experiments the fit never used to build the result: brand-new LHC measurements sensitive to exactly the right region of the proton, and decades-old data that was historically the original (shaky) hint of intrinsic charm. Both independently point the same direction, pushing the combined confidence to roughly 3 standard deviations — the conventional threshold for "evidence," if not full discovery.

The result matters beyond settling an old argument: it affects how precisely physicists can predict processes at the Large Hadron Collider and future facilities, anywhere charm quarks in the proton's initial state matter. The main open question is theoretical precision at low momentum fractions, where the calculation's uncertainty is still large — and whether a fully independent research group, using its own separately-built fit, will reproduce the same signal.

**Q1 (user):** In the 3FNS/4FNS matching-condition mechanism, walk through why forcing intrinsic charm to zero forces the residual to cancel exactly.

**A1 (Claude):**

Two flavor schemes describe the same proton at different resolving scales: below the charm mass, only u, d, s, and the gluon are tracked as active radiative degrees of freedom (3FNS); above it, charm joins them as a fourth active parton (4FNS). A perturbative "matching matrix," computed order-by-order in α_s (Buza–Matiounine–Smith–van Neerven at NLO/NNLO, extended to N3LO by later work — the same machinery NNPDF runs through their EKO evolution code), translates PDFs across the threshold scale Q_h (here, the charm mass, ≈1.51 GeV):

f^(4)(Q₁²) = E^(4)(Q₁²←Q_h²) · A^(3)(Q_h²) · E^(3)(Q_h²←Q₀²) ⊗ f^(3)(Q₀²)

Within that matching matrix, an off-diagonal piece (A_Hg, and at higher order A_HΣ) generates charm purely out of gluon and light-quark content — the precise statement of "radiative charm." At leading order this piece has the closed form A_Hg^(1)(x, μ²/m²) = P_qg(x)·ln(μ²/m²), with P_qg(x) = T_F[x² + (1−x)²] — a textbook VFNS result (flagged (A) Consensus on the general shape, with the exact normalization constant held with somewhat lower confidence since it wasn't independently verified against the paper's own text). Because this LO term is purely proportional to ln(μ²/m²), it vanishes exactly at μ = m — the charm mass — which is the origin of the old "charm PDF starts at zero at its own threshold" convention used in standard zero-mass VFNS fits. Beyond LO, though, non-log terms appear (via A_Hq and NNLO corrections to A_Hg), so "no intrinsic charm" is really the stronger, all-scales claim that the *entire* 4FNS charm PDF, at every x and Q, equals a specific perturbatively-computable functional of the 3FNS gluon and singlet-quark PDFs — not merely "charm is zero at one point."

The paper doesn't assume this. It fits c^(4)(x, Q_h) freely from data (a neural network, no assumed shape), then checks whether that fitted object matches the matching-predicted one. Writing the "no intrinsic charm" hypothesis as an equation, c^(4)(x,Q_h) ≡ [A_Hg⊗g^(3) + A_HΣ⊗Σ^(3)](x,Q_h), and solving for what's left over via the (well-conditioned, invertible) diagonal A_HH entry:

c^(3)(x,Q_h) = A_HH⁻¹ ⊗ [ c^(4)_fit(x,Q_h) − A_Hg⊗g^(3)(x,Q_h) − A_HΣ⊗Σ^(3)(x,Q_h) ]

If the hypothesis holds exactly, the bracketed term is identically zero for every x — not approximately, but by the defining equation itself — and zero mapped through any linear operator stays zero. That's the forced cancellation: it isn't a numerical coincidence, it's structural, the same way subtracting a value from an identical copy of itself always nets zero regardless of what's done to it afterward. This mirrors the original analysis's "recompute and subtract" / memoization framing, now made mechanically precise. The companion methods paper (arXiv:2208.08372) states the outcome directly: "the 3FNS charm PDF is purely intrinsic... in the absence of intrinsic charm it would vanish."

In practice this is only exact up to two limits the paper itself flags: the matching coefficients (A_Hg, A_HΣ, A_HH) are known to finite perturbative order (NNLO, cross-checked at N3LO), and the fitted g^(3)/Σ^(3) carry their own fit uncertainty — so "should vanish" becomes "should be statistically consistent with zero," which is exactly the ~2.5σ departure the fit reports. Sources consulted: [arXiv:2208.08372](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.08372) (companion methods paper, eq. A.2 and Methods text on the inversion/vanishing statement), [arXiv:1510.02491](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.02491) (Ball, Bonvini, Rottoli — general VFNS/perturbative-charm formalism this builds on).

**A2 (Claude):**

Two "modes" describe the same proton depending on how hard you probe it. Below the charm quark's mass, only up, down, strange, and gluons are tracked — charm isn't part of the accounting yet. Above the charm mass, charm joins the accounting too, because collisions are now energetic enough to radiate charm quark–antiquark pairs into existence on the fly. These aren't two different physical realities — they're two bookkeeping conventions for the same proton, with a known, calculable recipe for translating between them (like converting a spreadsheet between currencies once you know the exchange rate).

The key piece of that recipe: starting from the 3-flavor picture (no charm in the accounting) and translating up to the 4-flavor picture, the recipe tells you exactly how much charm *should* appear, purely as a byproduct of gluons (and, more weakly, light quarks) radiating it into existence — pure, known QCD math, nothing fitted.

Normally physicists just trust that recipe completely: assume zero "native" charm, and treat whatever charm shows up in the 4-flavor picture as entirely explained by the radiation recipe. This paper doesn't assume that — it fits the proton's charm content as a completely free unknown, directly from collision data, then asks whether that freely-fitted charm matches what the pure-radiation recipe predicts, or whether something's left over.

To check, they run the translation backward: take the data-fitted charm, subtract off exactly what the radiation recipe says should be there, see what's left. If "no native charm" is correct, that subtraction has to come out to zero no matter how you slice it, because in that world the fitted charm and the recipe's predicted charm are, by definition, the same number — subtracting one from the other can only give zero. It's like knowing a spreadsheet cell is supposed to be pure formula output: if you re-derive the formula independently and it doesn't match the cell's actual value, the mismatch tells you something else fed into that cell besides the formula.

With real proton data, the subtraction doesn't come out to zero — a specific, non-random bump survives, concentrated where a single charm quark would carry a big share of the proton's momentum. That's the intrinsic charm signal: whatever the radiation recipe structurally cannot explain, left over after subtracting honestly.

Caveat: the "radiation recipe" itself is only known to finite precision (a perturbative calculation, computed to a certain order and no further), so in practice "should be zero" really means "should be statistically consistent with zero within known uncertainty" — which is why the result comes out as a confidence level (2.5–3σ) rather than a clean yes/no.
