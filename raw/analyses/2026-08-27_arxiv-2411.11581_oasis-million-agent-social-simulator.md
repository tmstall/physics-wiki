# 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**One Million Digital Citizens, No Ground Truth Required**

Build a social-media simulator that can scale from a few hundred LLM agents to a full million, and the same herd effects, polarization spirals, and information cascades that smaller experiments hinted at don't just persist — they visibly switch on somewhere around the 10,000-agent mark, as if the population itself needed to cross a threshold before the group started behaving like a group.

# 2. Big-Picture Context

This paper sits in a fast-forming subfield: replace the hand-coded, rule-based agents in a classical agent-based model (ABM) with LLM agents that can actually reason, converse, and act like plausible social-media users, then watch what a *population* of them does. The pitch is that individual-agent evaluation — the dominant AI-safety and capability-evaluation paradigm — can miss things that only show up when many agents interact: a single model answering a single prompt doesn't reveal how a crowd of copies of that model behaves when they can see, react to, and imitate each other. The problem the field has had until now is that every LLM-based ABM built to study one specific phenomenon (a particular platform, a particular social dynamic) has been a bespoke, small-scale build — typically tens to low thousands of agents — that's expensive to repurpose for a different question. Real social platforms, meanwhile, run at populations of tens or hundreds of millions.

OASIS is an infrastructure paper aimed squarely at that gap: a single, reusable simulator — not tied to one research question — that models X (Twitter)-like and Reddit-like platforms with dynamically updated social graphs, a real recommendation system (not just random exposure), and a scalable execution engine that has been run with up to one million concurrent LLM agents. The authors use it to replicate three canonical, decades-old computational social science findings — information propagation, group polarization, and herd effects — and then push further by asking how those phenomena change as the population scales from hundreds to hundreds of thousands of agents.

This paper is a direct methodological cousin of two other papers already in this pipeline: De Marzo, Castellano & Garcia's majority-following paper (analyzed 2026-08-18), which fits LLM-agent coordination onto a Curie-Weiss mean-field ferromagnet and extracts a single clean critical group size (>1000 agents) above which consensus locks in; and Ashery, Aiello & Baronchelli's naming-game paper (analyzed 2026-08-26), which runs small controlled populations (N=24–200) through a statistical-physics-inspired pairwise coordination game and measures a critical committed-minority fraction. Both of those papers get their answer by *fitting a parameter* to a deliberately small, controlled, well-instrumented population. OASIS is the opposite pole of the same genre: instead of a clean fitted threshold, it's a brute-force "build the biggest sandbox you can, throw agents at it, and see what happens" experiment — no mean-field theory, no critical exponent, just three discrete population sizes (196; 10,196; 100,196 agents, plus separate runs up to 1,000,000) and an empirical observation that group-level behavior visibly intensifies above roughly 10,000 agents. That's the same *shape* of finding — a population threshold beyond which qualitatively new group behavior appears — arrived at by an entirely different method: scale and observe, rather than fit and predict.

**Paper Type & Stakes.** This is a systems/infrastructure paper (a released, open-source simulator) combined with an empirical replication study; the stakes are less "does this overturn what we know about LLM agents" and more "can the field's small-N findings survive being pushed toward the population sizes real platforms actually operate at" — which matters directly for AI-safety evaluation of deployed multi-agent systems, and for the reader's own running interest in emergent group behavior in LLM populations.

**Prior Belief Check.** The finding is confirmatory rather than field-overturning: it doesn't introduce a new theoretical mechanism, and researchers in multi-agent LLM systems broadly expect *some* form of scale-dependence given the adjacent human-social-physics literature (critical mass, committed minorities, network contagion thresholds) already establishes population thresholds as a generic feature of social dynamics. What is somewhat more informative to specialists than it might look to a general audience: there was a real, live worry in this subfield that small-N LLM-agent findings (N≈200-2000, as in the two comparison papers above) simply wouldn't generalize to platform-scale populations — either washing out or behaving qualitatively differently once agents can no longer "see" the whole population. OASIS's headline observation — that the same class of phenomena not only survives but sharpens at scale — is reassuring/informative along that specific axis, even though it isn't conceptually surprising in the abstract.

**Replication & Convergence Note.** This is a single-collaboration result (CAMEL-AI plus partner institutions — Shanghai AI Laboratory, Oxford, KAUST, Dalian University of Technology, Fudan University, among others named in the author list) with no independent outside team having reproduced the specific quantitative claims (the ~10,000-agent threshold, the NRMSE figures against real-world diffusion data). It differs from a typical single-group result in one respect worth naming: the code is fully open-sourced (github.com/camel-ai/oasis) and has visible community adoption, which is a *usage-based* form of validation — bugs and obviously-wrong claims tend to surface faster in an actively-used open codebase — but this is not the same as an independent group re-running the scale-threshold experiment and getting the same number. Independent confirmation here would look like a separate lab running OASIS (or a comparable simulator) on the same three phenomena and reporting where they see the threshold appear.

# 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Agent-based models (ABMs).** An ABM is a simulation built from many independent, interacting units ("agents") following local rules, where the population-level pattern emerges from those local interactions rather than being programmed in directly — the classic example is Schelling's segregation model, where simple individual preferences ("I want at least 30% of my neighbors to share my type") produce large-scale segregation nobody explicitly designed. Classical ABMs use hand-coded rule-based agents (if-then logic). This paper's move — replacing rule-based agents with LLM agents that read context and generate free-text reasoning before acting — is what the whole "LLM-agent-based social simulation" subfield is built on.

**Analogy:** Think of a classical rule-based ABM agent like a simple thermostat — a fixed lookup table mapping input state to output action, no genuine reasoning. An LLM agent is closer to a junior employee handed a policy handbook (the system prompt/persona) and asked to use judgment on each new situation, producing an explanation for their choice along the way.

**Breaks when:** you push this to ask about consistency. A thermostat is perfectly reproducible — same input, same output, every time. An LLM agent's "judgment" varies run to run (sampling temperature, subtle prompt-context differences), and unlike a human employee, it has no persistent memory or accountability across the simulation beyond whatever the simulator explicitly re-feeds it — so "the agent decided X" is really "the agent decided X given exactly this context window," which is a narrower and more fragile claim than it sounds.

**Recommendation systems (RecSys).** On a real platform, you don't see every post — an algorithm decides what enters your feed, typically ranking by some mix of relevance (do you follow this person, does this match your interests) and popularity/recency. OASIS implements two: an interest-matching system for X-like feeds (using a pretrained text-similarity model, TwHIN-BERT, to score posts against an agent's stated interests) and a hot-score ranking for Reddit-like feeds (a formula combining vote margin and recency — worked in detail below).

**Analogy:** A recommendation system is functionally a caching/prefetch layer sitting between a firehose of all possible content and a user with limited attention — exactly the kind of "what do you evict, what do you keep hot" decision a systems engineer makes when sizing an LRU cache, just applied to attention instead of memory pages.

**Breaks when:** you push this to ask about the training signal. An LRU cache's eviction policy is a fixed, transparent rule you chose. A real platform's recommendation algorithm is typically an opaque model trained on billions of real behavioral signals (clicks, dwell time, A/B-tested engagement proxies) that nobody fully audits even internally — OASIS's RecSys is a deliberately simplified, transparent stand-in for that opaque real system, which is a reasonable engineering choice for building a controllable simulator, but means any OASIS finding that depends on *feed-curation* behavior specifically inherits the gap between "simplified, legible ranking rule" and "real platform's actual algorithm."

**Diffusion metrics — scale, depth, breadth.** When a post goes viral, researchers describe the resulting cascade with three numbers: *scale* (how many total people eventually participated), *depth* (the longest chain of "A reacted to B who reacted to C..." — how many hops deep the cascade goes), and *breadth* (the largest number of people participating simultaneously at any one hop). This paper compares simulated cascades against these same three curves computed from real social-media data.

**Analogy:** This is the same shape of accounting used for a distributed cache-invalidation storm or a viral outage-notification chain in a large service — how many nodes total got touched, how many hops did the notification travel through the dependency graph, and how many nodes were "hot" at the same instant.

**Breaks when:** you push this toward causal interpretation. A cache-invalidation chain has a known, deterministic dependency graph — you can trace exactly why node C got touched. A social cascade's "who saw what because of whom" is inferred from timestamps and follow-graphs, not directly observed causally, in both the real-world baseline data and the simulation — so a close scale/breadth match doesn't guarantee the simulator is getting the *mechanism* right, only the aggregate shape.

**Group polarization and herd effects — background from prior papers in this pipeline.** The reader has already built up working familiarity with adjacent concepts here: critical mass / committed-minority tipping dynamics (from the Ashery naming-game paper) and population-scale consensus thresholds via mean-field fitting (from the De Marzo majority-following paper). This paper's "herd effect" is the same broad family — agents' individual judgments get pulled toward the group's apparent judgment — but tested via a much more direct manipulation: seed a comment with an artificial initial upvote or downvote, then watch whether later agents pile on in the same direction more than a neutral (untreated) control comment would predict.

**Central analogy for this paper:** A game server built for a million LLM NPCs.

# 4. Core Technical Explanation

OASIS is architecturally a distributed system with four cooperating pieces, and the fact that the whole thing is describable in systems-engineering terms is not incidental — the paper's central engineering contribution *is* a scaling problem, not a modeling-novelty problem.

**Environment server.** A relational database holding the ground truth of the simulated world: users, posts, comments, follow relationships, action logs, and the current recommendation state. Every agent's "what do I see right now" query resolves against this single authoritative store — this is the piece the game-server analogy is built on: one backend, many concurrently-connected clients (agents), each pulling their own view of shared state.

**RecSys.** Sits between the environment server and each agent's feed, applying either the X-like interest-matching algorithm or the Reddit-like hot-score formula (below) to decide what that agent actually sees this round, out of everything that exists.

**Agent module.** Each agent is an LLM (the paper's large-scale runs use the open-source Llama3-8b-instruct, chosen specifically because it's cheap enough to run at a million-instance scale; GPT-4o-mini is used separately, as an *evaluator*, not as agent brains, for scoring polarization) equipped with a memory component and access to 21 distinct possible actions (follow, post, comment, like, repost, and so on — a notably larger action space than prior LLM-ABM work, which the paper's own comparison table caps at 4–6 actions). Agents also produce a short chain-of-thought explanation alongside each action, which is what lets the authors read off *why* an agent polarized or herded, not just *that* it did.

**Time engine.** Each agent carries a 24-dimensional vector of hourly activity probabilities (a rough proxy for "this user tends to be active around 9pm, not 4am") that governs when it gets activated to take its next action, rather than every agent acting in perfect lockstep every round.

**Scalable inferencer.** The piece that actually makes a million agents tractable: agents, the environment server, and the LLM inference backend run as independent, asynchronously-communicating processes, with agents allowed to fire off multiple concurrent inference requests rather than waiting in a strict queue, and GPU load balanced across whatever inference hardware is available. This is a classic horizontally-scaled service pattern — the interesting wrinkle, discussed further below, is that unlike a stateless web-server fleet, these "workers" aren't interchangeable and identical; each one is accumulating its own trajectory through the simulated social world.

**Worked equation — the Reddit hot-score formula.** Reddit's real ranking algorithm (which OASIS reimplements directly, not a paper-specific invention) is:

$$h = \log_{10}\!\big(\max(|u-d|,\,1)\big) \;+\; \frac{\mathrm{sign}(u-d)\cdot(t-t_0)}{45000}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$u$ : upvote count · $d$ : downvote count · $t$ : the current time (seconds) · $t_0$ : the post's creation time (seconds) · $\mathrm{sign}(u-d)$ : $+1$ if net votes are positive, $-1$ if negative, $0$ if tied · $45000$ : a tuning constant, in seconds (≈12.5 hours), fixing how fast the recency term accumulates.

**What this actually means:** This is functionally a priority-with-decaying-TTL scheme, exactly like a cache-eviction policy that boosts recently-touched entries. The log-compressed vote-margin term means going from a 1-vote to a 10-vote margin matters far more to the score than going from 100 to 1000 — diminishing returns on popularity, the same shape as a logarithmically-weighted hit counter. The time term then steadily erodes rank at a fixed rate of one score-point per 12.5 hours, so a brand-new post with a modest positive margin can and will outrank a much older, much more heavily upvoted post — the same "recency beats raw historical hit count" behavior an LRU-with-priority cache is built to produce. Concretely: a comment with $u-d=8$ that's one hour old scores $\log_{10}(8) + 3600/45000 \approx 0.90 + 0.08 = 0.98$; the same comment twenty-five hours later scores $0.90 - 90000/45000 = 0.90 - 2.0 = -1.10$ — it has fallen well behind a fresh comment with even a single net upvote ($\log_{10}(1)+0 = 0$).

**The three replicated phenomena, in brief:**

*Information propagation* (on the X-like platform): the paper compares simulated diffusion-curve shape (scale, depth, breadth over time) against real-world cascade data, reporting roughly 30% normalized RMSE on scale and breadth, but a materially worse match on depth — the simulator underestimates how many hops deep real cascades go, which the authors attribute to their simplified RecSys failing to correctly model "intermediary" users (people who see a post via the algorithm rather than via a direct follow, and who are disproportionately responsible for extending a cascade's depth in real platforms).

*Group polarization* (on the X-like platform): agents discuss a moral/social dilemma over 80 simulated time steps; opinions measurably drift from moderate toward extreme positions, and this drift is stronger when the underlying LLM is an "uncensored" (fewer safety-tuned) variant, which the authors characterize as adopting more absolutist language ("always better") over the course of the discussion.

*Herd effects* (on the Reddit-like platform): comments are seeded into three conditions — artificially up-treated (given an initial upvote), down-treated (given an initial downvote), and untreated control — across seven topics, then later agents' voting behavior is compared across conditions. The key asymmetry: for down-treated comments, later agents pile on with further downvotes more readily than a matched real-human baseline from the literature would be expected to; the up-treated (positive) direction shows a smaller or less pronounced gap. Framed the paper's way: LLM agents are more herd-prone specifically when the herd is being *negative*.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** the reader might assume "one million agents" means one million agents each carrying a rich, individually-authored persona and long private history, in the spirit of the 25-agent Smallville simulation this paper's own comparison table cites. The paper's actual approach at the million-agent scale draws on real (anonymized) user data combined with a network-generation model to seed a scale-free social graph, and runs the *same* underlying 8B-parameter model as the reasoning engine for essentially all agents at that scale — "one million agents" is best read as an engineering throughput achievement (the pipeline can execute a million concurrent LLM-driven decision loops) rather than a claim that a million meaningfully distinct synthetic personalities were created.

> **Watch:** the reader might assume the "agents herd more than humans" claim comes from a matched, same-paper controlled experiment — real human subjects and LLM agents run through the identical up/down-treatment protocol by these authors. From the retrieved text, the comparison instead leans on characterizing the *general* pattern in the human social-psychology literature (humans "deliberate more carefully," in the paper's own phrasing) against this paper's own agent results, rather than a fresh human control condition collected specifically for this study. This is worth verifying directly against the paper's methods section before treating it as a head-to-head RCT-style comparison.

> **Watch:** the reader — who has just spent two prior analyses (De Marzo, Ashery) building intuition for population thresholds as *fitted, mean-field-style critical points* — might assume the ~10,000-agent threshold here is a similarly derived quantity, with an identified order parameter and a fitted critical exponent. It isn't: the paper tests three discrete population sizes (196; 10,196; 100,196) and reports that group-opinion diversity/helpfulness visibly improves at the larger two relative to the smallest, which is an empirical observation across a handful of scale points, not a swept continuous parameter with a fitted transition point the way the Curie-Weiss framing in the De Marzo paper provides.

# 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Two things stand out, and they're genuinely different kinds of contribution.

First, **generality as the actual innovation.** Prior LLM-ABM work (the paper's own comparison table: Smallville at 25 agents in a fictional town, S3 at 1,000 agents on X with 4 actions, HiSim at 300–700 agents with 5 actions) is each purpose-built for one scenario. OASIS's claim to novelty isn't a new social-science finding — it's that the same codebase, with the same 21-action space, dynamic network, and real recommendation-system logic, can be pointed at X-like or Reddit-like platforms and reused across three unrelated phenomena (diffusion, polarization, herding) without a rebuild. That's an infrastructure contribution, and its value is mostly to *other researchers*, not to a specific empirical claim about the world.

Second, **the scale-dependent observation itself** — that qualitative group behavior (opinion diversity, "helpfulness," and by extension the sharpness of herd/polarization effects) visibly strengthens once the simulated population crosses roughly 10,000 agents, and this holds up to the full million-agent runs. To this reader specifically (already primed by the mean-field and critical-mass framings of the two comparison papers), this is "new to the field" mainly in the sense of being the first time this class of finding has been checked at genuine platform-adjacent scale rather than at N in the hundreds to low thousands — it's a scale-extension of an already-suspected phenomenon, not a new mechanism.

**Predictive Content Check**

1. **Falsifiable handle (always):** Yes, in two forms. The information-propagation comparison makes a checkable numerical claim — simulated scale/breadth/depth curves should match real-world cascade data to within the paper's own reported ~30% normalized RMSE (worse on depth specifically) — which another team could re-run against a different real-world dataset and either confirm or refute. Separately, the scale-threshold claim ("group behavior measurably sharpens somewhere around 10,000 agents") is directly reproducible given the open-sourced code: an independent group could run the same three population sizes (or intermediate ones) and check whether they see the same qualitative jump.

2. **Formalism load (conditional):** This check largely doesn't fire here in its usual "decorative math dressing up a claim" sense — the one piece of real mathematical structure in the paper, the Reddit hot-score formula, is not the authors' invented theory at all; it's a direct reimplementation of Reddit's actual public ranking algorithm, doing real engineering work (deciding what agents see) rather than being included to lend the paper theoretical weight. There's no heavy formalism elsewhere generating the paper's claims — the core results are empirical comparisons (RMSE against real data, vote-count comparisons across treatment conditions), so this half of the check is skipped as not applicable rather than judged and passed.

# 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> The million-agent and scale-threshold results are generated almost entirely with a single, relatively small open-source model (Llama3-8b-instruct), not a frontier-scale model (GPT-4-class, Claude-class) — whether the same threshold location or even the same qualitative pattern holds for more capable agents is untested at scale. **(A) Consensus** — this is an implicit but unavoidable consequence of the compute cost of running a million concurrent frontier-model calls, and the authors' own choice of model for the large-scale runs makes the tradeoff explicit. **(analyst inference, paper's stated methodology)**

> The simplified recommendation system underperforms specifically on cascade *depth*, which the authors attribute to an inability to correctly model intermediary users. **(A) Consensus** — explicitly acknowledged by the authors as the source of the depth-metric gap. **(paper, methodology/results discussion)**

> The "agents herd more than humans, especially on negative feedback" claim rests on comparing this paper's own agent results against a general characterization of human behavior from the wider literature, rather than a matched human control condition collected in this same study. **(C) Speculative** — this reader's assessment from the retrieved extraction; it's plausible the paper does include or cite a directly comparable human dataset, but that could not be confirmed from the material retrieved here, and the strength of the claim depends heavily on which it is. **(analyst inference — flagged for direct verification)**

> No independent, non-CAMEL-AI-affiliated team has yet reproduced the specific scale-threshold numbers (the 10,000-agent inflection) or the reported RMSE figures, despite the codebase being open and actively used. **(B) Contested** — reasonable observers could weigh the open-source community adoption as meaningfully de-risking this (bugs and obviously-wrong numbers tend to surface in a widely-used codebase) versus still treating it as unconfirmed until someone explicitly re-runs and reports the same experiment. **(analyst inference)**

> The three tested population sizes (196; 10,196; 100,196) are a sparse set of discrete points, not a systematic sweep — the specific location of "around 10,000" as the threshold is an interpolation between the smallest and middle points rather than a directly measured transition. **(B) Contested** — a specialist in agent-based modeling might reasonably want finer-grained scale sampling before treating "~10,000" as more than an order-of-magnitude estimate. **(analyst inference)**

# 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

OASIS is a generalizable, open-source LLM-agent social-media simulator built to solve two problems the authors see in prior work: existing LLM-based agent-based models are each built for one specific research question, and none of them operate at anything close to the population scale of real social platforms. The system separates concerns into an environment server (the authoritative record of users, posts, follows, and votes), a recommendation layer (X-like interest-matching via TwHIN-BERT, or Reddit-like hot-score ranking using the platform's real formula), an agent layer (LLM-driven, mostly Llama3-8b-instruct at scale, with 21 possible actions and chain-of-thought reasoning attached to each), a time-of-day activation model, and a distributed, asynchronous inference backend that lets the whole system run with up to one million concurrently-simulated agents.

Using this infrastructure, the authors replicate three well-established computational social science phenomena: information propagation (comparing simulated cascade scale/depth/breadth against real-world data, with roughly 30% error and a specific weakness in matching cascade depth), group polarization (opinions drift toward extremes over an 80-step discussion, more sharply for less safety-tuned model variants), and herd effects (artificially seeded up- or down-votes bias later agents' voting behavior, with a stronger effect in the negative direction than a human baseline would predict). Layered on top of all three, the authors report that pushing the simulated population from a few hundred agents up through 10,000 and 100,000 (and eventually a million) makes these group-level effects more pronounced — opinion diversity and "helpfulness" measurably improve at the larger scales, and by extension the herd/polarization dynamics visible at small N appear to sharpen rather than wash out as the crowd grows.

The interpretive choice worth flagging explicitly: this paper is being read here primarily as a *scale-extension* check on a phenomenon this pipeline has already encountered twice (critical mass in Ashery et al., mean-field consensus thresholds in De Marzo et al.), rather than as a standalone discovery. That framing is deliberate — it's what makes the paper's real contribution legible: not a new mechanism, but evidence that a mechanism already suspected at small N (population thresholds governing when qualitatively new group behavior appears) survives, and in some sense strengthens, when you actually build the infrastructure to test it at four to five orders of magnitude larger scale. The reader should take away that OASIS's main value is as a released, reusable tool — the specific numbers reported here (the ~10,000 threshold, the ~30% RMSE) are best treated as a first, single-team demonstration of what the tool can measure, not yet as settled facts about LLM-agent social dynamics in general.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the exact evidentiary basis for the "agents herd more than humans" comparison — whether it rests on a matched human control condition within this same paper, or on a looser comparison against the general social-psychology literature's characterization of human deliberation — could not be pinned down from the retrieved extraction, and this matters a fair amount for how strong a claim it actually is. The precise numeric details behind the three population-scale conditions (exactly how the 10,196- and 100,196-agent populations were constructed relative to the 196-agent baseline — whether they nest the same core sample or are drawn independently) are similarly underspecified in what was retrieved and would benefit from a direct read of the paper's methods section rather than this paraphrase-mediated summary.

# 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **Scale isn't just a bigger version of small — it's where the interesting behavior turns on.** OASIS finds that group-level social dynamics (polarization, herding, opinion diversity) measurably intensify somewhere around 10,000 simulated agents and keep intensifying up to a million, echoing a pattern this same reading pipeline has already seen in two smaller, more theoretically-clean papers about LLM-agent consensus and critical mass.

2. **LLM agents pile onto negativity more readily than people reportedly do.** When a comment gets an artificial early downvote, later LLM agents are more likely to keep downvoting it than a human-baseline comparison would predict — the herd effect this paper measures is specifically asymmetric, stronger on the negative side.

3. **The real contribution here is the tool, not a single number.** Unlike a bespoke small-scale simulation built to answer one question, OASIS is released as reusable, open-source infrastructure capable of running the same experiment across different platforms and population sizes — its lasting value is likely to be everything other researchers build with it next, not the specific 30%-RMSE or 10,000-agent figures reported in this first paper.

# 9. Shorter Summary

OASIS is an open-source simulator that lets researchers populate a fake version of X (Twitter) or Reddit with large-language-model "agents" instead of real people, and watch what happens. Unlike earlier tools of this kind — which typically max out at a few hundred to a couple thousand agents and are each built for one specific question — OASIS is designed to be reused across different research questions and can scale all the way up to a million simulated agents at once, using a real ranking algorithm (the same math Reddit itself uses to decide which posts you see) and a distributed computing setup that spreads the workload across many machines.

The authors use OASIS to reproduce three classic findings from social science: information spreads through the simulated network in patterns that roughly match real social-media data; agents' opinions drift toward more extreme positions the longer they discuss a divisive topic; and a comment that gets an early, artificial downvote attracts more piling-on downvotes from later agents than a neutral comment would — more so than a comparable pattern reported for real human behavior. The most notable finding layered on top of all three: these group effects get stronger, not weaker, as the simulated population grows, becoming clearly visible somewhere around ten thousand agents and continuing to intensify up to the full million-agent scale tested.

This is a preprint (not yet published in a peer-reviewed venue as of the version checked here), though its code is open-sourced and has seen real use by other researchers — a form of real-world scrutiny distinct from, but not equivalent to, formal peer review or independent scientific replication. The large-scale runs rely on a relatively small, non-frontier language model, so it's an open question whether more capable models would show the same threshold in the same place. The comparison to human herding behavior appears to draw on general findings from the social-psychology literature rather than a matched experiment run with real human participants in this same study — worth verifying directly before treating it as a strict head-to-head comparison.
