## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**One name, three different experiments: the HRV biofeedback literature can't be replicated as written**

Across 143 studies published over two decades, researchers have used "HRV biofeedback" for three genuinely different breathing protocols. About two-thirds of papers don't report enough to rebuild their protocol, so the field's efficacy meta-analyses have been pooling experiments nobody could rerun.

---

## 2. Big-Picture Context

**Paper Type & Stakes:** a pre-registered systematic review of *methodology and reporting quality*, not efficacy. It ends with an 8-point reporting guideline and checklist. What's at stake is whether the HRVB evidence base is interpretable and replicable, not whether HRVB works.

**The intervention, built from zero (tier 3: psychophysiology).** Heart rate isn't constant. It speeds up slightly on each inhale and slows on each exhale; this is respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA). Separately, the baroreflex tries to hold blood pressure steady: when pressure rises, it slows the heart, and the correction arrives with a lag of a few seconds. Breathe at the right slow rate, typically near 6 breaths per minute (b/m), and the breathing-driven heart-rate swing lines up in phase with the baroreflex's lagged correction. The two then add, and the heart-rate oscillation grows much larger. That breathing rate is the person's **resonance frequency (RF)**. HRVB means breathing at RF, usually with real-time feedback on the heart-rate swing, repeated over weeks. Earlier meta-analyses report small-to-moderate benefits for anxiety, stress, depression, some cardiovascular outcomes and performance (e.g., Goessl, Curtiss & Hofmann 2017; Lehrer et al. 2020).

> **Worked numbers — what "resonance frequency" means as a clock**
>
> Breathing period $T$ from rate $f$ (b/m):
>
> $$T = \frac{60}{f}$$
>
> **Symbol definitions:**
> $T$ : duration of one full breath (seconds)
> $f$ : breathing rate (breaths per minute)
>
> - 6 b/m → $T$ = 10 s → 0.1 Hz. A 4:6 inhale:exhale ratio at 6 b/m means 4 s in, 6 s out.
> - The classic **stepped protocol** for finding RF tries 6.5, 6.0, 5.5, 5.0, 4.5 b/m for 2 min each, which is 10 min of paced breathing. It picks the rate with the largest heart-rate swing.
> - The newer **sliding protocol** (Fisher & Lehrer 2022) sweeps continuously over 78 breaths from 6.75 to 4.25 b/m, lengthening each breath by 67.04 ms. Check: 60/4.25 − 60/6.75 = 14.118 − 8.889 = 5.229 s, and 5.229 s ÷ 78 = 67.04 ms. ✓
>
> **What this actually means:** the baroreflex acts like a control loop with a fixed sensing-and-actuation latency. Think of a DVFS governor that reacts to a thermal reading ~5 s late. Drive its load with a 10 s period and each correction lands exactly as the next swing starts, so the oscillation amplifies. Drive it at 4 s or 20 s and the correction partly cancels the swing. RF is simply the drive period that matches the loop's latency.
>
> **Breaks when:** the baroreflex is not one fixed delay. RF differs between people (it correlates with body size and blood volume), and whether it drifts within a person across sessions is contested. That drift question is exactly the one this paper flags.

**What the review did.** The authors searched PsycINFO, CINAHL, MEDLINE and Web of Science for 2000–April 2021 (English, Spanish, Catalan). They included 143 experimental or quasi-experimental studies of active HRVB with no competing simultaneous intervention, then coded every study's protocol and reporting. Inter-rater agreement on selection was Cohen's κ = 0.72. Risk of bias was assessed with the Cochrane tool for experiments and ROBINS-I for quasi-experiments. The outcome-related bias domains were deliberately dropped, because the review is about method, not results.

**What it found.** 145 interventions sort into three protocols plus a residual group:

| Protocol | n | Definition | Typical dose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Optimal RF** | 37 | RF detected first (stepped protocol), then each person breathes at their own RF | 2–8 weeks, ~1 lab session/week, 10–20 min, plus daily home practice |
| **Individual** | 48 | A device computes the optimal rate from live cardiovascular data; the person follows it | Similar to Optimal RF |
| **Preset-pace** | 51 | Everyone breathes at the same fixed rate, usually 6 b/m | Mostly a single acute session; home practice rare |
| **Referenced** | 9 | Too little detail to classify; the paper just cites a prior protocol | — |

The headline is a reporting audit:

- ~2/3 of studies omit enough of the following to block replication: breathing duration, inhale:exhale ratio, breathing-rate control, or body position.
- Only **19 of 88** studies (22%) checked whether participants actually breathed at the target rate.
- Only **27** (~19%) reported the inhale:exhale ratio.
- Only **20 of 85** Optimal RF + Individual studies (24%) reported the sample's mean RF.
- Only **6** studies rechecked RF every session; most measured it once at baseline.
- Of Optimal RF studies, 84% reported the tested rate range but only 70% reported the minutes per step.
- Body position was reported by fewer than half; room conditions by 27%; time of day by 21%; pre-session instructions (caffeine, sleep, smoking) by 30%.
- Designs: 75% experimental, 25% quasi-experimental. 19 studies had both active and passive controls; 21 had no control group.
- Samples skew young: ~60% early adulthood (18–39) and 26% university students.

Where studies did monitor breathing, the data show why it matters. One Optimal RF study found participants breathing ~8.5 b/m at the start of training, reaching the target ~6.3 b/m only by the end (Sutarto et al. 2012). Some preset-pace studies reported achieved rates different from the protocol.

**Prior Belief Check:** this result aligns with, rather than contradicts, the field's direction. Efficacy meta-analyses had already noted large protocol heterogeneity, and general HRV reporting guidelines already exist (e.g., the GRAPH guidelines, Quintana et al. 2016, and Laborde et al. 2017). None is specific to the biofeedback *intervention* itself. The paper's specific contribution is quantifying the gap and supplying an HRVB-specific checklist. That is confirmatory and incremental, and specialists would not be surprised. The three-way taxonomy is useful but mostly names distinctions practitioners already make informally.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** this is a single group's coding of the literature. No independent team has re-coded this corpus to check the reporting percentages, and the only reliability statistic given is for study *selection* (κ = 0.72), not for extracting individual protocol variables. Independent confirmation would mean a second team re-extracting a random sample of the 143 studies and reporting agreement per variable. That matters because every headline percentage is a coding judgment ("did this paper report X?").

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

1. **An operational taxonomy that separates the dose variable from the label.** Splitting "HRVB" into Optimal RF / Individual / Preset-pace isolates the one parameter that defines the intervention: whether the breathing rate is personalized, and how. It lets future meta-analyses stratify by protocol instead of pooling. New to the field as a formal, counted classification, even though the distinctions themselves were already known to practitioners.
2. **A checklist aimed at the intervention, not the measurement.** Existing HRV guidelines cover recording and analyzing HRV. This one covers the *delivery* variables: RF detection procedure and how often RF is rechecked, mean RF, achieved-rate monitoring, inhale/hold/exhale durations, actual breathing minutes per session separated from other tasks, home-practice verification, and the device (brand, pacer type, feedback type, validation status). The call to report *achieved* rather than instructed breathing rate matters most: it's the manipulation check the field mostly skips.

The discussion also usefully points to two measurement confounds that the checklist then asks authors to report. First, photoplethysmography (optical, wrist/finger) is less accurate than ECG for beat-to-beat timing during paced breathing (Jan et al. 2019). Second, two direct comparisons of inhale:exhale ratio reached opposite conclusions. Lin et al. 2014 favored equal ratios for maximizing HRV; van Diest et al. 2014 found longer exhalation raised high-frequency HRV and perceived relaxation. So a parameter most papers don't report has unresolved effects.

**Predictive Content Check**

1. **Falsifiable handle:** there's no prediction about physiology or outcomes. The review deliberately makes no efficacy claim. Its empirical content is descriptive (reporting frequencies), and it can be checked by re-coding the studies, not by experiment. The nearest thing to a testable claim is implicit: that the missing variables (achieved rate, inhale:exhale ratio, RF recheck frequency, protocol type) moderate outcomes. That would be falsified if a stratified meta-analysis found effect sizes indistinguishable across Optimal RF, Individual and Preset-pace, and unrelated to monitored breathing compliance. The paper doesn't run that test. So as it stands, **it audits and prescribes rather than predicts**, which is appropriate for its genre but should be stated plainly.
2. **Formalism load:** not triggered. There is no mathematical structure.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

1. **No efficacy comparison: the review can't say which protocol works best.** **(A) Consensus** — the authors name this their main limitation and say it was outside scope; any reader wanting a protocol recommendation leaves without one. **(paper, Limitations)**

2. **RF measurement and HRV analysis methods were not examined.** Protocols also differ in what "maximal" means when picking RF: low-frequency spectral power, peak-to-trough heart-rate amplitude, or heart-rate/breathing phase alignment. They also differ in how HRV outcomes are computed. So two "Optimal RF" studies can still differ in the variable that defines the intervention. **(A) Consensus** — the authors explicitly list this as a second limitation, and differences in RF criteria are recognized in the methods literature. **(paper, Limitations)**

3. **The screening numbers don't reconcile.** The text says 262 records were selected, 19 were excluded (8 no full text, 11 language) and 20 were added from reference lists, yet it says 282 went to full-text review. 262 − 19 + 20 = 263, and 262 + 20 = 282 only if the 19 exclusions are ignored. The text doesn't give reasons for excluding the remaining 139 of 282 at full text (they may be in Fig. 1, which I did not see rendered). **(C) Speculative** — the arithmetic is checkable fact, but whether it reflects a typo or a real accounting gap is my judgment. It likely doesn't change any conclusion, but in a reporting-quality paper it's worth a correction. **(analyst inference)**

4. **The literature search closed in April 2021, before the wearable/mHealth wave.** Consumer HRV apps and optical-sensor biofeedback have multiplied since, which may shift the protocol mix toward "Individual"-type device guidance and make the PPG-accuracy concern more pressing. Language restriction (English/Spanish/Catalan) also excluded 11 otherwise-eligible studies. **(A) Consensus** — search-window staleness is a standard, uncontroversial caveat for systematic reviews; the size of the post-2021 shift is not quantified. **(broader literature)**

5. **The "Individual" category hides device heterogeneity.** It groups any device that calculates a target rate in real time, but those devices' algorithms (what they maximize, over what window) differ and are often proprietary. So "Individual" may be as internally heterogeneous as the undifferentiated label the taxonomy was meant to fix. The checklist asks for device brand and feedback type, which helps but doesn't expose the algorithm. **(C) Speculative** — the paper does not analyze within-category device differences; this is my extrapolation. **(analyst inference)**

6. **The checklist is proposed, not validated.** I saw no formal consensus process (e.g., Delphi panel) described in what I retrieved. There's also no evidence yet that adopting the checklist improves replication or reduces between-study variance. Its value is plausible by analogy with CONSORT/PRISMA-style reporting standards, but untested. **(C) Speculative** — I may have missed a development-process description in sections the fetch tool summarized. **(analyst inference)**

7. **Dropping outcome-related risk-of-bias domains limits what the bias results mean.** The bias results describe design quality (randomization, confounding, deviations) but not selective outcome reporting, which is the domain most tied to inflated efficacy. **(B) Contested** — reviewers could reasonably defend the choice for a methods-focused review or argue it discards the domain most relevant to the evidence base's credibility. **(paper, Methods)**

8. **The RF-stability question is flagged but unresolved.** Lin et al. 2012 saw mean RF shift from 6 to 5 b/m across sessions in young prehypertensive students. Hallman et al. 2011 found it stable over ten sessions in ~40-year-olds. Fisher & Lehrer 2022 found high stability with the sliding method. The authors recommend following a published stepped or sliding protocol and rechecking RF when time allows. That recommendation hedges between the two findings rather than settling them, and it's the open empirical question most likely to matter for Optimal RF protocols. **(B) Contested** — the primary studies disagree, and the paper states the evidence is conflicting. **(paper, Discussion)**

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **"HRV biofeedback" is three different treatments under one name.** You can breathe at your own measured resonance rate, follow a device that sets the rate live, or just breathe at 6 per minute like everyone else. The literature has mostly pooled them.
2. **Most studies never checked whether people actually breathed at the target rate.** Only about 1 in 5 monitored it, and when someone did look, participants started closer to 8.5 than 6 breaths per minute. The manipulation check is largely missing.
3. **This paper is a building inspection, not a verdict.** It says two-thirds of the HRVB evidence can't be rebuilt from its own blueprints and supplies a checklist. It says nothing about which protocol works best, and nobody has yet shown the checklist fixes replication.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Heart rate variability biofeedback (HRVB) trains people to breathe slowly, usually around six breaths per minute. At that pace, the breathing-driven rise and fall of heart rate lines up with the body's blood-pressure reflex, and the heart-rate swings grow large. The best pace differs from person to person and is called the resonance frequency. Earlier reviews found modest benefits for anxiety, stress and related outcomes.

Lalanza and colleagues asked a different question: not whether HRVB works, but whether published studies describe it well enough for anyone to repeat them. They reviewed 143 studies from 2000 to 2021 and found three distinct methods hiding under one name. Some studies first measure each person's resonance frequency and train at it. Some use a device that computes the best pace in real time. Some simply have everyone breathe at a fixed six breaths per minute, often in a single session.

Reporting is thin. About two-thirds of studies leave out details needed to rebuild the protocol, such as breathing duration, inhale-to-exhale timing, body position, or whether breathing was monitored. Only about one in five checked that participants actually hit the target pace. In one study that did check, people started near eight and a half breaths per minute and only reached the target by the end of training. Few studies rechecked the resonance frequency after baseline, even though evidence on whether it drifts is mixed.

The authors propose an eight-point checklist: pre-registration, sample details, allocation, missing data, the exact breathing protocol, session counts and minutes, room and home conditions, and equipment specifications.

The review's limits are clear. It doesn't compare how well the three methods work, doesn't examine how resonance frequency is measured, and its screening numbers don't quite add up. The checklist sensibly targets the right variables, but it hasn't yet been shown to improve replication.
