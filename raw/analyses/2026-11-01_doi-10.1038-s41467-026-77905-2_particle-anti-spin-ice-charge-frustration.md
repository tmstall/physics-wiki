# When Ice Breaks Its Own Rule: Attractive Colloids Pile Up Charge — Until Odd Polygons Say No

**Hook:** Flip the sign of the interaction in a colloidal "spin ice" from push to pull, and the century-old ice rule doesn't just weaken — it turns inside out, producing a perfect charge crystal on simple lattices and a brand-new kind of frustration on a lattice of pentagons and heptagons.

---

## 1. Title and Hook

**Emergent charge crystallization and frustration in a particle anti-spin ice** — Baillou, Terkel, Nisoli & Tierno (University of Barcelona / Los Alamos), *Nature Communications* 17, 11152 (2026), DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-77905-2.

Every artificial spin ice built so far has been governed by the same compromise: each junction of the lattice tries to push its neighbours away, the whole lattice can't satisfy everyone, and the system settles into a balanced, disordered "ice" state. This paper builds the first version where the particles *attract* each other — and shows that the result is not simply the mirror image of ordinary ice.

---

## 2. Big-Picture Context

**Paper type and stakes:** this is a combined simulation-and-experiment paper in soft-matter physics that inverts the defining interaction of a well-studied model system (colloidal artificial ice) and asks whether frustration survives the inversion. The stakes are conceptual: frustration has almost always been described as the failure to *minimize* something locally; this paper argues it can equally be the failure to *maximize* something globally.

The ice rule — every junction as close to balanced as possible — is the organizing principle of water ice, of spin-ice magnets, and of the artificial versions built since 2006 (Section 3 tells that story). Artificial spin ices are built by design: arrays of nanomagnets drawn with lithography, and later many other platforms — superconducting vortices, quantum annealer qubits, liquid crystals, mechanical metamaterials. One of the cleanest is the **particle (colloidal) ice**: microscopic magnetic beads, one per elongated trap, with each trap running along an edge of a lattice. A bead can sit at either end of its trap, so each trap is a two-state "spin." Until now, every particle ice used *repulsive* beads. Repulsion makes each junction try to push beads away, and the best collective compromise is again the ice rule.

What was missing was the other sign. An attractive version had been sketched only as an abstract network model of wealth inequality, never on a regular lattice in a real physical system. The reason is practical: a static magnetic field can make beads attract, but only along one direction — side by side they repel — so a static field produces a messy, direction-dependent interaction. This paper's enabling trick is a rapidly spinning in-plane field that averages that messy interaction into a clean, direction-independent pull.

**Prior Belief Check:** the paper *complicates*, rather than contradicts, the consensus. Nobody expected attraction to obey the ice rule, and the staggered charge crystal on simple lattices is the outcome an expert would guess after a moment's thought (attractive beads clump at alternating junctions). Two parts are genuinely informative to specialists. First, the honeycomb lattice, which is famously *unable* to satisfy the ice rule everywhere in the repulsive case, becomes perfectly orderable under attraction — the sign flip removes, rather than mirrors, its frustration. Second, the pentaheptite lattice produces a degenerate low-energy manifold through a mechanism the authors call topological charge frustration, mapped to a dimer model. That second result is the real novelty; it is a clean conceptual extension of "vertex frustration" ideas that Nisoli and collaborators developed for magnetic artificial ices, rather than an overturning of anything.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** this is a single-group result (the Tierno lab, with Nisoli as theorist, who are also the principal developers of colloidal ice), with no independent confirmation; moreover, the square and honeycomb results are **simulation-only** in this paper — only the pentaheptite lattice was realized experimentally. Independent confirmation would look like a second group (for instance one using optical traps, or the Libál/Reichhardt simulation lineage) reproducing the pentaheptite low-field-to-high-field charge inversion and the plateau of persistent ice-rule junctions, and an experimental square or honeycomb anti-spin ice showing the full staggered crystal.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

### What is a "topological charge" at a junction?

Picture one junction (a **vertex**) of a lattice where several traps meet. Each trap holds one bead, which sits either at the end touching this vertex ("in") or at the far end ("out"). Count how many beads point in, compare with how many point out, and you have the vertex's **topological charge**: zero when they balance, positive when more point in, negative when more point out. The name comes from spin ice, where an unbalanced vertex acts like a source or sink of magnetic field lines.

One small example: on the square lattice, four traps meet at each vertex. Two in and two out is charge zero; three in and one out is a modest positive charge; all four in is the maximum positive charge. The paper divides by the number of traps at the vertex so that "all in" is always plus one and "all out" is minus one, on any lattice.

There is a conservation law hiding here, and it matters later. Each bead is "in" for one vertex and simultaneously "out" for the vertex at the other end of its trap. So every bead adds one unit to one vertex and removes one from its neighbour, and the total charge across the whole lattice is always zero: charges come in matched pairs.

> **Analogy:** each trap is a single job that must be scheduled on exactly one of the two servers at its ends; a vertex's charge is how overloaded or underloaded that server is relative to an even split.

> **Breaks when:** a scheduler can choose freely, while beads move by thermal jiggling and local forces only; and real charge carries an energy that depends on geometry (angles between traps), not just on a count of jobs.

### What is the ice rule, and why does repulsion produce it?

The **ice rule** is the low-energy rule for repulsive beads: make every vertex as close to neutral as possible. Repulsive beads crowded at the same vertex cost energy, so each vertex wants as few "in" beads as possible — but every bead pushed out of one vertex lands in the next one. The best global compromise is to spread beads evenly: on the square lattice, two in and two out everywhere.

When the number of traps at a vertex is odd (three on the honeycomb), perfect neutrality is impossible — two-in-one-out or one-in-two-out is the best any vertex can do — so every honeycomb vertex carries a small leftover charge of one-third. That is why the repulsive honeycomb ice is "frustrated" in a more complicated way and shows several intermediate ordered phases.

> **Analogy:** repulsion is load-balancing — the cost of a server grows faster than linearly with its load, so the scheduler spreads jobs evenly.

> **Breaks when:** load balancing in a data centre has a global controller that can find the even split; the ice has no controller, and on lattices where an exactly even split is impossible (odd numbers of traps per vertex), the leftovers must go somewhere, which is where the interesting physics starts.

### Turning push into pull with a spinning field

Each bead is a polymer sphere stuffed with tiny iron-oxide grains. It has no magnetic moment of its own, but an applied field induces one, pointing along the field. Two such induced magnets interact in a direction-dependent way. Lined up nose-to-tail along the field, they attract; standing side by side across the field, they repel; and the nose-to-tail attraction is twice as strong as the side-by-side repulsion.

The trick is to spin the field in the plane of the sample, fifty times a second. Two beads now see the field sweep through every orientation relative to the line joining them: sometimes nose-to-tail (strong pull), sometimes side by side (weaker push). Averaged over a turn, the pull wins: the net attraction is a quarter of the full nose-to-tail pull, or equivalently half the size of the repulsion that a vertical field of the same strength would produce, with the opposite sign. The beads cannot follow the field fast enough to care about its instantaneous direction, so all they feel is a clean, direction-independent attraction.

For a chemist there is a precise echo here: **magic-angle spinning** in solid-state NMR. The direction-dependent part of a dipole–dipole coupling carries the same angular factor that vanishes at the "magic angle" of about 55 degrees, which is why spinning a sample at that angle wipes out dipolar broadening. Here the field spins in the plane (90 degrees from the vertical), so the average does not vanish — it lands on the attractive side. The paper's outlook uses exactly this: tilt the spinning field into a cone, and at the magic angle the averaged interaction switches off, with repulsion on one side and attraction on the other.

> **Analogy:** a tight polling loop that samples a fluctuating signal far faster than the consumer can react; the consumer sees only the running average, and if the signal spends more "weight" positive than negative, the average is positive.

> **Breaks when:** the spinning field also makes each bead physically rotate (its magnetization lags the field), and near a wall a spinning sphere rolls. That side effect is real and, as Section 4 shows, forced the experiments down to a much slower spin rate than the simulations.

### Bipartite lattices and odd loops

A lattice is **bipartite** if you can colour its vertices with two colours so that every trap connects one colour to the other. Square and honeycomb lattices are bipartite. Any lattice containing a loop with an odd number of edges — a triangle, a pentagon, a heptagon — is not: walk around an odd loop alternating colours and you return to your starting vertex with the wrong colour. This is a tier-1 idea for a CS reader: it is the same parity argument that makes graph two-colouring easy (a breadth-first search either finds a valid colouring or finds an odd cycle).

Why it matters here: if attraction pushes every vertex toward maximal charge, then every trap must join an "all-in" vertex to an "all-out" vertex — a bead fully "in" for one end is fully "out" for the other. That is exactly a two-colouring of the lattice. Bipartite lattices allow it; odd loops forbid it.

### Frustration, degeneracy, and dimers

**Frustration** means local preferences cannot all be satisfied at once. It often brings **degeneracy**: many different arrangements tie for the lowest energy, so the system has no single ground state and keeps residual disorder, like water ice. A **dimer model** is a classic combinatorial picture of such degeneracy: place "dimers" (short bars) on the edges of a graph so that every node is touched by exactly one bar. Counting the ways to do this is a well-studied problem — in CS terms it is counting perfect matchings of a graph — and the number grows exponentially with system size on most two-dimensional graphs, which is the hallmark of residual entropy. The paper maps its frustrated state onto exactly this kind of matching problem.

### Origins & Big Picture

The system studied here sits at the end of a ninety-year chain of ideas about local rules that cannot all be satisfied.

It begins with water ice. In 1933 Bernal and Fowler proposed that each water molecule in ice keeps its own two hydrogens close and shares the other two hydrogen bonds with neighbours, so each oxygen has two hydrogens near and two far. Pauling (1935) counted how many hydrogen arrangements satisfy that rule everywhere and got an exponentially large number, which predicted a residual entropy of ice that matched the calorimetric "missing entropy" Giauque and Stout measured in 1936. That is the original example of frustration producing degeneracy: a rule that is easy to satisfy locally but satisfied in astronomically many ways globally.

Sixty years later, the same mathematics appeared in magnets. In rare-earth pyrochlore oxides such as holmium titanate, magnetic moments sit on the corners of tetrahedra and are forced to point either into or out of each tetrahedron. Their interactions make the low-energy state two-in, two-out — the ice rule again — and a vertex that violates it carries a net magnetic charge that can wander through the crystal like an isolated magnetic pole. These "monopoles" became a major theme of the 2000s.

Natural magnets offer only the lattices chemistry provides, so in 2006 Schiffer's group drew a square array of elongated nanomagnets with lithography: **artificial spin ice**. Designers could now choose the lattice. That freedom revealed a second idea central to this paper: on some engineered lattices (mixed numbers of islands per junction), frustration comes not from any single junction but from loops of junctions whose preferred states cannot be stitched together — Nisoli and collaborators named this **vertex frustration**.

Also in 2006, Libál and the Reichhardts proposed building the same physics from colloids in optical double-well traps; Tierno's group realized it with magnetic beads in lithographically grooved substrates in 2016. Colloidal ice has two advantages: you can watch every bead move in real time under a microscope, and you can tune the interaction strength with a knob (the field) instead of re-fabricating the sample. It also has a subtlety: because the beads interact isotropically rather than as tiny bar magnets, the colloidal ice rule arises from charges "dumping" onto neighbouring vertices, and on mixed-coordination lattices it can behave differently from magnetic artificial ice.

This paper takes the colloidal platform's one missing degree of freedom — the *sign* of the interaction — and flips it. The physics question becomes: if every vertex now wants to be maximally charged instead of neutral, which lattices can satisfy everyone, and what happens on the ones that cannot? The answer connects back to the beginning of the story. On bipartite lattices, everyone can be satisfied, so there is no frustration and no residual entropy — a clean charge crystal, the colloidal analogue of rock salt. On lattices with odd loops, a global constraint (two-colourability) fails, and on the pentaheptite lattice specifically, the failure leaves many tied arrangements, reviving Pauling-style degeneracy through a new door.

The pentaheptite lattice itself has a materials pedigree: it was proposed in 1996 by Crespi, Cohen, Louie and co-workers as a hypothetical all-carbon sheet made of five- and seven-membered rings (a cousin of graphene in which each hexagon pair is rearranged into a pentagon–heptagon pair), and pentagon–heptagon defects appear in real graphene, nanotubes and nanoribbons. It has also been studied theoretically as a host for exotic quantum spin liquids. This paper is its first appearance in a spin-ice setting.

> **Analogy:** the progression from water ice to this paper is like the progression from one hand-tuned server to a configurable cluster where you can also flip the scheduler's objective from "spread the load" to "consolidate it."

> **Breaks when:** you treat the lineage as one continuous technology; the physical systems (protons, atomic magnetic moments, nanomagnets, colloids) differ in every microscopic detail and share only the counting rules at the vertices.

**Central analogy for this paper:** consolidating jobs onto alternating servers.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### Step 1 — Making a clean attraction

**The failure it prevents:** with a static in-plane field, two beads attract or repel depending on how the line between them is oriented relative to the field, and thermal jiggling keeps changing that orientation, so the interaction can flip sign and the lattice never settles.

**What the authors do:** spin the field in the plane at a fixed rate and let the beads feel only the average over a turn.

**What it buys:** a direction-independent pull that falls off with the cube of distance, a quarter as strong as the static nose-to-tail attraction (and half as strong as the familiar vertical-field repulsion). This is the single equation the whole paper rests on:

$$\langle U \rangle = -\frac{\mu_0\, m^2}{8\pi r^3}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\langle U \rangle$ : the interaction energy between two beads, averaged over one turn of the field (joules)
- $\mu_0$ : the magnetic permeability of the surrounding water (essentially that of empty space)
- $m$ : the magnetic moment the field induces in each bead (grows in proportion to the field strength)
- $r$ : the centre-to-centre distance between the beads

**What this actually means:** the minus sign is the whole story — beads now pull together, with a strength that scales as the square of the field (so doubling the field quadruples the pull) and drops eightfold when the distance doubles. In computer terms, it is the steady-state average of a fast-oscillating input that spends more weight on one side; the beads are the slow consumer that sees only that average.

To test it, the authors film pairs of free beads falling together (Figure 1c). The averaged pull predicts a slow start and a sharp acceleration just before contact; rescaled by field strength and diffusion time, all runs collapse onto one predicted straight line.

![Figure 1](figures/baillou-77905_fig1.jpg)

*Figure 1 (from the paper): (a) beads in a square lattice of elongated traps; (b) how the field direction sets the interaction — vertical field repels (blue), static in-plane field gives a mixed pattern, spinning in-plane field attracts in all directions (red); (c) pairs of free beads approaching under a spinning field at three strengths, raw (top) and rescaled (bottom).*

**What to look at:** in panel (b), compare the three force maps — the rightmost is uniformly red (attraction all around) except at contact. In panel (c), the bottom plot is the test: data for three field strengths fall on one straight line through the origin, which is what the averaged attraction predicts.

One useful number from this step: a field of about a tenth of a millitesla is enough for the magnetic pull to beat random thermal motion for two touching beads. The lattice experiments run at tens of times that, so attraction dominates comfortably at high field.

### Step 2 — Redesigning the trap

**The failure it prevents:** earlier repulsive colloidal ices used a small bump in the middle of each trap, because repulsion squeezed beads toward the trap centre at high field and destroyed the two-state "spin."

**What the authors do:** remove the bump. Each trap is a smooth elongated well about two bead-diameters long.

**What it buys:** with attraction, the trap centre is unstable — a bead is always pulled toward whichever end has more company — so the two-state behaviour comes for free. This is a small design point, but it signals how differently the attractive system behaves: the potential landscape that repulsion needed is now redundant.

### Step 3 — Annealing by slowly turning up the pull

**The failure it prevents:** if the attraction is switched on suddenly, beads lock into whatever local clumps form first, leaving the lattice full of mismatched domains.

**What the authors do:** start from random bead positions and ramp the field slowly from zero to its maximum over many minutes (simulations) or about ten minutes (experiments), so the system can reorganize while the attraction is still weak enough for thermal motion to undo mistakes.

**What it buys:** a good approximation to the lowest-energy state, with a few leftover domain walls ("grain boundaries") that the authors argue would vanish with an infinitely slow ramp — simulated annealing, except the temperature stays fixed and the coupling rises instead.

### Step 4 — Square and honeycomb: a perfect charge crystal

**The failure it prevents (conceptually):** one might guess that flipping the sign of the interaction simply mirrors the repulsive case — ordered where ordinary ice is ordered, disordered where it is disordered.

**What the authors find (simulation only):** on both the square and the honeycomb lattice, rising attraction drives the system into a single ordered state: alternating vertices with every bead "in" (maximal positive charge) and every bead "out" (maximal negative charge). In plain terms, beads clump in groups of four (square) or three (honeycomb) at every other vertex, leaving the vertices in between empty.

**Why it happens:** the energy of a vertex with attractive beads gets lower faster than linearly as more beads crowd in — a cluster of four contains six close pairs, a cluster of two only one. That makes "all or nothing" cheaper than "half and half," the exact opposite of repulsion. On a bipartite lattice, the "all or nothing" pattern can be realized everywhere at once by giving every bead to the same colour of vertex. In the central analogy: on a bipartite network, you can consolidate every job onto one set of alternating servers and power the others down completely.

![Figure 2](figures/baillou-77905_fig2.jpg)

*Figure 2 (from the paper): numerical results for the square (left column) and honeycomb (right column) anti-spin ice. (a, b) Snapshots of the final state, red and blue marking maximal positive and negative charge; (c, d) fraction of each vertex type as the field rises; (e, g) charge–charge correlation between neighbours; (f, h) average plaquette chirality.*

**What to look at:** in (c) and (d), the solid red and blue curves (maximally charged vertices) rise to about one half each, while the dashed curves (low-charge vertices) drop to zero above roughly 0.8 millitesla. In (e) and (g), neighbour correlation reaches its maximum possible value — every neighbour has the opposite charge. Note the caption header: these are simulations.

**The honeycomb is the surprise.** With repulsion, the honeycomb cannot make every vertex neutral (three traps per vertex), so each vertex is stuck with a one-third charge and the system has several intermediate disordered and partly ordered phases. With attraction, nothing is stuck: three-in and three-out are perfectly compatible on a bipartite lattice. Flipping the sign does not mirror the honeycomb's frustration — it removes it.

### Step 5 — Two ways of reading the crystal

**The failure it prevents:** a fraction of vertex types says nothing about how they are arranged in space.

**What the authors do:** measure two complementary order parameters. The first is the neighbour charge correlation: negative when neighbouring vertices carry opposite charge, reaching its extreme (minus one) in a perfect alternating crystal. The second is **plaquette chirality**: around each small loop of the lattice, do the beads circulate in one sense, like a tiny current loop? Repulsive ices develop strong circulation (the ice rule naturally produces head-to-tail chains around loops). In the attractive crystal, the beads around a loop point alternately toward and away from the loop's corners, with no circulation.

**What it buys:** both agree — correlation hits its extreme and chirality drops to zero, so the charge crystal is a genuinely different state from repulsive ice, not a relabelled one.

### Step 6 — Odd loops are necessary but not sufficient

**The failure it prevents:** jumping to "any non-bipartite lattice will be frustrated and degenerate."

**What the authors find:** the simplest non-bipartite lattices — triangular (six traps per vertex) and the "direct kagome" (four traps per vertex) — still give a single ordered state under attraction. These lattices are tripartite (three colours suffice), and the methods section shows the predicted arrangement: one sublattice of fully charged vertices of each sign plus a third sublattice in between, repeating perfectly. Even though they contain triangles (odd loops), the frustration resolves into a unique pattern.

**What it buys:** a sharper statement of what pentaheptite is needed for. It is not odd loops alone; it is odd loops of *unequal* sizes in a lattice with only three traps per vertex, where no regular three-sublattice compromise exists. (The paper says this somewhat loosely in the Discussion, as Section 6 notes.)

### Step 7 — The pentaheptite lattice: frustration of a new kind

**The failure it prevents:** without an engineered odd-loop geometry, the anti-spin ice is always ordered and therefore uninteresting from a frustration standpoint.

**What the authors do:** build a pentaheptite lattice — every vertex joins three traps, and the plane is tiled by a repeating pattern of pentagons and heptagons — choosing a construction where every trap has the same length, so the bead-to-bead distances match across the lattice. They run it in simulation and, crucially, in experiment: lithographic wells in a polymer film, beads loaded at random, field ramped up while filming.

**What happens:** at low field, random bead positions give the statistics of coin flips: a vertex with three traps is fully charged (all in or all out) one time in four and carries the small one-third charge three times in four. As the field rises past about 0.9 millitesla (simulation scale), the populations swap: maximally charged vertices take over. But unlike the bipartite lattices, the one-third-charged vertices never disappear. They plateau at about one vertex in four (one in eight of each sign).

![Figure 3](figures/baillou-77905_fig3.jpg)

*Figure 3 (from the paper): (a) simulated low-energy state of the pentaheptite anti-spin ice with vertex charges colour-coded (legend at bottom); (b) the experiment — beads in elliptical wells on a pentaheptite pattern; (c) vertex fractions versus field, experiment (symbols) and simulation (lines); (d) neighbour charge correlation; (e, f) chirality of pentagonal and heptagonal loops.*

**What to look at:** in (c), the dashed lines (one-third-charged vertices) fall but level off near an eighth each rather than dropping to zero as in Figure 2 — that floor is the frustration. In (d), the correlation saturates around 0.6 instead of reaching 1. In (e) and (f), chirality settles on the dashed "predicted" lines at one-fifth and one-seventh rather than zero. The experiment (symbols) tracks the simulation curves, after the field rescaling discussed in Step 8.

**Why the floor is exactly where it is.** The argument is the parity rule from Section 3. Every fully charged vertex must sit next to fully charged vertices of the opposite sign, which is a two-colouring. Around a pentagon or heptagon that cannot close, so at least one vertex on every odd loop must give up and settle for a one-third charge. The repeating unit of this pentaheptite contains eight vertices and four loops (two pentagons, two heptagons), and two one-third vertices placed well can break all four loops at once. So the best possible state has six maximally charged vertices and two one-third vertices per repeat — exactly the plateau seen. Because these fractions come from counting loops rather than from temperature, the authors stress they are fixed by topology, not by thermal statistics.

A nice coincidence: those final fractions (three-quarters fully charged, one-quarter partially charged) are precisely the *reverse* of the random starting fractions. Annealing swaps the populations.

The same counting explains the chirality floors. Each pentagon carries one partially charged vertex out of five corners, each heptagon one out of seven, so the leftover circulation per loop is about one-fifth and one-seventh — the dashed lines in panels (e) and (f).

### Step 8 — Reconciling experiment and simulation

**The failure it prevents:** the experiment and simulation disagree on *where* along the field axis the crossover happens.

**What the authors do:** divide the experimental field values by 2.4 to line them up with simulation. They justify the factor with two measured or assumed differences. First, the beads in the experiment ended up farther apart at a vertex than the mask design intended (roughly six micrometres rather than four and a half), and the pull weakens steeply with distance. Second, the beads' magnetic responsiveness varies from batch to batch; the authors assume the experimental batch is about two-thirds as responsive as the value used in simulation. Together those require about 2.4 times more field for the same force.

The experiments also spin the field fifty times slower than the simulations, because at high rates the beads themselves rotate and roll along the cell floor in circular orbits that scramble the lattice. Supplementary simulations (not read here) reportedly show the averaging still works down to about one turn every eight seconds.

**What it buys:** quantitative overlap of the curves. **What it costs:** the 2.4 factor is fitted, with one of its two ingredients (the batch responsiveness) assumed rather than measured. The authors note — correctly — that the factor only shifts where the crossover happens, not the plateau values, which come from topology.

### Step 9 — The dimer picture

**The failure it prevents:** a vertex-fraction plateau shows that frustration exists but not what the degenerate states look like collectively.

**What the authors do:** draw a short bar across each trap that is "the odd one out" at a partially charged vertex, connecting the centres of the two loops on either side of that trap. At low field, many bars meet at single loops. At high field, each loop is touched by exactly one bar.

**What it buys:** "every loop touched by exactly one bar" is precisely a dimer model — a perfect matching on the graph whose nodes are the loop centres. Every low-energy state of the frustrated ice corresponds to one matching, and the many ways to choose a matching are the degeneracy. In central-analogy terms: on each odd ring of servers, consolidation must fail somewhere, and the set of places where it fails pairs up the rings two by two; any valid pairing is an equally good schedule.

### Claim check

*Checks were done with a short Python/SymPy script (`claimcheck_77905.py`) and by hand counting on the unit cell; nothing from the Supplementary Information could be checked.*

**Averaged attraction — Reproduced.** Averaging the direction-dependent dipole interaction over a full in-plane turn gives exactly minus one half of its prefactor, matching the paper's averaged potential. The core mechanism is sound.

**Field needed to beat thermal motion — Reproduced.** The paper's threshold of about a tenth of a millitesla comes out exactly using the simulation value of the beads' magnetic responsiveness. The quoted diffusion constant also matches the textbook value for a sphere of this size in water to within a few percent, so the inputs are internally consistent.

**Field rescaling factor of 2.4 — Reproduced, but fitted.** With the paper's stated distances and the assumed experimental responsiveness, the factor comes out at 2.44. The arithmetic holds; the input it rests on (the experimental responsiveness) is assumed, chosen to give this factor, and is only shown to lie within previously reported batch variation.

**Pentaheptite plateau and correlations — Partly reproduced.** The random-start fractions (one in eight and three in eight per sign) and the final plateau (two partially charged vertices per eight) follow directly from coin-flip statistics and the loop-counting argument. Counting bond by bond over the twelve trap-bonds of one repeat unit, the arrangement with the two partially charged vertices *adjacent* gives a neighbour correlation of exactly minus 128 over 216, matching the paper. For the arrangement with the two *separated* by one vertex, the same counting gives about minus 0.56, not the paper's minus 0.58. The difference is small and both values sit in the band of the measured saturation (Figure 3d), so the conclusion — short-range order that never reaches full alternation — is unaffected; the discrepancy may come from boundary bookkeeping in the six-cell cluster the authors used, which the counting here does not replicate. **(analyst inference)**

**Sensitivity check.** The single most influential assumption is the rescaling factor, which sets where the experimental crossover lands. The central claim survives any value of it: the plateau and correlation values are set by loop counting, not by field, so the topological conclusion holds unless the experimental field never actually reached the high-attraction regime. With the highest experimental field equivalent to roughly 1.6 millitesla on the simulation scale, it did, comfortably above the crossover.

**Robustness test (simulation type: an analytic limit).** The cleanest independent check is the exact counting limit: the simulated plateau should equal the topological minimum of partially charged vertices, and it does (one in eight per sign), along with the chirality floors at one-fifth and one-seventh. A long-range dipolar simulation landing exactly on a counting bound is strong evidence the simulations reached the ground-state manifold rather than a kinetic trap.

**Internal consistency — two items worth a line each.** First, the printed formulas for the pair force and for the approach-rate constant disagree with the averaged potential by factors of two and four respectively; the plotted theory line in Figure 1c has the slope that the *correct* derivation gives, so this is a typesetting slip in the printed prefactors, not a physics error, and it cancels out of the 2.4 ratio. Second, the field ramp rate for the bipartite simulations is quoted as three different values (main text, Figure 2 caption, Methods) differing by up to a factor of ten. Because ramp rate controls how many grain boundaries survive, a reader trying to reproduce the snapshots needs to check the Zenodo code for the actual value.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the square and honeycomb anti-spin ices were realized experimentally, as the abstract's "combining experiments and simulations across bipartite square and honeycomb lattices" suggests. The paper actually shows those lattices only in simulation (Figure 2 is labelled "Numerical results"); the only lattice experiment is the pentaheptite.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes experiment and simulation were run under matching conditions. The paper actually ran experiments at a fifty-times-slower spin rate and rescaled the experimental field by a fitted factor of 2.4, one of whose inputs is an assumed (not measured) bead property. The agreement in Figure 3 is therefore in shape and plateau values, not in absolute field.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "odd loops cause frustration" is the full rule. The paper actually finds lattices with odd loops (triangular, direct kagome) that still order uniquely; the degenerate manifold needs the specific combination of three traps per vertex and odd loops of unequal sizes, and the paper does not state a general criterion.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**The sign flip, done cleanly.** Spinning an in-plane field to manufacture an isotropic attraction is not new in itself (it has been used for decades to make magnetic colloids cluster in free suspension), but putting it into a lattice of traps — where each bead is a binary spin — is new to the field. It converts an established platform into its "dual" with a dial, and the paper's outlook (tilting the spin axis through the magic angle to switch continuously between ice and anti-ice) makes that dial explicit.

**Frustration as a failure to maximize.** The conceptual reframe is the real contribution. Ordinary frustration is "every junction wants to be neutral, but neutral junctions can't tile the lattice." Here it is "every junction wants to be maximally charged, which requires a two-colouring, and odd loops forbid two-colourings." That moves the frustration from the vertex to the lattice's loop topology, makes it calculable by pure counting (the exact one-in-eight plateau and the one-fifth and one-seventh chirality floors), and maps the leftover degeneracy onto a dimer model. New to the field, though a natural extension of the authors' earlier vertex-frustration work in magnetic artificial ice.

**Predictive Content Check**

1. **Falsifiable handle (always):** the strongest handle is the plateau. On the equilateral pentaheptite with strong attraction, partially charged vertices should never fall below one in eight of each sign, the neighbour correlation should saturate near minus 0.6 rather than minus 1, and pentagonal and heptagonal loops should keep chirality near one-fifth and one-seventh. The experiment already tests this and agrees. A second, so-far-untested handle: an experimental square or honeycomb anti-spin ice should reach a full staggered crystal with zero partially charged vertices and zero chirality given slow enough annealing — and a degenerate pentaheptite manifold should show the residual entropy of the corresponding dimer model, which the paper does not compute. These are genuine predictions, not relabelling.

2. **Formalism load:** skipped — the mathematics (dipole averaging and vertex counting) is light and plainly doing its job.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**Square and honeycomb results are simulation-only.** The headline "charge crystallization in bipartite lattices" has not been demonstrated in a lab; experimental realizations often reveal kinetic traps, disorder in trap geometry, and bead-size variation that simulations omit. **(A) Consensus** — the paper's own figure labelling makes this explicit, and the field generally treats unrealized simulation results as provisional. **(paper, Fig. 2 caption and abstract)**

**The experiment–simulation match depends on a fitted field factor.** The 2.4 rescaling uses an assumed bead responsiveness chosen to produce agreement, and the experimental spin rate is fifty times lower than simulated. The plateau values do not depend on this, but any statement about *where* the crossover occurs in a real sample does. **(A) Consensus** — the authors state the rescaling and the frequency difference openly; the only question is how much weight to put on it, and the topological conclusions are insulated from it. **(paper, Results section on the frustrated pentaheptite lattice)**

**No residual-entropy or degeneracy count.** The paper calls the pentaheptite manifold "degenerate" and maps it to a dimer model but never counts the states, estimates the entropy per vertex, or shows that the degeneracy is extensive (growing with system size) rather than a handful of arrangements. The two example configurations in the Methods show at least two; how many more exist sets whether this is Pauling-style ice entropy or a small finite degeneracy. **(B) Contested** — dimer models on most planar graphs have extensive entropy, so experts may consider this implied, but the specific dual graph here has not been analysed and the energy splittings between configurations (the paper notes vertex energies differ by up to about one percent depending on bond angles) could lift some of the degeneracy. **(analyst inference)**

**The stated criterion for frustration is looser than the results.** The Discussion says anti-spin-ice frustration needs lattices whose loops contain an odd number of traps; the triangular and direct-kagome results show odd loops can still give a unique ordered state. A precise statement — which lattices give extensive degeneracy under charge maximization — is left open. **(C) Speculative** — this is my reading of a possible gap between the Discussion wording and the Methods results; the authors may consider "unequal odd polygons" (their phrasing in the abstract) to be the criterion. **(analyst inference)**

**Equal-length trap construction introduces small vertex inequivalences.** Making every trap the same length forces several different bond angles, which splits each vertex type into slightly different energies (about one percent). In a frustrated manifold, small energy differences are exactly what can select one state over the others at low temperature ("order by disorder" or energetic selection), so the long-time ground state could be less degenerate than the annealed states suggest. **(B) Contested** — the paper presents the splitting as negligible, but in frustrated systems small perturbations often decide the true ground state. **(paper, Methods; analyst inference on consequences)**

**Ramp-rate inconsistency and grain boundaries.** The quoted annealing rates differ by up to tenfold across the text, caption, and Methods, and the bipartite snapshots retain grain boundaries that the paper argues would vanish with slower ramps — an assertion not demonstrated. **(C) Speculative** — the claim that grain boundaries are purely kinetic is plausible but untested, and the inconsistency makes the simulation protocol harder to reproduce from the text alone. **(analyst inference)**

**Open directions (12–24 months):** an experimental square or honeycomb anti-spin ice; the tilted-cone field protocol to sweep continuously from ice to anti-ice and look for hysteresis and memory; random or ordered decimation (removing beads) to probe how charge maximization reacts to defects; and a direct count of the pentaheptite degeneracy and its dimer-model entropy.

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

**Summary.** The authors build the first "anti-spin ice": magnetic beads, one per elongated trap along the edges of a lattice, made to *attract* rather than repel by spinning a magnetic field in the sample plane, which averages the beads' direction-dependent interaction into a uniform pull (confirmed with pairs of free beads).

In ordinary (repulsive) particle ice, each lattice junction tries to keep beads away, and the collective compromise is the ice rule: junctions stay as close to neutral as possible. With attraction the preference reverses: each junction wants all its beads or none. On lattices that can be two-coloured — the square and honeycomb — that preference can be met everywhere, and simulations show a perfect alternating crystal of maximally charged junctions, with neighbour correlations at their extreme and the loop circulation characteristic of ordinary ice wiped out. Strikingly, the honeycomb, which is frustrated with repulsion, becomes perfectly ordered with attraction.

The key result comes on the pentaheptite lattice, tiled with pentagons and heptagons and studied in both simulation and experiment. Every odd loop forbids the alternating pattern somewhere, so a minimum of two in every eight junctions stays partially charged even at the strongest attraction. Simulation and experiment both show this plateau, a saturated-but-incomplete neighbour correlation, and leftover loop circulation of about one-fifth on pentagons and one-seventh on heptagons — all of which follow from pure counting on the lattice's repeat unit. The leftover arrangements map onto a dimer model, suggesting a degenerate low-energy manifold produced not by failing to minimize a local quantity but by failing to maximize one globally.

**Why the summary is framed this way.** The four lattices are not equally important: the bipartite results are the expected (simulation-only) baseline, the tripartite lattices are a control, and the pentaheptite holds the new physics and the only lattice experiment. The central interpretive choice is to emphasize that the pentaheptite's plateau values are topological — they come from counting loops in a repeat unit — because that is what makes the result robust to the fitted field rescaling and to the experimental compromises. The reader should take away the reframe: frustration is about the compatibility between a local preference and global topology, and that holds whichever direction the preference points.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the claim that the pentaheptite manifold is genuinely and extensively degenerate. The paper shows the plateau and the dimer mapping convincingly but never counts the states; I have inferred from general dimer-model behaviour that the degeneracy probably grows with system size, and I could not check the Supplementary Information or reproduce the paper's six-cell correlation bookkeeping, which is also where my roughly five-percent correlation discrepancy lives.

**Revision notes:** the referee pass added the simulation-only status of the bipartite results to the Replication Note and Assumption Audit, sharpened Step 6 into "odd loops are necessary but not sufficient," added the vertex-energy-splitting limitation, and moved the prefactor and ramp-rate slips into the Claim check as one-line consistency items.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **Make colloids attract instead of repel, and the ice rule turns inside out:** junctions stop trying to stay neutral and start trying to grab all their beads or none, so on simple lattices the beads clump at every other junction in a perfect charge crystal.

2. **Frustration doesn't need a minimization:** on a lattice of pentagons and heptagons, "every junction maximally charged" would require colouring the lattice in two alternating colours, which odd loops make impossible — so about one junction in four is forced to compromise, a number fixed by counting, not by temperature.

3. **The sign of the force is now a design knob:** a spinning magnetic field turns repulsion into attraction, and tilting that spin toward the NMR "magic angle" could dial continuously between ordinary ice and anti-ice in the same sample.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Artificial spin ices are lattices of tiny two-state elements whose interactions can't all be satisfied at once, producing a balanced, disordered low-energy state governed by the "ice rule" first worked out for water ice. In particle versions, magnetic beads sit one per elongated trap along the lattice's edges, each able to rest at either end, and repel each other — so every junction tries to stay as neutral as possible.

This paper makes the beads attract instead. Spinning a magnetic field in the plane of the sample, about fifty times per second, averages the beads' direction-dependent magnetic interaction into a uniform pull; pairs of free beads confirm it by collapsing together exactly as predicted.

With attraction, each junction wants all of its beads or none. On square and honeycomb lattices, which can be coloured in two alternating colours, simulations show that wish granted everywhere: beads clump at every other junction, forming a perfect alternating crystal of maximum "charge." The honeycomb, frustrated in ordinary ice, becomes fully ordered.

On a pentaheptite lattice — tiled with pentagons and heptagons — the alternating pattern is impossible, because a loop with an odd number of sides cannot alternate cleanly. Both simulations and a real experiment show about one junction in four permanently stuck in a compromise state, a fraction fixed by counting the loops in the lattice's repeating unit. The leftover arrangements map onto a classic "dimer" counting problem, pointing to many equally good states: frustration produced by failing to *maximize* something, rather than failing to minimize it.

Caveats: the square and honeycomb results are simulation-only; experiment and simulation were reconciled with a fitted field factor and a fifty-times-slower field rotation in the lab; and the paper never counts how many degenerate states exist. Still, the work turns the sign of the interaction into a design knob and reframes frustration as a mismatch between local preferences and global topology, whichever way the preference points.
