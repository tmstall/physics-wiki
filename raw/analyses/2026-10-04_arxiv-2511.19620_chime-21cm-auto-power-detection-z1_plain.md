## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**CHIME Hears the Hydrogen Hum on Its Own**

For fifteen years, radio telescopes could only find the faint glow of distant hydrogen by matching their maps against a galaxy catalog, which told them where to look. CHIME has now pulled that glow, from 8 to 9 billion years ago, straight out of its own data. It did so by being extremely careful never to let its own processing fake the signal. The price was giving up the very large scales the telescope was built to study.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

**What's being measured.** Hydrogen atoms give off a very weak radio signal at a wavelength of 21 cm. Because the universe is expanding, hydrogen farther away shows up at a lower radio frequency. So a radio telescope that tunes across a range of frequencies is really looking through the universe at a range of distances. It can build a 3D map of where the hydrogen is without picking out individual galaxies. It just records the total glow in each chunk of space. This is called **intensity mapping**. The hope is that it can become a cheap, fast way to map the large-scale structure of the universe. One prize is the "baryon acoustic oscillation" (BAO) scale, a standard ruler for measuring how the universe's expansion has changed over time.

**Why it's been stuck.** The hydrogen glow is buried under radio emission from our own Galaxy and from bright radio galaxies, about ten thousand times brighter. Until now, the safe way to dig it out was **cross-correlation**: line the radio map up against an optical galaxy survey. The foreground junk doesn't know where the galaxies are, so it averages away, and only the real hydrogen survives. Every detection since 2010 has worked that way, including CHIME's own earlier results, where it stacked its maps on galaxies and quasars from the eBOSS survey. Cross-correlation proves the signal is there. But it always depends on another survey and is limited by how much it overlaps with that survey.

**What this paper does.** It reports the **auto-power spectrum**: the hydrogen map compared with itself, with no outside survey needed. This is much harder, because any leftover junk that stays the same from night to night looks exactly like signal. The paper uses 94 nights of 2019 data from a band around redshift 1.16, over a patch of sky about 2,200 square degrees. It detects the signal at 12.4 standard deviations, and each half of the frequency band detects it separately as well.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** an observational methods-and-detection paper from a large collaboration. Most of its pages describe a rebuilt data-processing pipeline. The question it settles is whether a wide-field radio telescope with no moving parts can control its systematics well enough to stand on its own. That matters for CHIME's long-term goal and for successors like CHORD and HIRAX.

**Prior Belief Check:** the result fits mainstream expectations. People expected that someone would eventually detect this, and the strength of the signal agrees with CHIME's earlier stacking result. What's notable is how strong the first detection is (12σ, not a marginal 3σ) and how it was achieved: by careful processing order rather than a better model of the sky. It is a systematics-control milestone, not a cosmology result. The one possibly surprising physics result is in the companion paper: a leading galaxy simulation (IllustrisTNG) gets the hydrogen's clustering wrong at roughly 3–4σ.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** one instrument, one team, no independent confirmation at this distance. The closest comparison is a MeerKAT auto-detection at much lower redshift. CHIME cites it at about 8σ and 11σ, but MeerKAT's own revised (now published) version reports a more cautious 3–3.5σ under conservative data flagging. Independent confirmation would need a second telescope (CHORD, HIRAX, Tianlai or MeerKAT at higher redshift). CHIME running the same pipeline on its seven-year archive would test stability, but it wouldn't be independent.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

### Where this hydrogen came from

After the Big Bang, the universe cooled into mostly neutral hydrogen gas. As stars and galaxies formed, their ultraviolet light stripped the electrons back off most of the gas between galaxies (reionization), and that process finished about a billion years after the Big Bang. After that, neutral hydrogen survives only in dense clouds inside and around galaxies, where the outer layers shield the inside.

Oddly, the total amount of neutral hydrogen in the universe has stayed roughly constant for most of cosmic history, even while star formation rose to a peak and then fell tenfold. It behaves like a buffer that stays about half full while the traffic through it surges and fades: hydrogen keeps getting used up by star formation and topped up by fresh gas falling in.

CHIME looks at this hydrogen 8–9 billion years ago, when it lived mostly in ordinary star-forming galaxies. Those galaxies trace the cosmic web of dark matter, so the hydrogen glow traces it too. Two things set how the hydrogen sky looks: **how much hydrogen there is**, and **how strongly it clumps** compared with the underlying matter. The measurement only sees the two multiplied together.

CHIME itself is four fixed half-pipe reflectors, each 100 m long, with about a thousand antennas along their focal lines and no moving parts. The Earth's rotation sweeps the sky past it each day. It was designed for this hydrogen measurement, and along the way it became the world's leading detector of fast radio bursts.

### Intensity mapping: measure the total, not the individuals

**Analogy:** a galaxy survey is like logging every packet on a network, which is precise but expensive. Intensity mapping is like reading the traffic counters on each switch port: you lose the individual packets but get the overall load pattern almost for free. For cosmology, the overall pattern is what matters.

**Breaks when:** you ask what's actually in each counter. A traffic counter reports a clean total. A radio map "pixel" reports the hydrogen *plus* foregrounds thousands of times brighter. No network counter has that problem built in, and that problem is what the whole paper is about.

### How a radio interferometer sees, and why smooth sources stay in a corner

CHIME doesn't take pictures directly. It compares the signals from pairs of antennas. A source off to one side reaches one antenna a tiny bit later than the other, and that lag is the **delay**. The delay shows up as a pattern that cycles gently up and down as you step across frequency. Bigger delays cycle faster, and no source can produce a delay larger than the time light takes to cross the antenna spacing.

If you sort the data by how fast it wiggles across frequency (a Fourier transform along the frequency axis), each source lands in its own spot on a "delay" axis. All the bright foreground emission (smooth, slowly changing with frequency) collects in the low-delay corner. That corner is the **foreground wedge**. It's bigger for widely spaced antennas, but it has a firm edge.

The hydrogen behaves differently. Each frequency channel is a thin slab of the universe, about 2 h⁻¹ Mpc (roughly 9 million light-years) deep. Stepping through channels is like stepping outward through space. Neighbouring slabs share some of the same structures, but the hydrogen's lumpiness means its brightness also changes irregularly from slab to slab. Those faster changes land at higher delay, outside the foreground corner. High delay means fine structure along the line of sight. The analysis keeps only modes that cycle faster than about once every 18 h⁻¹ Mpc.

### The central difficulty: predictable foregrounds, unpredictable signal

> **Concept:** The foregrounds change smoothly with frequency, so a few slowly varying numbers describe them almost completely. The hydrogen changes irregularly. Foreground removal throws away everything a smooth description can predict and keeps what's left. That works only if nothing has added sharp, non-smooth features to the foregrounds. Instrument quirks and data-processing steps can add them, and because the foregrounds are 10,000 times brighter, even a tiny sharp feature can be as big as the signal.

> **Vehicle:** A compressor fed a slowly varying sensor log shrinks it to a few numbers, and the leftover after its prediction is the truly unpredictable part. But if one sample was dropped and zero-filled, the compressor's model breaks, and that glitch shows up in the "unpredictable" leftover at full size.

> **Analogy:** smooth foregrounds ↔ the compressible sensor log; the foreground filter ↔ subtracting the compressor's prediction; the hydrogen ↔ the genuinely unpredictable leftover; masked channels, gain changes and averaging nights with different masks ↔ dropped samples that manufacture fake "unpredictable" content.

> **Breaks when:** you ask how to tell real leftovers from manufactured ones. A compressor can't, because a residual is a residual. The paper can only do it statistically. Real hydrogen is the same every night, unpolarized, the same in every patch of sky and with every antenna spacing, and it lines up with known quasars. That's why the validation tests in Section 4 matter as much as the detection.

Figure 2 shows the most important way processing can manufacture fake signal.

![Fig. 2: gain variation coupling to the RFI mask](figures/detection-2511.19620_fig2.png)

*Figure 2 (from the paper): two nights of data (red, blue). Each is perfectly smooth, but they differ by a 1% gain, and different channels are blanked out on each night (the gaps). Their average (black) jumps wherever only one night contributes.*

**What to look at:** neither coloured line has any structure, yet their average is jagged. That jaggedness is fake signal created purely by averaging. This is why CHIME now cleans each 10-second snapshot *before* averaging anything.

### The power spectrum, and the even/odd trick

The **power spectrum** says how much the map fluctuates at each size scale. Squaring a noisy map always adds noise, so CHIME splits its nights into two interleaved halves ("even" and "odd" nights) and multiplies one half's map by the other's. Noise is different in the two halves, so it averages out. The sky is the same in both, so it survives. A bonus is that the result has a "real" part (signal plus noise) and an "imaginary" part that should be pure noise, which gives a free built-in check.

**Analogy:** comparing two independent replica logs of the same events. Shared events line up; each replica's private jitter doesn't.

**Breaks when:** the error is shared by both replicas. Junk that is stable from night to night shows up in both halves and survives just like signal. The trick removes noise, not stable contamination.

### Why only "amount × clumpiness" is measured

How bright the hydrogen glow is depends on how much hydrogen there is. How lumpy it looks depends on how strongly the hydrogen clusters, boosted a little by the motions of galaxies falling toward dense regions (which squash structures along the line of sight). On small scales, random internal motions smear structures out instead (the "fingers of God" effect). The measurement sees these combined into one number, which the paper calls the amplitude, **A²_HI ≈ 3.6**.

**Analogy:** a network monitor that reports total bytes × burstiness but neither on its own. Double the traffic and halve the burstiness and the reading barely moves.

*(A small note: the paper's introduction and conclusion write this amplitude formula slightly differently from the main definition, with the bias squared inside the bracket. That looks like a typo; the main definition is the one used.)*

> **Central analogy for this paper:** signal is what the smooth predictor can't compress.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### The pipeline at a glance

*Analyst-built concept diagram (not from the paper):*

```
 94 clean nights (2019)
        |
 find & blank out radio interference (3 detectors)
        |
 make the beam look the same at every frequency
        |
 remove smooth foregrounds from EVERY 10-s snapshot
 + small extra corrections (bandpass, cross-talk)
        |
 average nights -> "even" and "odd" maps
        |
 mask bright sources + 40 hydrogen absorbers
        |
 keep only the fine-scale modes; compare even x odd
        |
 compare with 1,000 noise-only simulations -> 12.4 sigma
```

Each box removes one way of manufacturing fake signal. The last box asks whether what's left is bigger than noise alone could produce.

### Step 1: aggressive interference removal

Human-made radio signals (TV, cell phones, signals bouncing off aircraft and meteor trails) are spectrally sharp, so they land exactly where the hydrogen signal lives. CHIME now runs three independent detectors, each catching a different kind of interference, and throws out anything any of them flags. Together they remove about 40% of the night-time data in this band, and the daytime data is excluded entirely. The authors deliberately over-mask. A side observation: the measured noise is a couple of percent higher than theory predicts, for reasons nobody yet understands.

### Step 2: a beam that doesn't change with frequency

A telescope's view naturally gets sharper at higher frequency. That change is itself a spectral feature that can stamp ripples onto bright sources. CHIME now weights its antennas so that its view has the same shape at every frequency, trading a little sensitivity for smoothness.

### Step 3: clean first, average later (the key change)

This is the heart of the new pipeline. Figure 2 showed that averaging nights with different blanked-out channels creates fake structure. So CHIME now applies its foreground filter to every single 10-second snapshot, using that snapshot's own interference mask, *before* any averaging. It also keeps track of exactly what the filter did, so the identical filtering can be applied to simulations later.

Two smaller fixes follow:

- **HyFoReS** notices that the leftover leakage from bright sources has a common pattern, the signature of a small daily calibration error, and subtracts an estimate of it. The paper itself calls the improvement "modest": it's one helpful layer, not the main fix.
- **Cross-talk subtraction** removes a steady offset caused by antennas picking up each other's electronic noise.

The result is dramatically cleaner maps. The blotches and stripes of the old pipeline largely vanish. What remains are curved tracks from a handful of very bright radio sources far off to the side, and those get masked.

### Step 4: maps, absorbers, and the split

The nights are split into even and odd sets and turned into two sky maps. A bonus discovery: the clean maps reveal 40 narrow dips where distant hydrogen *absorbs* light from a background radio source. One is a known system; the other 39 are new candidates. They're masked so they don't contaminate the measurement.

### Step 5: masks, then the measurement

About a third of the sky patch is masked around bright sources. The remaining maps are compared even-against-odd and averaged into 8 size bins. To convert to physical units, the team pushes a fake sky through the entire pipeline and sees what comes out, so any signal the filter eats is accounted for automatically. To know what "just noise" looks like, they push 1,000 noise-only simulations through the same pipeline.

### The result

![Fig. 13: the 1D auto-power spectrum (central result)](figures/detection-2511.19620_fig13.png)

*Figure 13 (from the paper): the measured hydrogen power spectrum. Red is the real part (signal plus noise), blue the imaginary part (should be noise only), and black the best-fitting model. The bottom panel shows the leftovers after subtracting the model.*

**What to look at:** the red points fall steadily from large to small scales and every one sits clearly above zero. The model passes right through them. The blue points hover near zero, as noise should. (They all happen to sit slightly above zero, which comes up in the Claim check.)

The 2D version of the measurement, before averaging into bins, looks like pure noise cell by cell, with no sign of foreground leakage. The signal only appears once tens of thousands of cells are combined. Splitting the band in half gives two independent detections (about 9σ each) whose amplitudes agree.

The significance comes from asking how much better "signal" fits the data than "nothing", and how often noise alone could fake that improvement. The paper quotes the most conservative of three ways of calculating this (12.4σ); the others give about 13σ.

### Is it really hydrogen? The validation

The authors run ten checks. Each is designed so that a particular kind of contamination would show up as a mismatch, while real hydrogen would not:

- **Imaginary part** looks like noise → no calibration mismatch between the halves.
- **Even nights minus odd nights** looks like noise → no interference that changes night to night.
- **Polarized map** looks like noise → hydrogen is unpolarized; leaking polarized foregrounds would show up here.
- **Two halves of the sky** agree, even though one half has much more Galactic emission.
- **Short versus long antenna spacings** agree, though foreground leakage should grow with spacing.
- **The two frequency halves** agree, though foregrounds are much brighter at low frequency.
- **Tighter source masks** change nothing, though leakage from bright sources should drop noticeably.
- **Stacking on 33,000 quasars** recovers the signal at 9σ, with an amplitude matching the auto-spectrum (below).

![Fig. 23: auto vs cross amplitude](figures/detection-2511.19620_fig23.png)

*Figure 23 (from the paper): how strongly hydrogen clusters, measured two ways. Black curves: from stacking on quasars. Red: from the auto-spectrum.*

**What to look at:** the red peak sits inside the broad black curve. Contamination would inflate the auto-spectrum but not the quasar stacking (junk doesn't know where quasars are), so it would push the red curve to the right. It doesn't. This is the strongest single argument that the signal is hydrogen, though the quasar measurement is fuzzy enough that it only rules out *large* contamination.

### Claim check

I re-ran the key numbers from values read off the paper's figures.

1. **Detection significance — reproduced.** Reading the plotted data and the noise correlations gives 12.4σ with the paper's method, and about 13σ with simpler ones, matching the paper.
2. **Scales and sky area — reproduced.** The delay-to-scale conversions, bin widths and sky area all check out.
3. **Amplitude — consistent.** The auto-spectrum and quasar-stacking amplitudes agree. If you plug in a standard estimate of how much hydrogen there is, the implied clumpiness is on the high side of expectations, which points the same way as the companion paper's disagreement with simulations. But the shape model soaks up part of the amplitude, so this isn't a clean measurement *(analyst inference)*.

**Where the signal comes from:** most of the detection strength sits in the three largest-scale bins, right next to the filter's edge. Dropping them still leaves a clear detection (around 7σ), so the *detection* is safe. But the *amplitude* depends on getting the filter's effect right in exactly those bins.

**Two small patterns the paper doesn't mention:** all 8 imaginary points sit slightly above zero, and the polarized map is above zero in 7 of 8 bins. Each test as the paper ran it (an overall "is this consistent with noise?" score) passes. But that kind of score can miss a small, consistent offset. A plausible harmless explanation for the polarized pattern is a little real hydrogen leaking through slight differences between the antennas' two polarizations. Neither pattern threatens a 12σ detection; both matter only for percent-level amplitude claims. *(Read by eye from figures, test chosen after looking, so weakly held.)*

**One more point from the source:** the error bars come only from noise simulations. That is the right choice for asking "is there a signal?", but it leaves out the natural scatter of the signal itself, so the uncertainty on the amplitude is a bit understated.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** "First auto-detection" might suggest CHIME has now measured cosmology from hydrogen alone. It hasn't. Only fine scales are measured, and the filter throws away the large-scale BAO ruler, as the authors say plainly. What's constrained is one combined "amount × clumpiness" number plus two loosely defined shape knobs.

> **Watch:** "12σ" might suggest the amplitude is known to about 8%. It isn't: 12σ means "definitely not zero," while the amplitude itself carries roughly ±30% statistical plus ~17% systematic uncertainty. Detecting something and measuring it are different things here.

> **Watch:** "No external galaxy survey needed" might suggest no outside inputs at all. The *detection* needs no survey, but the unit conversion relies on a beam model and simulations, the redshift trends use outside fitting formulas, and the strongest anti-contamination argument is the quasar comparison. "Standalone" describes the signal, not the validation.

> **Watch:** "Null tests passed" might suggest contamination is ruled out. Each test rules out contamination only at the level it's sensitive to, and small consistent offsets can slip through.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

The sharpest idea is **treating the order of processing steps as a source of error, and fixing it by cleaning before averaging**. Radio astronomers have long known that blanked-out channels can interact with bright foregrounds. Figure 2 shows the effect in one picture: two smooth nights, two different masks, one jagged average. The old approach (CHIME's 2023 pipeline included) averaged first and cleaned later, by which point the damage was baked in. Cleaning every 10-second snapshot with its own mask, and carrying a record of that cleaning through every later step, changes the goal from "model the sky better" to "never create the problem in the first place." It is computationally heavy but general, and other telescopes can adopt it directly.

The second clever move is **designing each validation test around a physical difference between hydrogen and contamination**: Galactic brightness differs across the sky, leakage grows with antenna spacing, foregrounds rise at low frequency, polarized junk shows up in the polarized map, and junk doesn't line up with quasars. One test even predicts how much leakage should drop with tighter masks (about half), and the data show no such drop. Together this is the most complete case yet that a hydrogen auto-signal is real.

A bonus: 39 new candidate hydrogen absorbers, found as a by-product, which opens a separate line of science from the same data.

### Predictive Content Check

1. **Falsifiable handle:** any independent telescope mapping hydrogen at this distance should find about the same amplitude and the same fall-off with scale. Adding CHIME's remaining six years should raise the significance roughly as the square root of the extra data, and tighter masks should keep leaving the result unchanged. If the amplitude shrinks as masks tighten or data are added, part of it was contamination. The companion paper adds a physics prediction: better simulations of hydrogen motions should close the 3–4σ gap with IllustrisTNG without changing the total amount of hydrogen.
2. **Formalism load:** skipped. The pipeline's mathematics is plainly doing its job; the fitted model is simple and openly described as approximate.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**The large scales (including BAO) are thrown away.** The filter that removes foregrounds also removes all large-scale structure, so the cosmology CHIME was built for isn't reachable yet. **(A) Consensus** — the authors say so directly.

**Only "amount × clumpiness" is measured.** Separating the two needs outside information. **(A) Consensus** — built into this kind of measurement.

**The shape model is loose, and its best fit sits at odd parameter values.** The authors argue the averaged-over answers are what count. **(B) Contested** — some modellers will want a more physical model before trusting the amplitude.

**The noise model is approximate at around the 10% level**, and the measured noise is slightly higher than theory for unknown reasons. Even a 10% underestimate would lower the significance by only about 1σ. **(B) Contested** — reasonable, but a strict referee might ask for an independent check.

**The error bars leave out the signal's own natural scatter.** That's fine for detection, but it slightly understates the amplitude's uncertainty. **(B) Contested** — standard practice for a detection paper, though worth noting for the amplitude.

**The null tests can miss small consistent offsets** (the imaginary and polarized patterns above). **(C) Speculative** — my own reading of the figures, possibly harmless.

**Most of the detection strength sits next to the filter edge**, where getting the filter's effect right matters most for the amplitude. **(C) Speculative** — the paper models this carefully; the concern is mine.

**Heavy masking, one small patch of sky, one year of data**, and the closest-spaced antennas excluded entirely. **(A) Consensus** — the authors' stated scope.

**A small statistical wrinkle:** one of three sets of noise simulations failed one fit test; the authors show it's a mild outlier. **(B) Contested** — handled reasonably.

**No independent confirmation, and the main outside comparison has weakened.** MeerKAT's lower-redshift claim has been revised down to about 3σ under conservative flagging. **(A) Consensus.**

**Open questions for the next year or two:** Can gentler filtering recover the large scales? Does the amplitude hold steady with seven years of data? What are the 39 new absorbers? And why does IllustrisTNG get the hydrogen's small-scale clustering wrong?

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

CHIME has detected the radio glow of neutral hydrogen 8–9 billion years ago by comparing its own maps with themselves, without leaning on a galaxy catalog. Using 94 nights of 2019 data over about 2,200 square degrees, it measures how the glow fluctuates on fine scales. The signal stands 12.4σ above noise, and each half of the frequency band detects it on its own.

Most of the paper is about keeping the foregrounds out. The foregrounds are about ten thousand times brighter but change smoothly with frequency; the hydrogen doesn't. Filtering out smooth behaviour leaves the hydrogen, as long as nothing has roughened the foregrounds. The new pipeline is built around never roughening them:

- three interference detectors;
- a beam that looks the same at every frequency;
- cleaning every 10-second snapshot before any averaging;
- small corrections for a calibration error and antenna cross-talk;
- masks over bright sources and 40 newly found hydrogen absorbers.

Both the noise and the filter's effect on the signal are handled by pushing simulations through the identical pipeline.

The validation is the paper's strongest asset. The signal is the same across sky halves, antenna spacings, frequency halves and mask choices. It is absent from night-to-night differences and from the polarized map. And it matches what the same maps give when stacked on 33,000 quasars.

Physically, the result pins down one combined number, the amount of hydrogen times how clumpy it is. The companion paper finds that a leading simulation gets the clumping wrong at 3–4σ, apparently through how hydrogen moves inside galaxies rather than how much there is.

**Why I framed it this way.** I treat this as a systematics-control milestone rather than a cosmology result. The authors themselves say the large-scale ruler is gone, and the measured quantity is a single combined amplitude. I gave the validation almost as much weight as the detection, because for an auto-spectrum the hard question isn't "is there power?" but "is the power hydrogen?" My own checks reproduce the headline number, so it's sound. My added caveats affect how precisely the amplitude is known, not whether the detection is real.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the "slightly above zero" patterns rest on reading values by eye, with the test chosen after looking, and could easily be chance or harmless polarization leakage. I also explained the filtering and map-making steps by what they protect against, without checking how they're implemented; subtle problems would live in those details.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. A Canadian radio telescope with no moving parts has detected the faint collective glow of hydrogen from 8–9 billion years ago using only its own maps, at 12 standard deviations. It used an optical survey only afterwards, to confirm the result.

2. The breakthrough came from software discipline more than hardware: the team cleaned every ten-second snapshot *before* averaging, so differences between nights couldn't manufacture fake signal.

3. This proves the method works, not yet the cosmology. The large-scale ruler that carries dark-energy information was deliberately thrown away to make the detection clean, and the result is a single "amount × clumpiness" number.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Neutral hydrogen gives off a faint radio signal at 21 centimetres. Because the universe expands, hydrogen at different distances shows up at different frequencies, so a radio telescope can map hydrogen in 3D without seeing individual galaxies. Until now, the signal could only be found by matching radio maps against optical galaxy catalogs, because radio emission from our own Galaxy is about ten thousand times brighter.

The CHIME telescope in British Columbia has now detected the hydrogen signal on its own, using 94 nights of 2019 data, from 8–9 billion years ago, at 12 standard deviations. The key was a rebuilt pipeline that removes the smooth Galactic glow from every ten-second snapshot before averaging, so nightly differences can't create fake signal. A long series of checks argues that the signal is real hydrogen: it's the same in different parts of the sky, with different antenna spacings and in both halves of the frequency band, and it matches what you get by lining the maps up with known quasars.

The limits are clear. The large scales that carry dark-energy information were discarded to make the detection clean, the result measures only "amount of hydrogen × how clumpy it is," a companion paper finds a leading simulation gets the clumping wrong, and no other telescope has confirmed the signal at this distance yet.
