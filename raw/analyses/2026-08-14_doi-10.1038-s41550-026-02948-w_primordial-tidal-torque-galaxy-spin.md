## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**"Galaxies Remember How They Were Born: A 7-Sigma Fingerprint of the Infant Universe, Still Written in How They Spin"**

By comparing how nearby galaxies are actually observed to be spinning against how a 13-billion-year-old gravitational "birth environment" — mathematically reconstructed by rewinding today's cosmic map — predicts they *should* spin, this study finds a real, statistically robust alignment, confirming a 55-year-old theory of where galaxy rotation ultimately comes from.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Every rotating galaxy has angular momentum — spin — and for decades cosmologists have had a specific theoretical story for where that spin originally came from, called **tidal-torque theory (TTT)**: as the raw material that eventually collapses into a galaxy's dark matter halo sits, still diffuse, in the early universe, it experiences a gravitational *tug* from all the lumpy matter surrounding it. If that surrounding tug (the tidal field) doesn't line up perfectly with the shape of the collapsing patch itself, the mismatch produces a torque, and that torque is what spins the protohalo up as it collapses — meaning a galaxy's spin *direction*, not just its existence, should in principle be traceable back to the specific geometry of the gravitational field it was born in, more than 13 billion years ago.

That's a striking claim, and until now it has been almost entirely untested directly, because doing so requires two extremely hard things simultaneously: precisely measuring which way large numbers of real galaxies are actually spinning today (which needs resolved velocity maps, not just a single average spectrum per galaxy), and independently reconstructing what the *primordial* gravitational environment around each of those specific galaxies' birthplaces actually looked like — something nobody directly observed, and which has to be inferred mathematically by essentially running the movie of cosmic structure formation in reverse from today's galaxy map. This paper does both, using integral-field spectroscopy from the MaNGA survey (which maps rotation across a galaxy's whole visible face) for the observed side, and a large, previously built reconstruction of the primordial density field (called ELUCID) for the predicted side — then asks, across hundreds of real galaxies, whether the two independently derived spin directions actually line up more than random chance would allow.

**Paper Type & Stakes.** This is a primarily **observational test of a long-standing theoretical prediction** — not a new theory, and not a simulation study, but a direct empirical confrontation between a 1969-vintage theoretical mechanism and real galaxy data. The stakes: if galaxy spins genuinely retain a measurable, directional fingerprint of the primordial gravitational field, that opens up spin direction as an entirely new observable for cosmology — a way to extract information about the early universe beyond the standard toolkit of galaxy clustering statistics, with the paper specifically flagging neutrino-mass measurement as a downstream application worth pursuing.

**Prior Belief Check.** This is *not* a surprise to specialists in the sense of overturning consensus — tidal-torque theory itself is decades-old, well-established theory, and this isn't the very first claimed detection of a spin–initial-condition correlation (see the Replication Note below). What is genuinely notable to the field is the *strength* and *specificity* of this particular detection: a ~7σ signal, isolated to one specific galaxy population and component (gas in central massive ellipticals) in a way that also passes an internal physical consistency check (the signal's characteristic spatial scale grows with halo mass exactly as TTT predicts it should) — a substantially sharper and more targeted result than earlier, more tentative claims in this research area.

**Replication & Convergence Note.** This detection is not independent of prior work in the most important sense: it comes from largely the same research group and collaborators (several of the current authors, including the corresponding author, co-authored a 2021 paper in this same journal, *Nature Astronomy* — Motloch, Yu, Pen & Xie 2021, "An observed correlation between galaxy spins and initial conditions" — already claiming evidence for a spin–initial-condition link). This paper should be read as an escalation and refinement of that group's own ongoing research program (using a richer galaxy sample, IFU-resolved gas-versus-stellar spins, and morphology splitting) rather than a first-ever, independently-arrived-at detection. Genuinely independent confirmation — a different team, an independently built reconstruction, and/or an independent galaxy survey finding the same gas-in-ellipticals signal — does not yet appear to exist, though a different, related large-scale galaxy-orientation measurement from an unaffiliated team was posted only months earlier (da Silveira Ferreira et al. 2025, "Where galaxies point: first measurement of the large-scale axial intrinsic alignment") — a related but distinct phenomenon (large-scale axial alignment, not this paper's protohalo tidal-torque spin-direction correlation), worth knowing about as adjacent context rather than as independent replication of this specific claim.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Where galaxy spin was thought to come from, before tidal-torque theory.** In an idealized, perfectly smooth early universe, a collapsing blob of matter has no reason to rotate at all — rotation requires some source of torque. Tidal-torque theory (Peebles 1969; Doroshkevich 1970; White 1984, building on Hoyle's original 1949 idea) supplies that source: it's not that anything spins the protohalo "on purpose," it's that gravity from the surrounding, unevenly distributed matter pulls on different parts of the (not-quite-spherical) collapsing patch slightly differently, and that differential pull is a torque, purely as a geometric consequence of the protohalo's shape not lining up with the surrounding pull's own geometry.

> **Analogy:** picture an unevenly loaded flywheel — one whose mass is distributed a bit lopsidedly — sitting in a directional pulling field (like a fan blowing air, or a magnet's field) whose "grain" doesn't line up with the flywheel's own long axis. Even though the pulling field itself has no rotation built into it, the mismatch between "which way the flywheel is heavy" and "which way the field pulls hardest" creates a net twisting force — the flywheel starts to spin, and which way it spins is set entirely by the specific geometric mismatch between those two things. A protohalo works the same way: its own lopsided mass distribution (formally, an "inertia tensor") sits inside an external gravitational stretching field (a "tidal tensor"), and wherever those two shapes' natural axes don't align, a torque — and eventually a spin — results.
>
> **Breaks when:** pushed to imply the flywheel/protohalo has some fixed, rigid shape the way a real flywheel does. A protohalo is a diffuse, still-collapsing cloud of matter, not a rigid body — the "shape" being torqued is a statistical description of a mass distribution mid-collapse, not a solid object, and the torquing process operates continuously as that shape itself keeps evolving, rather than as a one-time kick.

**Why testing this needs "rewinding the movie": the ELUCID reconstruction.** You can't observe the primordial gravitational field directly — by definition, it existed roughly 13 billion years before any telescope existed. What you *can* do is take today's map of where galaxies actually sit in space (from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey), and — because gravity's role in growing structure over cosmic time is a known, largely deterministic physical process — statistically infer what smooth, gently perturbed initial density field, evolved forward under known gravitational physics, would most plausibly have produced today's observed structure. That's what the ELUCID project does: a Bayesian reconstruction (technically, a Hamiltonian Monte Carlo search using particle-mesh gravity dynamics) that infers the primordial density field consistent with the real, observed local universe.

> **Analogy:** this is conceptually the same move as reconstructing an application's initial memory state from a sequence of transaction logs, by running the known, deterministic state-transition rules backward (or by searching forward from many candidate starting states until one reproduces the observed final state) — a form of state reconstruction via a known forward model, rather than a direct backward "undo." You don't observe the initial state directly; you infer the initial state that a known, trusted forward process would have had to start from to land on the final state you *can* observe.
>
> **Breaks when:** pushed to imply this reconstruction is exact or unique the way replaying a deterministic transaction log is. Cosmic structure formation is only cleanly reversible on large, still-close-to-linear scales; once matter has undergone enough nonlinear gravitational collapse (particle orbits crossing, structures merging), the "log" effectively loses information — which is exactly why the paper finds the technique breaks down (a "decorrelation region") for smaller, lower-mass halos, where nonlinear evolution has scrambled more of the primordial information than the reconstruction can recover.

**Measuring which way a real galaxy is actually spinning.** A galaxy's rotation axis can be inferred from how its light is Doppler-shifted across its visible face — one side approaching, receding on the other. Older surveys typically captured only a single spectrum per galaxy (like taking one pixel's reading and inferring an entire image), but MaNGA is an integral-field-unit (IFU) survey: it captures a full 2D velocity map across each galaxy's face, letting the rotation axis (and hence spin direction) be fit directly and far more reliably. This is done separately for the galaxy's **gas** (traced by emission from ionized hydrogen, Hα) and its **stars** (traced by the bulk motion imprinted in stellar absorption features) — two components that don't have to spin the same way, since gas can be added to or stripped from a galaxy well after it originally formed, while the stars' motion more directly reflects the galaxy's older dynamical history.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### Building the two independent spin estimates

For each of several hundred galaxies, the study builds two separate estimates of "which way this galaxy's angular momentum points," derived from completely independent inputs, then checks whether they agree more than chance would allow:

- **The observed spin ($j_G$)** — measured directly from MaNGA's resolved gas and stellar velocity maps (position-angle fitting via the PAFIT software package), restricted to **central** galaxies only (i.e., the dominant galaxy of its own dark matter halo, not a smaller satellite orbiting within a bigger one — centrals have a cleaner, more direct relationship to "their own" protohalo's history), cross-matched against a galaxy group catalog to identify each one's host halo. Quality cuts remove galaxies with poor gas signal or large position-angle fitting uncertainty (over 20°), leaving 781 central elliptical galaxies and 815 central spiral galaxies with reliable gas-spin measurements (numbers confirmed directly from the paper's Extended Data captions).
- **The reconstruction-predicted spin ($j_R$)** — computed by applying tidal-torque theory's own directional prediction to the ELUCID-reconstructed primordial density field at each galaxy's specific protohalo location, using a tidal tensor smoothed over some chosen spatial scale $r$. Because bigger halos should, in principle, trace back to correspondingly bigger patches of the primordial density field, the theory predicts the *best-fit* smoothing scale $r_{\rm opt}$ should scale with halo mass — roughly $r_{\rm opt} \approx 0.8 \times r_q$, where $r_q$ is the halo's estimated Lagrangian (primordial, pre-collapse) radius.

### The correlation statistic, and what "7σ" actually measures

The core comparison is a simple, well-defined statistic: the mean cosine of the angle between the observed and predicted 2D spin directions,

$$
\mu = \langle \cos\theta_{GR} \rangle = \left\langle \frac{\vec{j}_G \cdot \vec{j}_R}{|\vec{j}_G||\vec{j}_R|} \right\rangle
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\mu$ — the correlation statistic itself: the average, over many galaxies, of how well the observed and predicted spin directions line up.
- $\vec{j}_G$, $\vec{j}_R$ — the observed (gas or stellar) spin direction and the theoretically predicted spin direction, respectively, both treated as 2D on-sky vectors.
- $\theta_{GR}$ — the angle between them for a given galaxy.

**What this actually means:** if the observed and predicted spins were completely unrelated, this average would come out to zero over a large enough sample (random 2D vectors have no preferred relative angle) — a nonzero, positive $\mu$ means the two independently-derived directions are, on average, tipped toward agreement rather than pointing in unrelated random directions.

The headline result: for **gas in central elliptical galaxies**, this correlation peaks at $\mu = 0.116$ at an optimal smoothing scale of $r_{\rm opt} = 3.4$ Mpc/h. To turn that number into a statement about statistical confidence, the authors compare it against a null distribution built by randomly shuffling which observed spin gets paired with which predicted spin 40,000 times (destroying any real physical correlation while preserving everything else about the data), then combine the resulting significance across different halo-mass bins by taking the root-mean-square of the individual bins' significances — arriving at an overall detection significance of roughly 7σ, meaning a result this strong would be extremely unlikely to arise from pure chance under the assumption that gas spins and reconstructed primordial tidal fields are actually unrelated.

For comparison, central **spiral** galaxies show "much weaker" correlations than ellipticals, and **stellar** spins (in both morphological types) are generally less correlated with the reconstruction than gas spins are — though the retrieved material reports these comparisons qualitatively rather than giving separate sigma values for each, a specific gap flagged again in Section 6.

### Why gas in ellipticals specifically — the physical interpretation

This is the paper's central interpretive move, and it's subtler than "gas is somehow a purer primordial signal." Elliptical galaxies are typically old stellar systems that exhausted or lost most of the cold gas they were originally born with; the gas an elliptical galaxy has *today* is disproportionately likely to be gas accreted more recently from its surrounding large-scale environment, rather than gas that's been part of the galaxy since formation. Because that gas's angular momentum is set by ongoing interaction with the surrounding environment — an environment whose large-scale geometry is itself still linked to (though not identical to) the primordial tidal field being reconstructed — its spin direction ends up tracking the reconstructed primordial tidal field more faithfully than the *stars'* spin does. The stars' spin instead reflects a much longer, more dynamically processed history (the original formation-era torque, plus subsequent mergers, secular evolution, and other complications), which dilutes the correlation with any single, cleanly-reconstructable primordial signal. In short: the strongest correlation shows up not in the component that's "been there longest," but in the component that's most actively, continuously coupled to the surrounding large-scale structure.

### Internal consistency check: mass-dependent smoothing scale

Beyond simply detecting a correlation, the authors show that — for halos above about $10^{12.5}\,M_\odot$ — the optimal smoothing scale $r_{\rm opt}$ increases systematically with halo mass, tracking the theoretically expected $r_{\rm opt} \approx 0.8\,r_q$ relation. This matters as a genuine validation, not just a curiosity: it means the detected signal isn't just "some large-scale alignment," it's an alignment that behaves exactly the way tidal-torque theory specifically predicts it should as a function of halo size — a much stronger form of evidence than a single overall correlation number alone would be. Below that mass threshold, the correlation weakens, which the authors attribute to reconstruction breakdown (Lagrangian remapping errors and reconstruction uncertainty growing large for less massive, more nonlinearly evolved protohalos) rather than to the underlying physical effect itself vanishing — a distinction examined further in Extended Data Fig. 3, where restricting the spiral-galaxy sample to a narrower, higher-mass band recovers a detectable correlation, though still weaker than ellipticals' at matched halo mass.

### Assumption Audit

- **Watch:** it's tempting to read this as "each galaxy's spin points where the theory predicted." It doesn't work that way — $\mu = 0.116$ is a modest population-average excess over zero, not a close per-galaxy match; most individual galaxies' observed and predicted spin directions do **not** closely align, and the correlation is only statistically detectable when averaged across hundreds of galaxies and compared to a random-shuffle null.
- **Watch:** "the primordial tidal field" sounds like something that was measured. It wasn't — it's a Bayesian statistical inference (ELUCID) built by finding an initial density field that, evolved forward under known gravitational physics, best reproduces the *present-day* observed galaxy distribution. It's a well-motivated, physically grounded inference, not a direct observation of the early universe.
- **Watch:** the finding that gas correlates better than stars in ellipticals can misread as "gas retained primordial information better." The paper's own interpretation is closer to the opposite: elliptical gas correlates best because it's largely *externally, recently* accreted and continuously tracks the (still partially primordial-linked) surrounding large-scale environment — not because it's a more pristine, untouched relic of the original formation-era torquing event.
- **Watch:** "μ = 0.116" and "7σ" answer two different questions and shouldn't be merged into one impression. μ describes the *size* of the effect (modest); 7σ describes how *statistically confident* the detection of that modest effect is (very high), given the specific null-hypothesis test and mass-bin-combination method used. A small effect size and a high-confidence detection are not in tension — but reading either number as standing in for the other invites either "the effect must be huge" or "the effect must be shaky," and it's neither.

## 5. What's Genuinely New

Two things distinguish this from the group's own prior work in this area (see the Replication Note in Section 2), which is the right comparison point given the direct authorship overlap. First, the **sharpness and specificity of the detection**: rather than a single aggregate spin-correlation claim, this paper isolates *where* the signal actually lives — gas, not stars; ellipticals, not spirals; central galaxies specifically — and offers a physically motivated account of *why* it concentrates there (the externally-accreted-gas argument above), turning a broad correlation claim into a much more falsifiable, structured one. Second, the **internal consistency check** via the mass-dependent optimal smoothing scale: showing that the signal's own characteristic scale moves the way tidal-torque theory specifically predicts as halo mass changes is a genuinely more demanding test than a single correlation-and-significance number, since a spurious or purely systematic alignment (an instrumental or reconstruction artifact, say) would have no particular reason to reproduce that specific scale-mass relationship.

**Predictive Content Check.**

1. **Falsifiable handle (always):** the paper makes two distinct, checkable predictions. First, the general one: applying the same method to an independently constructed dataset (a different reconstructed volume, or a different, larger IFU survey such as future DESI-era data) should reproduce a comparable gas-in-central-ellipticals correlation, concentrated at a similarly mass-dependent optimal smoothing scale — a null result, or a signal that doesn't track the predicted $r_{\rm opt} \propto$ (halo mass) relation, would directly undercut the claim. Second, a narrower one already partially tested within this paper itself: restricting to higher-mass halos (above the ~$10^{12.5}\,M_\odot$ "decorrelation" threshold) should reveal a real, if weaker, correlation even in spiral galaxies — which Extended Data Fig. 3 reports finding, itself a small internal falsification test that passed.
2. **Formalism load (conditional — this paper leans on real reconstruction and statistical machinery, so this fires):** the reconstruction (ELUCID) and the Monte Carlo significance-testing pipeline are unambiguously load-bearing — there's no way to test a *directional* theoretical prediction like tidal-torque theory without both an independently derived predicted direction and a rigorous null-hypothesis comparison, and neither is decorative. That said, the specific **7σ headline number** is somewhat method-dependent in a way worth flagging separately from the underlying signal's reality: it comes from a particular choice (root-mean-square combination of significances computed within separate halo-mass bins), which is a defensible but not the only possible way to aggregate the evidence — a different combination scheme applied to the identical underlying data could plausibly shift the exact reported sigma value, even though the qualitative conclusion (a real, mass-scale-consistent correlation exists) would likely survive.

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

Tidal-torque theory has, since the late 1960s and 1970s, proposed a specific mechanical origin for galaxy spin: the gravitational tug of a lumpy, non-uniform early universe on a not-quite-spherical patch of collapsing matter (a protohalo), torquing it into rotation as it collapses, in a way that should leave the resulting spin *direction* traceable back to the geometry of the surrounding primordial gravitational (tidal) field. This paper tests that prediction directly by comparing two independently derived estimates of galaxy spin direction for several hundred nearby galaxies: the actually-observed spin direction, measured from resolved velocity maps in the MaNGA integral-field spectroscopic survey (separately for gas and stars), and a theoretically predicted spin direction, computed by applying tidal-torque theory to a statistically reconstructed primordial density field (ELUCID) built by inferring, from today's observed galaxy map, what initial conditions would have evolved into it under known gravitational physics.

Among several galaxy-type and component combinations tested, the strongest signal by far comes from the gas component of central, massive elliptical galaxies: a correlation of $\mu = 0.116$ between observed and predicted spin direction at an optimal reconstruction smoothing scale of 3.4 Mpc/h, detected at roughly 7σ significance against a random-shuffle null. The signal passes a nontrivial internal consistency test — its characteristic spatial scale grows with halo mass in the way tidal-torque theory specifically predicts — and the authors interpret its concentration in elliptical *gas* specifically (rather than stars, or spiral galaxies generally) as reflecting that ellipticals' present-day gas is disproportionately externally accreted, and therefore more directly coupled to the surrounding large-scale environment than the more dynamically processed stellar component is. Spiral galaxies and stellar spins in both morphological types show weaker correlations in the full sample, though restricting to higher-mass halos recovers a detectable (if still weaker than ellipticals') correlation even for spirals — evidence that at least part of the weaker full-sample signal in lower-mass systems reflects the reconstruction method's own known breakdown at small scales, not necessarily a purely physical absence of the effect there.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **A 55-year-old theory about where galaxy spin comes from — a gravitational torque from the lumpy early universe — has now been tested directly, by comparing real galaxies' actual spin directions against spin directions predicted from a mathematical "rewind" of cosmic history, and the two agree more than chance would allow, at roughly 7-sigma confidence.**
2. **The signal isn't spread evenly across all galaxies — it's concentrated specifically in the gas (not the stars) of massive elliptical galaxies, because that gas is thought to be recently, externally accreted and therefore still actively tracking the surrounding large-scale gravitational environment, unlike the more dynamically settled stellar population.**
3. **This isn't the field's first claim of this kind — it's the same research program's own escalation of an earlier 2021 result — but it's a substantially sharper one, passing an added internal consistency check (the signal's characteristic scale grows with halo mass exactly as the theory predicts) that makes it harder to dismiss as a statistical fluke or generic large-scale alignment artifact.**

## 9. Shorter Summary

*(For quick reference or sharing — full nuance lives above.)*

Since the late 1960s, cosmologists have had a specific theory for why galaxies spin at all: as the raw material for a galaxy's dark matter halo collapses in the early universe, uneven gravitational pulls from the surrounding matter torque it into rotation — meaning a galaxy's spin direction should, in principle, still encode information about the gravitational environment it was born in, over 13 billion years ago. Testing this directly is hard, because it requires both precisely measuring how real galaxies spin today and independently reconstructing what that primordial gravitational environment actually looked like, since nobody could observe it directly.

This study does both: it measures spin directions for hundreds of nearby galaxies using detailed velocity maps from the MaNGA survey, and compares them against spin directions predicted by applying the theory to a mathematically reconstructed primordial density field built from today's galaxy map. The two independently derived spin directions turn out to agree significantly more than random chance would allow — most strongly for the gas (not the stars) inside massive elliptical galaxies, at a confidence level of about 7 standard deviations. The authors argue this concentration makes physical sense: elliptical galaxies' present-day gas tends to be more recently and externally acquired, so it stays more closely coupled to the surrounding large-scale environment than the galaxy's older stellar population does. The result also passes a meaningful consistency check — the scale over which the correlation is strongest grows with the galaxy's total halo mass, exactly as the theory predicts it should.

This builds on, rather than being wholly independent from, the same research group's own earlier work making similar claims, so it's best understood as a sharpened, more specific version of an existing claim rather than a first-ever discovery out of nowhere. If it continues to hold up under independent testing, it points toward using galaxy spin direction as a genuinely new cosmological measurement — a way to extract additional information about the early universe, potentially including the mass of neutrinos, beyond what standard galaxy-clustering statistics alone can provide.
