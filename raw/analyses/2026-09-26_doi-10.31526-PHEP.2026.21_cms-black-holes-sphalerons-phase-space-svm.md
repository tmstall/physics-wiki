## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**"No Black Holes at 11 TeV: CMS Measures Distances Between Whole Collision Events"**

CMS searched the full Run-2 dataset for the fireball-like decays of microscopic black holes and electroweak sphalerons and found nothing. What is new is the method: each event is mapped to a point on the geometric manifold of N-body phase space, and a support vector machine classifies events by distance on that manifold.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is a short experimental conference proceedings. It reports a null search for two exotic high-multiplicity signals and introduces a new event representation for machine-learning classification. The physics at stake is a narrow corner: TeV-scale gravity models are already heavily constrained. The method is the more transferable part of the paper.

In ordinary 3+1 dimensions, a black hole from a 13 TeV collision would need a radius of about 10⁻⁵⁰ m, fifteen orders of magnitude below the Planck length, so colliders have no reach. The ADD scenario (Arkani-Hamed, Dimopoulos, Dvali, 1998) changes this. Gravity spreads into *n* large extra dimensions, and the fundamental gravity scale M_D drops from about 10¹⁹ GeV to possibly a few TeV. The Schwarzschild radius then grows to about 10⁻²⁰ m, which is within reach of parton collisions at the LHC. Such a black hole would evaporate almost instantly by Hawking radiation into many high-energy particles spread evenly in all directions: jets, leptons, photons and missing momentum. The Randall–Sundrum models (refs. 5 and 6) belong to the same family.

Sphalerons are a separate idea with the same signature. They are saddle-point field configurations of the Standard Model electroweak theory. A transition over the sphaleron barrier changes the Chern–Simons number by ±1. That violates baryon number B and lepton number L while conserving B−L, and it creates one left-handed fermion from each doublet in every generation (12 fermions at once). The barrier is about 9 TeV high. Whether a collision at that energy can actually cross it is theoretically contested: Tye and Wong (2015) argued the rate is unsuppressed, and most of the community expects it to stay exponentially suppressed. The search therefore bounds a "pre-exponential factor" (PEF) instead of testing a firm prediction.

Before this analysis, the best CMS limits came from the 2016 data (35.9 fb⁻¹). Semiclassical black holes were excluded up to 10.1 TeV and the sphaleron PEF was bounded below 0.021. That search used simple cuts on the scalar-sum transverse momentum S_T and the object count N. This paper uses the full 138 fb⁻¹, adds a transverse-sphericity cut, and replaces "count and sum" with an SVM that works on phase-space distances between events. Those distances come from Larkoski and Melia (2020) and Cai, Cheng, Craig, Koszegi and Larkoski (2024).

**Prior Belief Check:** The null result matches mainstream expectations exactly, and no expert will be surprised by it. The exclusion gains, from about 10.1 to 11.4 TeV and a PEF roughly 6–8× tighter, are incremental and are what four times more data plus a better selection should deliver. The only part that might interest experts is methodological: this appears to be the first time an LHC experiment has used the Larkoski–Melia/Cai et al. phase-space metric in a real search, rather than in a phenomenology study.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** The physics conclusion (no microscopic black holes below about 9–11 TeV) is independently supported by earlier CMS and ATLAS Run-2 searches that used different techniques. The phase-space-distance/SVM method itself has been used only in this single CMS analysis. Independent confirmation of the *method's* value would mean ATLAS, or a CMS reanalysis, running the same metric against a conventional selection on the same signals and reporting the sensitivity gain directly.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**1. A geometric representation of the event, with the classifier working on distances.** An event with N massless final-state objects occupies a point on the N-body phase-space manifold. Larkoski and Melia showed that this manifold factorizes as the product of an (N−1)-simplex (energy fractions) and a (2N−3)-sphere (directions). CMS pads every event to N = 30 objects, so each event becomes a point in a space of about 29 + 57 = 86 dimensions. The distance between two events is a weighted combination of their simplex distance and their sphere distance:

$$
d_\Pi = \sqrt{\alpha\, d_\Delta^{2} + \beta\, d_S^{2}}
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $d_\Pi$ : total phase-space distance between events A and B (dimensionless)
- $d_\Delta$ : distance on the simplex, which describes how differently the two events share their energy among objects
- $d_S$ : distance on the hypersphere, which describes how differently their momentum directions are arranged
- $\alpha, \beta$ : fixed weights that depend on N and a reweighting constant c, chosen so that the combined distance is a valid phase-space metric

**What this actually means:** Think of a similarity-search index over fixed-width records. Every event becomes an 86-field vector in a coordinate system that respects the physics (energy is shared on a simplex, directions live on a sphere), and "how alike are two events" becomes a lookup of distance between two records. The SVM then learns a decision boundary in that geometry. With 10,000 training events, the pairwise kernel matrix has 10⁸ entries (Figure 3 is literally a 2×2 block view of it). That O(N²) memory cost explains why training stays at 10k events.

*New to the field versus new to the reader:* SVMs and distance-based classifiers are old (Cortes and Vapnik, 1995) and familiar from a CS background. What is new to the field is feeding them a *Lorentz-structured* phase-space metric instead of raw four-vectors or hand-picked observables.

**2. One threshold works across every model.** A single SVM, trained on a mixture of B1 (non-rotating, n = 2) black-hole mass points, uses one global cut at 0.63. That cut performs acceptably across all BlackMax and CHARYBDIS2 variants and for sphalerons, a signal the classifier was never trained on. Moving the cut between 0.5 and 0.7 changes the signal yield by only 4–25%. Because one cut serves many models, a single PASS/FAIL data-driven background estimate covers all of them. That is a real simplification of the analysis.

### Predictive Content Check

**Falsifiable handle:** This is a search, so its exposure is direct and it did make a prediction that could have failed. If ADD black holes existed with M_D of about 2–9 TeV and minimum masses below about 9–11 TeV, the PASS region (SVM > 0.63, S > 0.1, N > 3) would show an excess in the S_T tail above about 8 TeV over the exponential transfer-factor prediction. No excess appeared. The sphaleron handle is weaker: the analysis bounds a phenomenological fraction (PEF < 0.0025 in the proceedings, < 0.0034 in the final paper) rather than testing a calculated rate, because theory does not supply a firm rate.

**Formalism load (fires):** The paper presents the phase-space geometry as its novelty, so the question is whether the geometry does the work. The proceedings contains **no ablation**. It never compares the SVM on d_Π against the same SVM on raw four-vectors, or against the 2016 cut-based S_T + N selection, at the same luminosity. The "more interpretable than a neural network" claim is also not demonstrated, since an SVM with a kernel over an 86-dimensional metric is not obviously easier to interpret. A rough check of how much the method adds is in the box below.

> **Worked check: how much of the sphaleron gain is luminosity, and how much is method?**
>
> **Define.** For a counting search, the upper limit on the signal fraction f scales as 1/L when the search is background-free and as 1/√L when it is background-dominated.
>
> **Tiny example.** Luminosity ratio: 138 / 35.9 = 3.84.
> - Background-free scaling: 0.021 / 3.84 = **0.0055**
> - Background-dominated scaling: 0.021 / √3.84 = 0.021 / 1.96 = **0.0107**

>

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**L1. The semiclassical black-hole approximation fails in the region being excluded.** At large M_D (6–9 TeV), the excluded minimum masses (9–10 TeV) sit at M_BH/M_D ≈ 1–1.5. A black hole is only described reliably by semiclassical gravity when it is several times heavier than M_D, so these limits are really limits on generator ansätze near the quantum-gravity regime. **(A) Consensus**: the requirement M_BH ≫ M_D is standard in the collider black-hole literature, and earlier CMS papers state it explicitly. **(broader literature)**

**L2. The flagship benchmark is already disfavored.** Training and most of the displays use n = 2 with M_D of a few TeV. Supernova-cooling and neutron-star-heating bounds already push M_D for n = 2 far above the LHC's reach. The numerically strongest collider limits therefore fall in a corner that astrophysics has already closed. **(A) Consensus**: astrophysical bounds on n = 2 ADD are well established and uncontroversial. **(broader literature)**

**L3. The proceedings numbers are superseded, and one is internally inconsistent.** The proceedings reports black-hole exclusion at 9.0–11.4 TeV and PEF < 0.0025. The final JHEP paper reports 8.4–11.4 TeV and PEF < 0.0034, and adds string balls (9.0–10.7 TeV). Within the proceedings, the abstract's 0.0025 at 9 TeV also falls outside its own summary range of "5×10⁻⁴ to 2×10⁻³" for the PEF. **(A) Consensus**: this is a direct document comparison, not a judgment call. I did not read the JHEP full text, so the reason for the shift (updated systematics, different models or a changed threshold) is unconfirmed. **(paper §9; broader literature)**

**L4. The method's gain is not isolated.** The proceedings gives no ablation of phase-space distance against conventional inputs, and no quantitative support for the interpretability claim. The worked check above suggests a gain of roughly 1.5–2× that cannot be separated from the sphericity cut. **(C) Speculative**: the JHEP paper may include this comparison, and I could not verify either way from its abstract. **(analyst inference)**

**L5. The zero-padding and massless-object details are unclear.** Padding every event to N = 30 with zero-momentum objects puts events on the phase-space manifold, but directions for zero-energy objects are undefined, so how they are coordinated matters. The text says an event's distance to itself is "small but nonzero because of zero-padding." For a true metric that distance is exactly zero, so the padding scheme breaks the metric property, or Figure 3's diagonal shows something other than self-distances. The manifold also assumes massless objects, and how missing transverse momentum enters is not stated. **(C) Speculative**: the proceedings points to ref. 34 for details, and a specialist may know this is harmless. **(analyst inference)**

**L6. The background model extrapolates from a validation region with different topology.** The PASS/FAIL ratio R_P/F is modeled as an exponential in S_T and validated in the inverted-sphericity region (S < 0.1). The signal region selects isotropic events, so its topology differs from the validation sample. The analysis is statistically limited, which dampens this concern but does not remove it. **(C) Speculative**: the paper reports good validation pulls, so this is a residual concern, not a demonstrated flaw. **(paper §7; analyst inference)**

**L7. The sphaleron interpretation depends on a contested model.** Whether sphaleron transitions are unsuppressed at 9 TeV is disputed (Tye–Wong against the conventional expectation of exponential suppression). BaryoGEN also omits gauge-boson emission, which the authors call conservative. The PEF limit is meaningful only within that modeling frame. **(B) Contested**: reasonable theorists disagree about whether any observable rate exists at LHC energies. **(paper §3; broader literature)**

**L8. The "number of extra dimensions" framing overreaches.** Figure 9 plots excluded n on a grid of simulated points (n = 2, 4, 6), so it shows interpolation plus underflow and overflow points, not a continuous constraint on n. The text concludes that "the only possible extra dimension allowed is 1," but n = 1 ADD with TeV M_D is independently excluded because it would change gravity at solar-system distances. **(A) Consensus**: the n = 1 exclusion is textbook ADD phenomenology. **(broader literature; paper Fig. 9 caption)**

**L9. The kinematic reach is nearly exhausted.** The limits of about 11 TeV sit close to the 13 TeV beam energy, where parton luminosities fall steeply. Run 3 (13.6 TeV) and the HL-LHC mostly add luminosity, which buys only a few hundred GeV of mass reach. Real gains require higher collision energy. **(A) Consensus**: this is the standard behavior of steeply falling parton luminosities at a collider's energy frontier. **(broader literature)**

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **The LHC has found no microscopic black holes below about 9–11 TeV and no sphalerons, using its full 2016–2018 dataset.** TeV-scale gravity keeps losing room, and at 13 TeV the collider is close to the most it can reach.
2. **The novelty is geometry, not physics.** CMS turned every collision into a point on the manifold of N-body phase space and let an SVM classify events by distance. It is an elegant, physically structured input that generalized to a signal it was never trained on.
3. **The proceedings does not prove the method is better.** No head-to-head test is shown, and rough arithmetic credits the new selection with only about a 1.5–2× gain beyond what four times more data provides. The final JHEP paper has already revised the headline numbers.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Some theories propose that gravity leaks into extra spatial dimensions. If so, the true strength of gravity could be reached at energies of a few trillion electron-volts, and the Large Hadron Collider could create tiny black holes. Such a black hole would evaporate instantly into a spray of dozens of particles flying out evenly in all directions. A different Standard Model process, the sphaleron, would leave a similar spray while briefly violating the conservation of matter particles.

The CMS experiment searched its complete 2016–2018 dataset for these sprays. Its new tool treats each collision as a single point in a high-dimensional geometric space built from how the event's energy and directions are shared among its particles. A support vector machine, a classic classifier that draws a boundary between two groups of points, learned to separate black-hole-like events from ordinary particle jets by their distances in this space. One cutoff worked for every black-hole model tested, and also for sphalerons, which the classifier never saw during training.

No excess appeared. Black holes lighter than about 9 to 11 trillion electron-volts are ruled out in these models, roughly one trillion electron-volts beyond the earlier CMS limit. At most a few thousandths of very high-energy quark collisions can produce sphalerons, several times tighter than before.

Three caveats matter. The strongest limits sit where the semiclassical black-hole description is least reliable, and the main benchmark model is already disfavored by astrophysics. The proceedings never compares the new method against a conventional one, and simple arithmetic suggests it adds a modest gain beyond the fourfold increase in data. The final journal paper has already shifted the headline numbers, so those numbers should be the ones quoted.

The result is a clean, expected null result. It closes more of the room left for TeV-scale gravity and gives experiments a new, physics-shaped representation of collision data to test further.

## Appendix — Deep-Dive Discussion Log (2026-09-27)

### Deep dive 1: Electroweak sphalerons

*Prompt: "electroweak sphalerons" (paper §2; analysis §2, §5 and L7).*
*Calibration: electroweak gauge theory is tier 3, so it is built from first principles here. The main bridge is transition-state theory, which is foundational chemistry.*

#### 1. The electroweak vacuum is a ladder of states, not a single state

In the electroweak theory, the lowest-energy field configuration is not unique. The SU(2) weak gauge field has infinitely many classical vacua, all at exactly zero energy. Each carries an integer label, the **Chern–Simons number N_CS**. That integer counts how many times the gauge-field configuration wraps around the SU(2) group manifold (topologically a 3-sphere) as you sweep over all of space. Neighboring vacua have the same energy but different topology. You cannot deform N_CS = 0 into N_CS = 1 while staying at zero energy, because every path between them passes through configurations with real field energy. That is the barrier.

> **Analogy:** A rubber band wrapped around a broom handle *n* times. Each wrap count is a stable resting state. To go from one wrap to zero you must lift the band off the handle, which costs energy; it cannot slide off while lying flat.
>
> **Breaks when:** you take the wrapping literally. The band wraps in ordinary space, where you can see it. N_CS counts wrapping in an abstract internal space (the SU(2) group). The intermediate configurations are not stretched versions of the same object; they are real lumps of Higgs and gauge-field energy concentrated in a region about 1/M_W across.

#### 2. The sphaleron is the transition state

> **Concept:** Between neighboring vacua N_CS = n and n+1, the minimum-energy path peaks at a static, unstable solution of the classical field equations: the **sphaleron** (Klinkhamer & Manton 1984; the name is Greek for "ready to fall"). It sits at N_CS = n + ½. It has exactly one unstable direction, along N_CS, and is stable in all others, so it is a first-order saddle point. Its energy, E_sph ≈ 9 TeV, is the barrier height. There are three ways across:
> 1. **Quantum tunneling at zero temperature** (the instanton, 't Hooft 1976). This is suppressed by a factor of about 10⁻¹⁶².
> 2. **Thermal activation in the hot early universe.** Above the electroweak crossover the Higgs vacuum expectation value shrinks toward zero, the barrier collapses with it, and crossing is unsuppressed.
> 3. **Collision-induced crossing at a collider** with parton collision energy √ŝ > E_sph. Whether this is suppressed is unresolved.
>
> **Vehicle:** A chemical reaction on a potential-energy surface. Reactant and product are minima. The transition state is a first-order saddle on the minimum-energy path, with one imaginary-frequency mode (the reaction coordinate). There are three ways across: tunneling through the barrier (hydrogen transfer at cryogenic temperature); thermal activation over it (Arrhenius or Eyring kinetics, with rate proportional to $e^{-E_a/RT}$); and non-thermal activation by a single energetic collision, where it matters whether the energy lands in the reaction-coordinate mode (mode-specific chemistry).
>
> **Analogy:** Vacuum n ↔ reactant well. Vacuum n+1 ↔ product well; the two are degenerate, like a thermoneutral identity reaction such as Cl⁻ + CH₃Cl → ClCH₃ + Cl⁻. N_CS ↔ reaction coordinate. Sphaleron ↔ transition state. E_sph ↔ activation energy E_a. Instanton ↔ tunneling. Early-universe hopping ↔ the Arrhenius regime. Collider crossing ↔ single-collision excitation.
>
> **Breaks when:** you ask what distinguishes product from reactant. In chemistry it is nuclear geometry. Here it is **the occupation of the fermion vacuum**: crossing one rung creates 12 fermions (section 3). A potential-energy surface has no slot for a reaction coordinate whose traversal changes the particle content of the universe by a fixed counting rule. That comes from a quantum anomaly, not from the barrier's shape. The ladder is also infinite and periodic, unlike a two-well reaction, and that periodicity is exactly what the Tye–Wong argument (section 4) relies on.

**Worked box: why the barrier is 9 TeV.**

$$
E_{\text{sph}} = \frac{4\pi v}{g}\, B
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $E_{\text{sph}}$ : sphaleron energy, the barrier height (GeV)
- $v$ : Higgs vacuum expectation value, 246 GeV
- $g$ : SU(2) weak coupling, about 0.65
- $B$ : dimensionless shape factor set by the Higgs self-coupling. It runs from about 1.56 (light Higgs) to 2.72 (infinitely heavy Higgs); about 1.9 for m_H = 125 GeV

**Numbers:** 4π × 246 = 3091 GeV. Dividing by 0.65 gives 4756 GeV. Multiplying by 1.9 gives **≈ 9.0 TeV**, the paper's nominal threshold.

**What this actually means:** Rewrite 4πv/g using M_W = gv/2 and α_W = g²/4π, and it becomes 2M_W/α_W. So E_sph ≈ 2B × M_W/α_W: the W mass (80 GeV) scaled up by the inverse weak coupling (1/α_W ≈ 30). A sphaleron is a *classical* lump of field about 1/M_W across carrying roughly 1/α_W ≈ 30 quanta' worth of energy. That "about 30 quanta, coherently arranged" fact is what makes collider production so hard (section 4).

#### 3. Why crossing violates baryon and lepton number (the anomaly)

In the classical theory, baryon number B and lepton number L are each conserved. Quantum mechanically, the weak force couples only to left-handed fermions, and that chiral coupling makes both currents *anomalous*. Each rung crossed changes them together:

$$
\Delta B = \Delta L = n_{\text{gen}}\, \Delta N_{CS} = 3\, \Delta N_{CS}
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\Delta B, \Delta L$ : change in baryon number and lepton number
- $n_{\text{gen}}$ : number of fermion generations, 3
- $\Delta N_{CS}$ : number of rungs crossed, ±1 for a single sphaleron transition

**What this actually means:** B and L move in lockstep, so **B − L is exactly conserved**, while B + L changes by 6 per rung. The mechanism is *level crossing*. Track the fermion energy levels in the background gauge field as it winds from N_CS = 0 to 1. Every left-handed SU(2) doublet's spectrum shifts up by one level, so exactly one state rises out of the filled negative-energy Dirac sea and becomes a real particle. Each generation has four doublets (three colors of quark doublet plus one lepton doublet), giving 12 across three generations: **9 quarks and 3 leptons**. That yields ΔB = 9 × ⅓ = 3 and ΔL = 3.

> **Analogy:** Twelve counters incremented together by one hardware event. The winding is the event, each doublet is a counter, and every counter advances by exactly one, so each emits one entry across the "hidden/visible" boundary.
>
> **Breaks when:** you look for the stored entries. Nothing was buffered beforehand. "Creation" here means the vacuum itself shifting which states count as particles; there is no queue being drained.

**Worked example: a collider final state** (ΔN_CS = −1, the standard illustration in Ellis & Sakurai 2016, the paper's ref. [15]):

u + u → e⁺ μ⁺ τ⁺ + t̄ t̄ b̄ c̄ c̄ s̄ d̄ (+ W/Z/H bosons)

Checks, from initial state to final state:
- **Baryon number:** +⅔ → −7/3, so ΔB = −3 ✓
- **Lepton number:** 0 → −3, so ΔL = −3 ✓
- **Electric charge:** +4/3 → 3 − 4/3 + 1/3 − 4/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 = +4/3 ✓

The two incoming up quarks fill two of the 12 doublet slots, which leaves 10 hard final-state fermions. The antitops each decay to three more objects. The result is a spherical burst of well over ten energetic jets and leptons, the same signature as black-hole evaporation, which is why the paper reuses one analysis for both. The sign of ΔN_CS flips every particle to its antiparticle; the paper's p(N_CS) parameter mixes the +1 and −1 cases. That changes lepton charges and quark flavors but barely changes multiplicity, which is consistent with their similar SVM-score distributions.

#### 4. The collider crux: energy above the barrier is not enough

The zero-temperature tunneling suppression:

$$
\Gamma \propto e^{-\frac{4\pi}{\alpha_W} F(E/E_{\text{sph}})}
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\Gamma$ : transition rate
- $\alpha_W$ : weak fine-structure constant, g²/4π ≈ 0.034
- $F$ : the "holy grail function" of collision energy E in units of E_sph, with F(0) = 1

**Numbers:** At zero energy, with α_W = 0.0336, 4π/0.0336 = 374, and e⁻³⁷⁴ ≈ **10⁻¹⁶²**. Even if F dropped to 0.1 at LHC energies, the suppression would still be e⁻³⁷ ≈ 10⁻¹⁶.

**What this actually means:** The open question is whether F → 0 once E > E_sph, that is, whether suppression vanishes above the barrier. Semiclassical calculations for two-particle initial states suggest F stays well above zero even there. The reason is an **overlap problem**. Two partons concentrated at a point look nothing like a coherent configuration of about 30 quanta spread over a region 1/M_W across, and the quantum amplitude to go from one to the other is exponentially small.

Tye and Wong (2015) countered that the periodic ladder along N_CS produces Bloch-wave bands, as in a crystal, and that transmission above the barrier would then be unsuppressed. Critics reply that reducing the problem to a single N_CS coordinate discards exactly the many-particle overlap that causes the suppression.

> **Analogy:** Mode-specific chemistry. A large molecule struck by one fast atom may take on far more than E_a in total and still not react, if the energy lands in a bond that does not couple to the reaction coordinate.
>
> **Breaks when:** you wait. In a molecule, intramolecular vibrational redistribution eventually randomizes the energy (the RRKM picture), so the reaction recovers given time. A 2 → many scattering event has no "later": it is one quantum amplitude, and the suppression comes from wavefunction overlap, not from redistribution that is merely incomplete.

**Worked box: why LHC reach is marginal regardless.** Sphaleron production needs parton collision energy √ŝ > 9 TeV out of √s = 13 TeV. That requires the product of the two parton momentum fractions to satisfy x₁x₂ = ŝ/s ≥ (9/13)² = **0.48**: both quarks must each carry roughly 70% of their proton's momentum, a far corner of the parton distributions. That is why the rate the PEF multiplies is so small, and why a 100 TeV collider would matter far more than more luminosity at 13 TeV (compare L9).

**Reading the paper's limit.** The paper defines the sphaleron cross-section as PEF × σ(qq, √ŝ > 9 TeV). **PEF < 0.0025 means at most 0.25% of such collisions go over the barrier.** Conventional semiclassical estimates put the true fraction at 10⁻¹⁶ or below, so the LHC is testing only the "unsuppressed" (Tye–Wong-type) scenario, about 13 orders of magnitude above conventional expectations. This sharpens L7: the limit constrains a phenomenological knob, not a calculated Standard Model rate.

#### 5. The early universe: sphalerons certainly operate there, but they don't explain the asymmetry on their own

Above the electroweak crossover, thermal sphaleron transitions run fast and in equilibrium, with a rate per unit volume proportional to α_W⁵T⁴. They switch off ("freeze out") at T ≈ 130 GeV. Sakharov's three conditions for generating a matter–antimatter asymmetry are:

1. **B violation.** Sphalerons supply this. ✓
2. **C and CP violation.** The Standard Model's CKM phase is roughly ten orders of magnitude too weak. ✗
3. **Departure from thermal equilibrium.** With m_H = 125 GeV the electroweak transition is a smooth crossover, not the needed first-order transition. ✗

So Standard Model electroweak baryogenesis fails. Sphalerons still matter as a **converter**. Because they conserve B − L while erasing B + L, any B − L asymmetry created earlier (for example a lepton asymmetry from heavy-neutrino decays, i.e. leptogenesis) is partly converted into a baryon asymmetry. In the Standard Model, the final baryon number is about 28/79 of the initial B − L.

**Correction to the paper's framing:** "This mechanism can explain the matter-antimatter asymmetry problem" (paper §2) overstates the case. Sphalerons are a *necessary ingredient* in most baryogenesis scenarios, not an explanation by themselves.

#### 6. Tie-back to the paper

- **Why BaryoGEN neglecting gauge bosons is "conservative":** Semiclassical pictures have a real sphaleron decaying into order 1/α_W ≈ 30 W, Z and Higgs bosons as well as the 12 fermions. That would raise multiplicity and sphericity and make events easier to separate from QCD. Omitting the bosons simulates a less distinctive signal, which lowers efficiency and gives a weaker limit, hence conservative.
- **Why one analysis serves both black holes and sphalerons:** both produce more than ten hard, isotropically spread objects near the kinematic edge. This is the multiplicity and sphericity signature the SVM keys on.
- **L7, sharpened:** the observable is fixed by B − L-conserving counting rules, which are solid. The rate is the contested part, and the PEF limit sits many orders of magnitude above conventional expectations.

**Where I'm least confident in this deep dive:** The shape factor B ≈ 1.9 at m_H = 125 GeV, the ~130 GeV freeze-out temperature and the 28/79 conversion factor are standard values I recalled from memory, not checked this session. My summary of the Tye–Wong debate (Bloch bands against overlap suppression) is at the level of the argument, not the detailed calculations, and a specialist could point to more recent work that shifts the balance.

### Deep dive 2: Sphalerons rebuilt from the ground up

*Prompt: the reader recalled the ladder of vacua and the saddle between them, but many details of Deep dive 1 didn't land, and asked for a fuller explanation. This pass is pitched lower and built in five steps, each with a worked example that can be checked by hand.*

**The map.**
1. A field has a lowest-energy state, its vacuum.
2. The weak field has many vacua, labeled by a whole-number winding count.
3. Moving between neighboring vacua means crossing an energy barrier; the lowest crossing point is a saddle, the sphaleron.
4. Crossing it changes the amount of matter: 12 particles appear.
5. The hot early universe crossed it constantly; whether the LHC can is doubtful.

#### Step 1: A field is an array of values, and the vacuum is its zero-cost setting

A field is a value stored at every point in space. Picture a memory array with one cell per lattice site. The energy of any configuration is the sum of two costs:
- a **per-cell cost**: how far each cell sits from its preferred value;
- a **neighbor cost**: how much adjacent cells disagree.

The vacuum is whatever configuration makes the total zero. For the simplest field, that is just "every cell holds its preferred value." The vacuum is the ground state of this array; nothing more exotic.

#### Step 2: Why there are many vacua (a winding count)

The simplest toy that shows it: a ring of 8 sites, each holding an angle, like a compass needle. Walk around the ring and add up the turn from each needle to the next, taking each step the short way (between −180° and +180°). Then divide by a full turn:

$$
n = \frac{1}{360^\circ}\sum_{k=0}^{7} \Delta\theta_k
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $n$ : winding number, always a whole number
- $\Delta\theta_k$ : the turn from site k to site k+1, taken the short way (between −180° and +180°); site 8 is site 0 again
- $360^\circ$ : one full turn

**Worked example.**
- All needles at 0°: every step is 0, so n = 0.
- Needles at 0°, 45°, 90°, …, 315°: every step is +45°, including the closing step from 315° back to 0° (= 360°). The sum is 8 × 45° = 360°, so n = 1. The pattern wraps once around.

**What this actually means:** Because you end where you started, n must be a whole number, so it cannot drift smoothly from 1 to 0. Try to force it by shrinking every angle by a factor s, running s from 1 down to 0:
- The first seven steps shrink smoothly, to 45°×s each.
- The closing step, from site 7 (315°×s) back to site 0 (0°), stays on the "short-way" side until s = 180/315 ≈ 0.571. At that moment it is exactly 180°: the needles at sites 7 and 0 point in **opposite** directions.
- Just past that point the closing step flips sign and n drops from 1 to 0. Check at s = 0.5: seven steps of 22.5° (157.5° total), plus a closing step of −157.5°, sum 0, so n = 0.

The only way to change the count is to pass through a **tear**, where two neighbors point opposite ways. For a link, that is the maximum-cost state.

> **Analogy:** A rotary encoder that reports only the angle within one revolution, with firmware that counts revolutions by watching for the wrap from 359° to 0°. The revolution count is an integer, and it can only change when the shaft actually passes the wrap point.
>
> **Breaks when:** you look at the energy. In the needle toy, a wound pattern costs energy, because neighbors disagree by 45°. In the weak-force theory every winding costs exactly zero. That is special to gauge fields: a wound gauge configuration is a pure relabeling, like changing units cell by cell, with nothing physical out of place. That is why every rung of the ladder sits at exactly the same energy.

**Scaling up to the real theory.** Space is three-dimensional. Each cell holds not an angle on a circle but a setting in SU(2), the weak force's symmetry group, which is geometrically a three-dimensional sphere. The count survives the upgrade: it is still a whole number measuring how many times the field's settings wrap the group as you sweep all of space. For a vacuum, that number is the Chern–Simons number N_CS. For in-between (non-vacuum) configurations, N_CS takes fractional values, like the encoder's total angle in revolutions (2.37 revolutions, say), with the vacua at the whole numbers. That is what makes N_CS the natural reaction coordinate.

#### Step 3: The barrier and the saddle (the part you remembered)

Between rung 0 and rung 1 there are countless routes through the space of field configurations, and each route has a highest point. The sphaleron is the **lowest of those highest points**: the mountain pass, exactly the transition state of a reaction. It is unstable in one direction (downhill toward either rung) and stable in every other direction.

The ring toy needed a tear to change its count. The three-dimensional version of a tear is a spot where the **Higgs field drops to zero**. Everywhere else, the Higgs field sits at its normal value, 246 GeV. Forcing it to zero inside a small region, plus the tension of the gauge field wrapped around that region, costs energy. That energy is the 9 TeV.

**Worked size check.** The size of the blob is set by the W boson mass:

size ≈ ħc / M_W = 197 MeV·fm / 80,400 MeV ≈ 0.0025 fm ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁸ m

That is about 1/340 of a proton's radius. A sphaleron is a tiny, dense lump: 9 TeV packed into a region 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁸ m across.

#### Step 4: Why crossing creates matter (likely the part that didn't land)

This rests on two ideas.

**Idea A: the vacuum is a filled band.** You know this from semiconductors. At zero temperature the valence band is full and the conduction band is empty. Promote one electron and you get a particle above the gap and a hole below it. Relativistic quantum theory treats the vacuum the same way (Dirac's picture): every negative-energy state is filled (the "Dirac sea"), a filled positive-energy state is a particle, and an empty negative-energy state is an antiparticle. The one difference that matters here: **the sea has no bottom**. The ladder of states runs down forever.

**Idea B: during the crossing, the levels slide.** As the gauge field moves from rung 0 to rung 1, the energy levels of the left-handed fermions drift smoothly upward. By the time it reaches rung 1, the whole ladder has moved up by exactly one step. The new ladder looks identical to the old one, as it must, since the new vacuum is just as good as the old. But each state carries its occupancy with it as it slides.

**Worked example.** Take levels at −2.5, −1.5, −0.5, +0.5, +1.5 (spacing 1, continuing forever in both directions). Start in the vacuum, with everything below zero filled.
- **Before:** filled at −2.5, −1.5, −0.5 and all lower levels; empty at +0.5 and +1.5.
- **Slide everything up one step:** the state that was at −0.5 is now at +0.5, and it is still filled.
- **After:** the set of levels is identical to before, but one positive-energy state is now filled. That is one real particle.
- Nothing was taken from the bottom, because there is no bottom: there is always another filled state further down to move into each vacated slot. The count above the zero line simply went up by one.

> **Analogy:** Hilbert's hotel: infinitely many rooms, every one occupied. Everyone moves up one room. Nobody checks in or out, yet the number of guests above any given floor goes up by one.
>
> **Breaks when:** you ask why the shift is exactly one step, and why only left-handed particles take part. The hotel doesn't decide that; the topology does. One unit of winding moves each left-handed doublet's ladder by exactly one level. Right-handed particles don't feel the weak force, so their ladders never move. That left–right difference is what makes this a genuine quantum effect (an "anomaly") rather than an accounting trick.

**Counting to 12.** Each left-handed weak "doublet" (a pair the weak force treats as partners) gains exactly one new particle per rung crossed.
- **Per generation:** 3 quark doublets (the up-type/down-type pair, one per color) + 1 lepton doublet (the neutrino and its charged lepton) = 4 doublets.
- **Three generations:** 4 × 3 = 12 doublets, so **9 quarks and 3 leptons** appear.
- **Bookkeeping:** a quark carries ⅓ unit of baryon number, so 9 quarks is 3 units of baryon number (three protons' worth). Lepton number also rises by 3.

Both numbers jump by 3, so their difference, B − L, stays fixed. That is the paper's line "baryon and lepton number are violated, B − L is conserved."

**Why protons are safe today.** At everyday energies, crossing means tunneling through the 9 TeV barrier, suppressed by a factor of about 10⁻¹⁶². For scale, the universe is about 4 × 10¹⁷ seconds old. Nothing that improbable ever happens.

#### Step 5: Where crossings actually happen

**The early universe, before about 10⁻¹¹ s.** Everything was hotter than about 130 GeV, roughly 1.5 × 10¹⁵ K (1 GeV ≈ 1.16 × 10¹³ K). At that temperature the Higgs field's value shrinks toward zero, the barrier shrinks with it, and thermal hopping over it becomes routine. It is Arrhenius chemistry with the activation energy heading to zero. Sphaleron transitions ran constantly and in equilibrium.

**The LHC.** Colliding quarks can carry 9 TeV between them, but the energy arrives in the wrong shape. A sphaleron is a coherent lump built from the equivalent of about 30 quanta spread over 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁸ m. Two quarks hitting head-on are two quanta concentrated at a point. Turning one into the other is like getting a stadium wave out of a single person jumping very high: plenty of energy, wrong form. Quantum mechanics makes that mismatch exponentially costly. Most theorists expect the collider rate to stay suppressed by something like 10⁻¹⁶ or more. A minority view (Tye and Wong) argues the suppression disappears once the energy is above the barrier, and the CMS search is effectively testing that minority view.

**Worked check on the LHC's difficulty.** The protons collide at 13 TeV, but each quark carries only a fraction of its proton's momentum, typically well under a quarter. Reaching 9 TeV requires the product of the two fractions to be at least (9/13)² ≈ 0.48, so each quark needs about 70% of its proton's momentum. That is rare, which is why even an unsuppressed sphaleron would give only a trickle of events.

#### Why anyone cares: matter vs antimatter

The universe holds about one leftover proton for every billion or so matter–antimatter pairs that annihilated. Explaining that excess takes three ingredients (Sakharov's list):
1. **Something that changes baryon number.** Sphalerons provide this.
2. **A preference for matter over antimatter.** The Standard Model's is far too weak.
3. **A moment out of equilibrium.** The Standard Model fails this: with a 125 GeV Higgs, the transition is a smooth crossover.

So sphalerons are a necessary gear in most explanations, not an explanation by themselves, which is why the paper's line that they "can explain the matter-antimatter asymmetry" overstates the case. A common scenario: new physics first makes an excess of leptons, then sphalerons convert about a third of it into baryons.
