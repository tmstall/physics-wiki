## 1. Punchy Title & Hook

**Title:** Gravity's Best-Known Measuring Stick Says the World's Best-Known Number Is Still Off

**Hook:** NIST physically imported the BIPM's own torsion balance — the instrument, not just its blueprint — rebuilt it with new masses, new metrology, and new software, and got a value of the gravitational constant that disagrees with the *same hardware's own earlier measurement* by more than either team's quoted error bars allow, while turning up (and, in one case, fully explaining from first principles) several systematic effects nobody had previously budgeted for.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

The gravitational constant, *G*, is the odd one out among the fundamental constants of physics. Planck's constant, the electron charge, the speed of light — these are known to nine, ten, twelve significant figures, because the experiments that pin them down (atomic clocks, quantum Hall resistance, interferometry) can borrow enormous signal amplification from quantum coherence or electromagnetic precision. Gravity has no such trick. It's the weakest of the four fundamental forces by roughly 40 orders of magnitude, so measuring it in the lab means detecting a torque or a force from two lab-bench-sized masses sitting a few centimeters apart — a signal so faint that in this experiment the total torque being measured is about 30 nN·m, and the *difference* used to extract *G* is often just a few piconewton-meters on top of that. *G* is consequently known to only about 1 part in 20,000 (relative uncertainty ≈ 2×10⁻⁵ in the current CODATA value) — genuinely the worst-known fundamental constant in physics, more than three orders of magnitude worse than most of its peers.

Worse than merely imprecise, *G* is *inconsistent*. Figure 1 of this paper lays out roughly two dozen precision determinations of *G* made over the last five decades — pendulums, torsion balances, beam balances, different fiber materials, different continents — and they scatter over a range of about 1 part in 10,000, which is 5–10 times larger than the individual experiments' own quoted uncertainties would predict if they were all just independently noisy measurements of the same number. That's the "big *G* problem": either there's unrecognized new physics coupling to gravity at short range (the paper treats this as the less likely explanation), or — far more plausibly — every torsion balance and beam balance ever built for this purpose carries some subtle, apparatus-specific systematic error nobody has identified yet.

The specific instrument at the center of this paper has its own history worth knowing. In the late 1990s, a team led by Terry Quinn at the BIPM (the body that literally defines the world's system of measurement units) built a torsion balance specifically to measure *G*, using an unusual design choice — a rectangular copper-beryllium ribbon instead of the traditional fine circular fiber — to exploit an effect called *dissipation dilution* (explained in Section 3). That instrument produced two published values, in 2001 ("Mark I") and 2014 ("Mark II"), both among the most precise *G* measurements ever made — and also among the most divergent from the eventual international consensus value. In 2016, rather than let the debate simmer, Quinn arranged to ship the actual physical apparatus to NIST, where a different team (with only Speake, one original BIPM collaborator, bridging the two efforts) rebuilt it, re-instrumented it, and re-measured *G* from scratch. This paper is the result: ten years of work condensed into a single number, plus an unusually candid accounting of everything that went wrong along the way.

**Paper type and stakes:** this is a precision-metrology replication paper — not a new theoretical prediction, but a from-scratch redo of one specific historical measurement using literally the same hardware in a new location, explicitly designed to test whether the BIPM's own earlier result was an artifact of that specific lab, or something more fundamental. What's at stake is less "the value of *G* changes" (nobody expects new physics here) and more "can precision metrology actually diagnose its own hidden errors," which matters for every future high-stakes measurement built on the same kind of delicate mechanical apparatus.

**Prior Belief Check:** the *existence* of unexplained scatter among *G* measurements is not news to anyone in the field — it's the well-known, decades-old status quo, and this paper's abstract says as much. What is genuinely informative, and mildly surprising even to specialists, is *where* the disagreement turned out to land: reusing the identical physical hardware (not just the same design) in a new lab, with a new CMM, new test masses, new vacuum chamber sealing, and new software, still produced a result offset from the original BIPM number by 2.5×10⁻⁴ relative — several times larger than either team's quoted uncertainty. That's a meaningful result in its own right: it's evidence the "hidden systematics" plaguing *G* measurements are not simply baked into a specific piece of hardware (which transplanting the hardware would have fixed) but are sensitive to the surrounding environment, procedure, and instrumentation chain — a materially different and more troubling diagnosis than "this one balance has a flaw."

**Replication & Convergence Note:** this is explicitly framed by the authors as the *first-ever* like-for-like replication of a high-precision *G* measurement using the same physical apparatus in two different labs — a genuinely novel form of cross-check in a field where "independent measurements" have always meant independently *designed and built* instruments. It is not, however, independent confirmation of the BIPM's *original* number (it disagrees with it), nor does any third party get to re-run this exact hardware a second time. The four internal sub-results reported here (two mass materials × two measurement methods) partially cross-validate each other, but true external replication — a different lab building yet another instrument and landing on a compatible number — remains the unresolved, decades-long open item for the field as a whole.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**How a torsion balance measures *G*.** Hang a rigid frame from a thin ribbon so it's free to twist. Put test masses on that frame. Bring a second, larger set of masses (the "source masses") nearby, arranged so their gravity creates a tiny torque that twists the ribbon by some measurable angle. If you know the masses, the geometry, and the ribbon's stiffness, that twist angle tells you *G*. That's the whole idea behind every Cavendish-style experiment since 1798; everything else in this paper is about making that twist angle trustworthy to 5 parts in 100,000.

**Analogy:** think of the ribbon as a mechanical accumulator register that integrates a tiny, continuously-applied "force signal" (gravity) into a readable "output value" (twist angle), the way a leaky integrator circuit accumulates a small input current into a measurable voltage over time. The whole art of the experiment is keeping every other noise source — thermal, electrical, seismic — from also writing into that same register.

**Breaks when:** you push the analogy toward "just filter out the noise electronically." A torsion balance's twist angle is a genuine equilibrium mechanical state — it's not a transient signal you can time-average away in the usual electronic sense; some of the effects this paper hunts down (a decades-timescale anelastic relaxation in the ribbon material itself, Section 8.1) are properties of the sensor's own physical substrate, more like flash-memory charge trapping or long-term drift in an analog reference than like white noise on a wire.

**Two ways to read the twist — free deflection vs. electrostatic servo.** The experiment measures the same underlying torque two structurally different ways.

> **Concept:** the gravitational torque produced by the source masses must be converted into an absolute value of *G* without assuming *G* in the process. *Free deflection* infers the torque from how far the pendulum's equilibrium angle shifts when the source masses move, combined with the pendulum's own free-oscillation frequency and known moment of inertia — the calibration standard here is *inertial/mechanical* (mass and geometry you can put on a coordinate-measuring machine). *Electrostatic servo* instead applies a calibrated electrical torque, via voltages on nearby electrodes and a measured capacitance gradient, strong enough to completely null the pendulum's motion — the calibration standard here is *electrical* (voltage and capacitance you can trace to national voltage/capacitance standards). Both are trying to measure the identical physical torque; they just borrow their absolute scale from two unrelated corners of metrology.
>
> **Vehicle:** think of measuring an unknown electrical resistance two ways. Method A applies a known current and reads the resulting voltage on a calibrated voltmeter, then computes R = V/I — trusting the voltmeter's calibration chain. Method B instead balances a bridge circuit: you tune a calibrated reference resistor until a null detector reads exactly zero, and read the unknown resistance directly off the reference dial — trusting the reference-resistor's calibration chain instead. Both determine the same unknown R through two independently-traceable calibration paths.
>
> **Analogy:** free deflection ↔ Method A (a real signal, read out and converted using the balance's own inertia as the trusted calibration standard). Electrostatic servo ↔ Method B (the signal is nulled out entirely, and the size of the applied correction — traceable to electrical standards — is itself the answer). Agreement between the two, the way agreement between Method A and B would validate an unknown resistance, is the paper's built-in cross-check against a systematic riding on just one calibration chain.
>
> **Breaks when:** asked to explain *why* the two methods might disagree by a small, fixed, and reproducible amount rather than random scatter. A miscalibrated bridge reference resistor gives a resistance-bridge measurement a known, traceable failure mode — "the dial was wrong." Here, free deflection and electrostatic servo disagree by a highly consistent ≈1.8–2.1 pN·m (copper and sapphire runs alike) despite a dedicated search for a culprit (stray capacitive losses in cable dielectrics, time-dependent effects following servo-voltage application) — and none was found. The bridge-versus-voltmeter picture has no natural slot for "two independently-calibrated methods disagree by a stable amount with no locatable cause," which is exactly the honest, unresolved state this paper reports.

**Why a rectangular ribbon, not a round fiber — dissipation dilution.** Any real spring loses a little mechanical energy to internal friction every time it flexes; that lossy fraction is exactly what generates thermal (Brownian) noise in the readout, by the same fluctuation-dissipation logic that makes a resistor's Johnson noise proportional to its resistance, not its reactance. A torsion ribbon under load, however, gets part of its total "stiffness" for free from gravity itself pulling the ribbon straight (a purely elastic, lossless restoring force) on top of the ribbon's own intrinsic elastic stiffness (which *is* lossy). Load the ribbon more heavily, and the lossless gravitational contribution grows while the lossy elastic contribution's *relative share* of the total shrinks — diluting the noise-generating fraction of the total spring constant without adding any noise. That's why this balance is deliberately built to carry an unusually heavy load (a torque roughly 10⁴ times larger than a typical torsion-balance experiment): heavier loading buys a better signal-to-noise ratio essentially for free.

**Mass integration and the geometry factor Γ.** *G* is extracted from a measured torque via *N* = *G*·(8·m_s·m_t/R_s)·Γ(Δφ), where Γ is a dimensionless number (Table 6 puts it at ≈0.4858 for this geometry) that packages up the entire mass shapes, spacings, and orientations of the apparatus. Because *G* is inversely proportional to Γ, any error in modeling the exact 3-D shape of every mass, mounting screw, and structural component propagates directly and undiluted into the final G. The team computed this factor two ways — an idealized point-mass approximation (fast, but off by ~6% relative to the real geometry) and a full numerical integration over the actual measured cylinder dimensions, including 299 separate disk components down to balancing-ball-sized details — and cross-validated the numerical result against an independently coded version used at BIPM, finding identical answers.

**Central analogy for this paper:** same physical rig, new lab, exhaustive systematic audit.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### From torque to *G*: the central relation, worked

Everything in this experiment funnels down to one relation:

G = ΔN_max · R_s / (16 · m_s · m_t · Γ_max)

**Symbol definitions:**
ΔN_max : the measured difference in gravitational torque between the two extremal source-mass positions (units: N·m)
R_s : the radius of the circle the source masses sit on (m)
m_s, m_t : the mass of one source mass and one test mass, respectively (kg)
Γ_max : the dimensionless mass-integration factor described above, evaluated at the angle of maximum torque

**What this actually means:** this is a calibration equation in the same spirit as converting a raw ADC reading into a physical voltage using a gain and offset you measured separately — the "raw signal" here is the torque ΔN_max, and everything else in the fraction is a geometry-and-mass "gain factor" that converts torque units into a value of *G*. Get any input to that gain factor wrong, and *G* shifts by exactly that fractional amount — there's no averaging-away a bad gain constant, however many times you repeat the measurement.

**Worked micro-example**, using real numbers from Tables 2 and 15 (the copper, 0°-orientation, free-deflection configuration): ΔN_max = 31.1979 nN·m, R_s = 213.9642 mm, m_s = 11191.68 g, m_t = 1150.156 g, Γ_max = 0.485613.

Numerator: 31.1979×10⁻⁹ N·m × 0.2139642 m ≈ 6.6752×10⁻⁹ N·m²
Denominator: 16 × 11.19168 kg × 1.150156 kg × 0.485613 ≈ 100.014 kg²

G ≈ 6.6752×10⁻⁹ / 100.014 ≈ 6.674×10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²

— reproducing, from the raw numbers in the paper's own tables, the right order of magnitude and leading digits of the final quoted result. This is exactly the kind of arithmetic you'd run to sanity-check a calibration constant before trusting a whole measurement campaign built on top of it.

### The two measurement chains, and where the real work is

Free deflection (Section 3.2) fits a sinusoid to the pendulum's continuous free oscillation, extracting both the equilibrium angle *and* the resonant frequency ω₀ simultaneously from the same time series — the frequency matters because the effective "spring constant" driving that oscillation is what converts an angle into a torque (Equation 7 in the paper: ω₀² = ∂²E_pot/∂φ_t² / I). Electrostatic servo (Section 3.3) instead drives calibrated AC voltages onto electrode pairs surrounding the pendulum, exploiting the fact that the induced electrostatic torque depends on capacitance *gradients* (how fast the electrode-to-pendulum capacitance changes with angle) that were separately measured (Figure 6) by exciting a free oscillation and recording the capacitance bridge output in lock-step.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** a reader could easily assume that because NIST used the *literal same apparatus* as BIPM, this is essentially "the same experiment, run again" — a pure repeatability check. The paper actually treats it as a near-total requalification: a different CMM (Zeiss Xenos vs. BIPM's own machines), all-new diamond-turned test masses (replacing the originals specifically to fix a tri-lobed form error), a rewritten software stack for instrument control and mass-integration, a different vacuum chamber and pumping geometry, and an entirely new second set of source masses (single-crystal sapphire) added specifically to test for a magnetic-coupling systematic that copper alone couldn't rule out. Reusing the mechanical heart of the instrument did not mean holding everything else fixed.

> **Watch:** it's natural to picture a torsion balance as directly sensing a force, the way a kitchen scale does. Nothing in this apparatus directly measures force or torque at all — every result is inferred from an *angle* (read by an autocollimator watching a mirror) combined with either a *frequency* (free deflection) or a *voltage and capacitance* (servo). The autocollimator's own nonlinearity (Section 8.5) — an artifact of its internal CCD pixel structure — turns out to be one of the largest single contributors to the final uncertainty budget (10.7–85.7 parts per million depending on configuration, Table 17), precisely because everything downstream depends on trusting that one angle-reading device.

> **Watch:** the quoted final relative uncertainty (5.7×10⁻⁵) might read as "how noisy the data is," inviting the assumption that collecting more oscillation cycles would shrink it further. Most of the dominant terms in Table 17 — mass integration (16.2 ppm, common to every configuration), autocollimator nonlinearity (up to 85.7 ppm for sapphire-free), and the pressure/thermal correction (up to 21.5 ppm) — are systematic and calibration-driven, essentially independent of statistical averaging time. More strikingly, the authors add an entirely separate "dark uncertainty" term (Table 19) precisely *because* the four sub-measurements disagree with each other by more than their already-budgeted systematic-plus-statistical uncertainties predict — a discrepancy no amount of additional averaging within any one configuration would have caught, since it's a *between-configuration* effect.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**1. Apparatus-level replication as its own diagnostic tool.** The usual way the field tries to resolve the *G* discrepancy problem is to build a completely independent instrument (different fiber material, different mass geometry, different lab) and see if it lands near the consensus value — which is what most of the ~20 points scattered across Figure 1 represent. This paper does something structurally different: hold the physical hardware fixed, and vary almost everything else (lab, team, CMM, software, vacuum system, even adding a second mass material). That isolates a genuinely useful question the field hadn't cleanly answered before — is the scatter baked into specific pieces of hardware, or does it live in the surrounding metrology and environment? The answer this paper delivers (a 2.5×10⁻⁴ shift from the same hardware in a new setting) points squarely at the latter.

**2. First-principles derivation, and independent confirmation, of a previously-unrecognized thermal-gas torque.** Section 7 doesn't just note that measured *G* correlated with residual chamber pressure (Figure 15) — it builds a predictive kinetic-gas-theory model (Waldmann's thermophoretic-force formula, Equations 42–46) with parameters fit to one experiment (natural pressure rise over months) and then successfully predicts the response of an entirely separate, deliberately-run experiment (an external heater cycled at a controlled 24-minute period, Figure 17), matching both the pressure dependence and the absolute torque scale. That's a genuine mechanistic discovery, not just a correlation flagged and corrected for empirically.

**3. Configuration-specific "dark uncertainty" via a Bayesian hierarchical model.** Rather than pooling all discrepancy between the four sub-measurements into one blanket inflation factor, Section 9's model (Equation 63) assigns each of the four determinations (copper-servo, copper-free, sapphire-servo, sapphire-free) its own latent bias term with its own prior width — and the resulting posterior (Table 19) assigns the sapphire-servo measurement a "dark uncertainty" roughly 3–4× larger than the copper measurements, aligning with (though not fully vindicating) the team's own prior suspicion that the lower-signal sapphire runs were more susceptible to unmodeled effects.

**Predictive Content Check.**

*Falsifiable handle (always):* this is a measurement paper, so the "prediction" is inverted from the usual theory-paper sense — the paper commits to a specific, falsifiable number, *G* = (6.67387 ± 0.00038)×10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², with a stated 5.7×10⁻⁵ relative uncertainty. This is emphatically not a relabeling of an existing result: it differs from the very apparatus's own earlier (BIPM) value by 2.5×10⁻⁴ relative, and from the CODATA-22 consensus by −6.4×10⁻⁵ (not statistically significant at *p* = 0.29, but a real, falsifiable data point nonetheless — any future independent replication landing outside this number's error bars would be informative in exactly the same way this result was informative relative to BIPM's original value).

*Formalism load (conditional):* fires, and the verdict is load-bearing throughout. The Legendre-polynomial mass-integration expansion (Equations 24–34) directly sets the single largest systematic term in the uncertainty budget (16.2 ppm); the kinetic-gas thermal-torque model directly generates the pressure-extrapolation correction; the Bayesian hierarchical model directly produces the quoted consensus value and its uncertainty. None of the formalism here is decorative — every major mathematical structure in the paper terminates in a specific number that appears in the final uncertainty budget.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

The measured discrepancy between this NIST value and BIPM's original Mark II result — the paper's own central finding — remains, by the authors' own account, *unexplained*. **(A) Consensus.** Section 10 explicitly states "at present, we do not have a conclusive explanation for this systematic offset," and separately, "we did not identify a single dominant cause for the discrepancy with the BIPM result." This is the paper's own stated conclusion, not an outside critique. **(paper §9, §10)**

The internal offset between the two measurement methods (free deflection running systematically ~1.8–2.1 pN·m above electrostatic servo, in both the copper and sapphire runs) also has no identified cause, despite the two methods' near-total independence in calibration standard. **(A) Consensus** — the paper searched specifically for a capacitive-loss or time-dependent servo-voltage artifact and found nothing, and states plainly that "the origin of this behavior remains unknown," noting only that the *Mark II* apparatus showed a qualitatively similar effect historically, which argues for something apparatus-intrinsic without pinning down what. **(paper §9)**

The source masses' geometric form error is corrected using comparisons between partial-arc and full-360° coverage on the *same* coordinate-measuring machine, rather than validation against a second, independent CMM specific to each source mass's actual (imperfect, ~10–20 μm tri-lobed) form. The diamond-turned test masses got a tighter, independently-specified manufacturing tolerance (<0.25 μm cylindricity); the source masses did not receive an equivalent independent check. **(B) Contested** — reasonable metrologists could differ on whether a same-instrument partial/full comparison, backed by a separately-calibrated step gauge for overall CMM accuracy (Figure 10), is sufficient here; it's a defensible design choice given practical access constraints, not an obvious gap, but it is a weaker form of validation than the test masses received. **(analyst inference, informed by paper §5.2–5.3)**

The Bayesian "dark uncertainty" model's configuration-specific prior widths are set using an explicit "expert ranking of configuration reliability" (medians scaled 1×, 2×, 3×, 4× a reference value across the four configurations) rather than derived purely from the data. **(C) Speculative** — this is my own read of a modeling choice the paper is transparent about but doesn't itself flag as a limitation; a different, equally defensible expert ranking could shift the exact posterior weighting of the four sub-measurements by some amount, though probably not enough to change the headline number meaningfully given how close the four individual values already sit to each other in Figure 26. **(analyst inference)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

NIST set out to answer a specific, well-posed question: does BIPM's own torsion balance — the exact physical instrument, shipped across an ocean — reproduce its own earlier measurement of *G* when rebuilt and re-operated by a different team in a different environment? The answer, after roughly a decade of rebuilding, re-instrumenting, and measuring, is a firm no: the new value, *G* = (6.67387 ± 0.00038)×10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻¹ (0.57 parts in 10,000 relative uncertainty), sits 2.5 parts in 10,000 below BIPM's original number — several times larger than either team's stated error bars would predict from chance alone.

Getting to that number required requalifying nearly every subsystem of the apparatus. The team replaced the original test masses (which had a small but consequential tri-lobed manufacturing defect) with new diamond-turned cylinders; brought in a modern coordinate-measuring machine and discovered — and then corrected for — a subtle bias that arises whenever you can only physically access part of a cylinder's circumference with a measuring probe (Table 3, Figure 9); added an entirely new set of source masses made of single-crystal sapphire specifically to rule out a proposed AC-magnetic-coupling systematic that copper masses couldn't distinguish from gravity; and discovered, diagnosed, and fully modeled from first principles a previously unrecognized thermal-gas torque, in which small temperature gradients on the vacuum chamber's inner wall (driven by nearby heat sources and airflow patterns that shift as the source masses rotate) push on residual gas molecules hard enough to produce a torque that scales with chamber pressure — a torque roughly a thousand times smaller than the gravitational signal itself, but large enough to matter at this precision, and cleanly confirmed by an independent, deliberately-run heating experiment.

Every measurement was also run two structurally independent ways — a mechanical/inertial method (free deflection) and an electrical method (electrostatic servo) — specifically so that a systematic riding on one calibration chain wouldn't silently contaminate the final answer undetected. The two methods agree reasonably well but not perfectly, differing by a small, highly consistent offset whose cause the team searched for and did not find. Finally, because the four resulting sub-measurements (two mass materials × two methods) still disagreed with each other by more than their individually-budgeted uncertainties, the team built a Bayesian statistical model that explicitly assigns each sub-measurement its own "dark uncertainty" — an honest, quantified admission of unmodeled bias — rather than simply averaging the four numbers together as if they were compatible.

The interpretive choice underlying this whole write-up is to treat the paper's *systematic-effects catalogue* as at least as important as its headline number — because that is explicitly how the authors themselves frame their own contribution in the closing section: not as settling the value of *G*, but as adding "clarity in approach, technique, and craftsmanship" and a "solid, if complex, footing for future efforts." The headline number didn't resolve the big-*G* problem; if anything, by disagreeing with the very apparatus's own earlier result, it deepens it. But the itemized uncertainty budget (Table 17), the validated thermal-torque model, and the demonstrated CMM partial-coverage bias are each independently reusable by the next team that tries this measurement, regardless of what apparatus they use.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the exact mechanics of the Bayesian hierarchical "dark uncertainty" combination — how the configuration-specific priors on τ_j interact with the robust-statistics (Huber's M-estimator, Qₙ) combination step to produce the final consensus value and its quoted uncertainty (Equation 63 through Table 19) — are described by the paper at the level of "what was done and what came out," citing external methodological references ([67]–[71]) rather than showing the full posterior derivation. I've taken their stated conclusion (and the plausibility of the final number, which I independently sanity-checked against the raw torque/mass/geometry numbers above) at face value rather than reconstructing the Bayesian inference step by step myself, so this is the part of the analysis most likely to be missing a subtlety a working Bayesian-metrology specialist would catch immediately.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. NIST didn't just re-measure the gravitational constant — they airlifted BIPM's actual torsion balance across an ocean, rebuilt it with new masses, new coordinate-measuring equipment, and new software, and *still* got a number that disagrees with that same hardware's own earlier result by more than the quoted error bars allow. That's strong evidence the decades-long "big *G*" scatter problem lives in something about environment, procedure, or instrumentation chain — not simply a flaw baked permanently into one specific piece of hardware.

2. Along the way, the team found and fully explained (with first-principles kinetic-gas theory, independently confirmed by a deliberate heating experiment) a previously unrecognized systematic: tiny temperature gradients on the vacuum chamber wall pushing on residual gas to create a spurious, pressure-dependent torque. Even after catching that one and cross-checking two independent measurement methods against each other, an unexplained ~2 pN·m gap between the methods — and a larger gap versus BIPM's original number — remains openly on the books, reported honestly rather than argued away.

3. The paper's real product isn't the digits 6.67387 — by the authors' own framing, it's the newly itemized, quantified menu of systematic effects (test-mass form error, CMM partial-coverage bias, thermal-gas torque, autocollimator nonlinearity, cross-method disagreement) that any future high-precision *G* experiment now has to budget for explicitly, whether or not it uses this same apparatus.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Of the four fundamental constants, the strength of gravity (*G*) is by far the worst known — and, unlike the others, different careful measurements of it disagree with each other by more than their claimed error bars, a decades-old puzzle nicknamed the "big *G* problem." Ten years ago, NIST took an unusual approach to investigating it: rather than building a new instrument, they had the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) ship them its own actual torsion-balance apparatus — the same physical hardware that had produced one of the more outlying *G* measurements in 2001 and 2014 — so a different team, in a different lab, could see whether the same instrument gave the same answer.

It didn't. The rebuilt experiment, measured two structurally independent ways (a mechanical method and an electrical method) using two different sets of test masses (copper and sapphire, to rule out a magnetic-coupling explanation), returned *G* = 6.67387×10⁻¹¹ (cubic meters per kilogram per second squared), accurate to about 6 parts in 100,000 — but sitting about 2.5 parts in 10,000 below BIPM's own original number from the same hardware. That's a small-sounding shift, but several times larger than the measurement's own precision should allow by chance.

Getting there required an enormous amount of detective work. The team discovered and corrected a subtle measurement bias from only being able to access part of each mass's surface with their precision measuring equipment; discovered, from scratch, an entirely new physical effect where tiny temperature differences on the vacuum chamber's walls push on trace leftover gas molecules hard enough to fake a small extra gravitational-looking torque; and, even after all that, still found their two independent measurement methods disagreeing with each other by a small, unexplained amount. Rather than hide that disagreement, they built it explicitly into their final statistics as an honestly-quantified "we don't fully understand this" uncertainty term.

The bottom line: this measurement doesn't resolve the puzzle of why gravity is so hard to pin down — if anything, by disagreeing with its own predecessor, it sharpens the mystery. But it hands the field a far more detailed map of exactly which sources of error matter at this level of precision, which is arguably worth more to the next experiment than the specific digits of *G* itself.

## Appendix — Deep-Dive Discussion Log (2026-09-20)

**Quoted passage (Section 3, Concept/Vehicle analogy):**

> *Free deflection* infers the torque from how far the pendulum's equilibrium angle shifts when the source masses move, combined with the pendulum's own free-oscillation frequency and known moment of inertia — the calibration standard here is *inertial/mechanical* (mass and geometry you can put on a coordinate-measuring machine)

**Unpacking:**

Good passage to drill into — that line compresses three separate measurements and a subtle equivalence-principle argument into one sentence. Let me unpack the actual mechanism.

**The governing equation, and where each measured quantity enters.**

A free torsion pendulum obeys (ignoring the source-mass torque for a moment) something structurally like a driven spring: *I·φ̈ + β·φ̇ + κ·φ = N*, where *I* is the pendulum's moment of inertia and *κ* is the torsion strip's spring constant. When the source masses shift position, the equilibrium angle the pendulum settles into shifts by

Δφ_t = ΔN_max / (∂²E_pot/∂φ_t²)

The denominator — the second derivative of the total potential energy with respect to twist angle — is exactly the effective spring constant κ. That's the whole trick of free deflection: rather than *measuring* κ directly (which would require some independent calibrated torque, defeating the purpose), the paper gets it from something already sitting in the same dataset — the pendulum's own free-oscillation frequency, via ω₀² = κ/I (Equation 7). So "known moment of inertia" isn't a side fact; it's the second of two ingredients (the first being ω₀) that together *reconstruct* the ribbon's stiffness without ever touching it directly.

**How ω₀ and Δφ_t come out of the same fit, not two separate measurements.**

This is the part worth flagging explicitly, because it's easy to picture "measure the angle shift" and "measure the frequency" as two sequential steps. They're not — Section 3.2 fits a single sinusoidal model directly to the continuous autocollimator time series (Figure 4), and both the equilibrium angle *and* ω₀ fall out as parameters of that one fit, simultaneously. ω₀ is treated as a nonlinear fit parameter, seeded with a precise starting estimate derived from the pendulum's period, so the fit converges quickly rather than searching blind. Practically: the same 6-period stretch of data at φ_s,max and the matching stretch at φ_s,min each yield their own (equilibrium angle, ω₀) pair; the equilibrium-angle *difference* between the two positions is Δφ_t.

**Assembling the moment of inertia — and a worked check against the paper's own numbers.**

*I* isn't a single measured quantity either; it's built from Equation 9 as a sum of point-mass, parallel-axis, and disk terms:

I = 4·m_t·R_t² + 4·m_t·(b_t² − a_t²)/2 + I_disk

Using Table 2's values (m_t = 1.150156 kg, R_t = 0.1200315 m, b_t = 0.027481 m, a_t = 0.002502 m, I_disk = 7.45×10⁻³ kg·m²):

- Point-mass term: 4 × 1.150156 × 0.1200315² ≈ 0.066301 kg·m²
- Parallel-axis correction: 4 × 1.150156 × (0.027481² − 0.002502²)/2 ≈ 0.0017228 kg·m²
- Disk: 0.00745 kg·m²

Total I ≈ 0.075474 kg·m². Dividing the correction terms by the point-mass term reproduces the paper's own stated typical values almost exactly: (b_t²−a_t²)/(2R_t²) ≈ 0.026 and I_disk/(4·m_t·R_t²) ≈ 0.112 — matching the paper's quoted 0.026 and 0.11. That's a clean internal-consistency check: the moment of inertia isn't a black-box number handed down from elsewhere, it's fully reconstructible from the same dimensional-metrology table used everywhere else in the paper.

Combined with ω₀ = 2π/T₀ (T₀ = 121 s, so ω₀² ≈ 2.696×10⁻³ rad²/s²), the product ω₀²·I *is* the calibrated spring constant — entirely inertial/mechanical in origin, never an independently measured torque standard.

**The equivalence-principle subtlety, which is the real reason "inertial/mechanical" matters.**

Here's the non-obvious part the quoted sentence gestures at but doesn't spell out: the test masses' mass m_t appears on *both* sides of this measurement. It's part of *I* (the inertial mass setting the oscillation frequency) and it's part of the gravitational torque N being measured (the gravitational mass being pulled on by the source masses). Because inertial and gravitational mass are equal (that's the equivalence principle, not an assumption the paper has to defend separately), m_t very nearly cancels out of the final G_f — the paper states explicitly that G_f "depends only weakly on m_t." That's a genuinely elegant design consequence: free deflection is *self-calibrating* against the test-mass value to first order, precisely because it uses the test mass's own inertia as the ruler for the same test mass's gravitational coupling.

It's not a perfect cancellation, though, and the paper is upfront about why: the torsion disk and other pendulum-assembly components (the mirror tower, bottom clamp, leveling balls — collectively "the disk," 11% of total I per Table 7) contribute to *I* but aren't gravitationally coupled to the source masses the same way the test masses are. So the disk's presence in the denominator has no matching presence in the numerator, breaking the clean m_t cancellation by exactly the disk's fractional share of the moment of inertia — which is also precisely why Section 6.9's independent cross-check of I_disk (SolidWorks model vs. experimental measurement, agreeing to 1.6×10⁻⁴) matters as much as it does: it's validating the one piece of this calibration chain that *doesn't* get to hide behind the equivalence principle.
