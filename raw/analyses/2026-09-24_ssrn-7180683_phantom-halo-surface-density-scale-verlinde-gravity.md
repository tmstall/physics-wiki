## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**A Cusp Hides Inside the Coincidence: Verlinde-Gravity's Own Number for the Dark-Halo Surface-Density Puzzle**

Yoon plugs his own 2024 fix into Verlinde's emergent-gravity formula, gets a number for a 15-year-old dark-matter-halo regularity that lands within spitting distance of the observed value — and then, almost as a footnote, discovers the theory actually predicts the *wrong-shaped* halo to go with it.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

The "missing mass problem" is usually framed as a fight between adding invisible matter (dark matter) and rewriting the law of gravity (MOND and its relatives) to explain why galaxies rotate faster at their edges than the visible matter alone can account for. One of the odder pieces of evidence in that fight is a regularity Donato et al. (2009) found buried in dark-matter-halo fits: if you fit a galaxy's rotation curve with a cored (flat-center) halo profile called the Burkert profile, and multiply its core density by its core radius — call the result μ₀D, a quantity with units of mass per unit area — that number comes out nearly the same, roughly 141 M☉/pc² (with about a factor-of-1.6 scatter), across galaxies spanning 14 magnitudes in luminosity: dwarf spheroidals next to giant spirals, both landing on the same areal-density scale. (M☉/pc² — solar masses per square parsec — is just an astronomer's shorthand for "how much stuff per unit sky-projected area"; treat it as an arbitrary yardstick unit, the way you'd treat "bytes per square micron" for die-area budgeting.) Under standard dark matter theory, there's no obvious reason galaxies this different should converge on the same areal density — you'd need some fairly specific, currently unidentified conspiracy in how galaxies assemble their halos to produce it. MOND, by contrast, gets a number in the same ballpark almost for free, because it has a single built-in acceleration constant (Milgrom's a_M) that combines with Newton's G to set a mass-per-area scale directly, independent of any particular galaxy's formation history.

Verlinde's emergent gravity is a second no-dark-matter contender, built from a different starting point: rather than positing a modified force law by hand, it derives an "apparent dark matter" contribution to gravity, g_D, from holographic/thermodynamic arguments about how baryonic matter perturbs the entropy of a de Sitter horizon. In its original 2017 form, Verlinde combined this with ordinary Newtonian gravity from baryons, g_B, by simple addition:

$$g = g_B + g_D$$

**Symbol definitions:** g — the total gravitational acceleration actually felt; g_B — the ordinary Newtonian acceleration from visible (baryonic) mass alone; g_D — the extra "apparent dark matter" acceleration Verlinde's theory derives from the horizon-entropy argument.

**What this actually means:** two contributions to the same effect are just summed, the way you'd add two independent forces acting in the same direction.

Yoon flagged a technical inconsistency in that additive combination in a 2024 paper (this paper's direct predecessor) and proposed replacing it with a quadrature (Pythagorean) combination instead:

$$g = \sqrt{g_B^2 + g_D^2}$$

**What this actually means:** rather than adding the two contributions directly, you add them the way you'd combine two independent, uncorrelated error or noise sources into a total — root-sum-of-squares, the same operation you'd use to combine an oscillator's independent random-jitter and reference-clock-jitter contributions into one timing-margin number, rather than just stacking them linearly. I don't have the paper's own stated reason this specific combination rule is the right fix for Verlinde's theory — the source material I could retrieve states that it addresses "a technical problem" without spelling out the argument, and that's a real gap in what I can tell you here, not a detail I'm glossing over.

That 2024 paper tested the quadrature relation against exactly one dataset — wide binary star systems, where it predicted an acceleration-anomaly ratio of 1.29 against an observed 1.37 (within uncertainty). This new paper is the first time that same relation has been run forward analytically to compute a galactic dark-matter-halo's inner density profile, specifically to check whether it also reproduces Donato et al.'s central-surface-density regularity. It gets r·ρ_ph(r) = 10^2.24±0.09 M☉/pc² ≈ 174 M☉/pc² in the region where the local (Newtonian-regime) acceleration is well above a_M, versus Donato's 10^2.15±0.2 M☉/pc² ≈ 141 M☉/pc² — about 23% higher, comfortably inside Donato's own ~60% uncertainty band, so statistically consistent rather than a razor's-edge hit.

**Paper Type & Stakes.** This is a short theoretical Letter ("Short communication") performing one targeted analytic calculation inside an already-published modified-gravity framework — not a new theory proposal, not new data. The stakes are correspondingly modest: does this specific corrected version of Verlinde gravity also reproduce a known galactic regularity that MOND already gets in rough order, or does it fail to? A pass is a mild point in the theory's favor; a clean failure would have been a real problem for the 2024 fix this paper depends on.

**Prior Belief Check.** This is not a surprising result to experts in the sense of overturning anything — Milgrom already showed decades ago that MOND predicts a central-surface-density scale of order a_M/G (the paper says so itself in its abstract), and Verlinde's theory is explicitly constructed to reproduce MOND-like phenomenology in this same low-acceleration regime, so most people in the field would have expected *something* in this ballpark to fall out once someone did the calculation. What's more genuinely interesting — including, by the paper's own account in its AI-disclosure section, to the author himself — is that the derived profile shape turned out to be cuspy (density rising as 1/r toward the center) rather than the cored, flat-centered shape Donato et al.'s Burkert-profile fits assumed. That's a real point of tension buried under the headline number, not a confirmation of it (more on this in Section 5).

**Replication & Convergence Note.** This is a single-author result built directly on the same single author's own single prior paper — the 2024 quadrature-combination fix, itself checked against exactly one observational regime (wide binaries) via one summary ratio. So there are two stacked single-source dependencies here, not one: nobody outside Yoon has independently tested the combination-law modification itself, and nobody outside this paper has independently derived or checked this specific phantom-halo profile calculation. Independent confirmation would mean another group testing the quadrature relation against a different dataset (rotation curves, galaxy clusters, lensing), and someone other than Yoon reproducing the profile derivation — ideally numerically rather than via the stated analytic approximation, and across the full radial range rather than just the "near, but not extremely close to, the center" window this paper restricts itself to.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Two things stand out, and they're not the same thing, even though the abstract only headlines one of them.

**First**, and least interesting on its own: this is the first time anyone has run the numbers to check whether Yoon's corrected Verlinde-gravity relation reproduces Donato et al.'s central-surface-density regularity, and it does, to within the observed scatter. New to the field in the narrow sense that nobody had done this specific calculation with this specific (2024, single-author) modification before.

**Second, and more interesting**: buried in the paper's "Declaration of generative AI" section is an admission that changes the physics. The author initially assumed the phantom dark matter density would approach a flat, non-zero constant near the center — mirroring the Burkert profile's own cored assumption, since that's the profile he's comparing against. During revision, that assumption was checked against the actual relation between local density and enclosed mass:

$$\rho_{ph}(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\frac{dM_{ph}(r)}{dr}$$

**Symbol definitions:** ρ_ph(r) — the phantom (apparent) dark matter density at radius r; M_ph(r) — the phantom dark matter mass enclosed within radius r; the 4πr² factor is just the area of a thin spherical shell at radius r.

**What this actually means:** this is the ordinary spherical-shell mass-accounting identity — local density equals the derivative of the enclosed-mass curve, divided by the shell's own area — the same relationship as recovering an instantaneous rate from a cumulative counter by differentiating the actual curve, rather than assuming the rate is constant just because the counter itself looks smooth. Applied honestly to how M_ph(r) actually grows under the quadrature-combination force law, this relation forces ρ_ph(r) to be locally proportional to 1/r near the center — a cusp, not a core.

That's a structurally different claim than "we matched a known number." It says: *this specific version of Verlinde gravity doesn't just predict a characteristic surface-density scale — it predicts a specific, falsifiable radial shape, and that shape is a cusp, not the cored shape the observational comparison point (Donato's Burkert fits) assumes.* The paper is upfront that this makes its own headline comparison only approximate — matching a magnitude while quietly disagreeing on functional form — and that's a more honest and more interesting position than the abstract's single number lets on at first glance.

**Predictive Content Check.**

1. **Falsifiable handle (always):** the surface-density magnitude match is closer to *relabeling a known coincidence* than predicting something new — MOND already lands in the same ballpark via a_M/G, and this paper says so itself. The genuinely falsifiable content is the cusp claim: this version of Verlinde gravity predicts a 1/r-cuspy inner phantom-halo profile, not a cored one. That's directly checkable against detailed inner-profile shape measurements in nearby dwarf and low-surface-brightness galaxies — exactly the observational battleground of the long-running "core-cusp problem," where much of the literature has favored cored profiles over cuspy ones. If that literature holds up, it's a real tension for this specific combination law, not just an approximation artifact.
2. **Formalism load (conditional, fires here):** the quadrature-combination law is what's doing the real work — it's the specific ingredient that turns a would-be-flat center into a 1/r cusp once the mass-density relation is applied correctly. I can't independently verify *how* load-bearing beyond that, since the actual derivation chain isn't in what I could retrieve — flagging that as a genuine gap rather than asserting confidence I don't have.

As a rough independent sanity check (not from the paper, mine): a_M/G works out to roughly 860 M☉/pc² by straightforward unit conversion (1.2×10⁻¹⁰ m/s² ÷ 6.674×10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹s⁻² ≈ 1.8 kg/m², converted through 1 M☉/pc² ≈ 2.09×10⁻³ kg/m²) — about 5-6× larger than either Donato's or Yoon's number. That's still "the same order of magnitude" in the loose sense the paper's own caveat allows for ("the precise numerical coefficient can depend on the interpolating function"), but it's worth knowing the naive a_M/G estimate isn't a tight match either — MOND's own stated agreement here already depends on an unspecified numerical prefactor, same as this paper's does.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**The observational anchor itself is contested.** The paper's own introduction cites five separate studies (refs. 6–10) arguing the central surface density isn't actually constant, but instead correlates with luminosity, halo mass, morphology, color, or gas content — and a sixth (ref. 11) questioning whether MOND's own acceleration scale a_M is even universal. **(B) Contested** — this is a live empirical dispute in the literature, not a settled fact either way; reasonable researchers land on different sides depending on sample selection and fitting methodology. **(broader literature, cited in paper's own Introduction)**

**The derived profile shape doesn't match the comparison profile's shape.** The headline number matches a Burkert (cored) fit's surface density, but the actual derived profile is cuspy — the paper states this explicitly as a limitation to its own comparison. **(A) Consensus** — this is the paper's own stated caveat, not a criticism I'm adding. **(paper's own abstract and discussion, as excerpted)**

**Both layers of this result rest on a single author's own single prior calibration.** The quadrature-combination law was tested against exactly one dataset (wide binaries, one ratio number) in a single 2024 paper by the same author, and this paper is the first and only application of it to a halo-profile calculation. **(C) Speculative** — this is my own read on how much epistemic weight the result should carry, not something the paper states about itself; a specialist in the Verlinde-gravity literature might have a view on how solid the 2024 fix already is that I don't have access to. **(analyst inference)**

**The result only covers a restricted radial and acceleration window.** By the paper's own framing, the calculation holds "near, but not extremely close to, the center" and only where the local acceleration is "significantly greater than 10⁻¹⁰ m/s²" — it explicitly doesn't cover the innermost cusp region itself or the deep low-acceleration regime. **(A) Consensus** — this is the paper's own explicit scope restriction, stated directly in the abstract. **(paper's own abstract)**

**The technical justification for the quadrature combination law isn't available to me.** I can tell you *that* Yoon proposed replacing addition with quadrature to fix "a technical problem" in Verlinde's original superposition, but not the actual argument for why quadrature is the right fix rather than some other correction — that lives in the 2024 paper's derivation, which I also couldn't retrieve. **(C) Speculative, in the sense that I can't rule out this being either a well-motivated or a somewhat ad hoc fix from what's in front of me** — this is an access gap, not a paper-stated limitation. **(analyst inference / retrieval gap)**

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. A modified-gravity theory (Verlinde's, with Yoon's own 2024 quadrature fix) reproduces, to within observational uncertainty, a 15-year-old dark-matter-halo regularity that standard dark matter theory still can't explain without invoking complicated, unidentified galaxy-formation physics — MOND already got here first in rough order, so this is confirmatory rather than a first discovery, but it's a real point in the theory's favor.

2. The more interesting result is buried, not headlined: correctly applying the mass-density relation shows this theory actually predicts a cuspy (not cored) inner halo profile, which quietly undercuts the very comparison the headline number is built on — the magnitude matches, the shape doesn't, and the paper says so itself.

3. Everything here rests on one author's own single, previously single-dataset-tested modification to Verlinde's formula — worth watching for independent replication before weighting this too heavily, and the paper's own AI-disclosure section is a nice touch of transparency about how the profile-shape correction was actually caught during revision.

## 9. Shorter Summary

A century-old puzzle in galaxy dynamics — do galaxies have invisible "dark matter," or is gravity itself different at low accelerations? — has a curious side-regularity: when you fit galactic dark-matter halos with a particular flat-centered density profile, the product of central density and core radius comes out nearly the same number across galaxies of wildly different sizes, from dwarf galaxies to giant spirals. Standard dark matter theory has no easy explanation for why; MOND, a rival theory that modifies gravity's law at low accelerations instead of adding invisible matter, gets roughly the right number almost automatically, because it has one built-in acceleration constant that sets the scale directly.

This short paper checks whether a second modify-gravity contender, Verlinde's "emergent gravity," also gets this number right — using a 2024 fix by the same author that changes how Verlinde's theory combines ordinary gravity with its own "apparent dark matter" contribution (adding them in quadrature, like combining two independent error sources, rather than simply adding them directly). The result lands about 23% above the observed value, comfortably within the observational uncertainty — a pass, though not a dramatic one, and one MOND already achieved by similar reasoning decades earlier.

The more interesting finding is quieter: working the calculation through correctly (a correction the author credits to ChatGPT catching during revision) shows this version of Verlinde gravity actually predicts a halo that gets denser toward the center in a specific "cuspy" way, not the flat-centered shape the comparison profile assumed. So the magnitude matches, but the shape doesn't — a genuine, checkable prediction (testable against real galaxies' measured inner density profiles) hiding under a number that looks, at first glance, like simple confirmation.

Both the theory's fix and this new calculation come from the same single author, tested so far against a total of two observational comparisons (wide binary stars, and this halo-surface-density number) — worth remembering as one data point in an ongoing modified-gravity research program, not yet a broadly vetted result.
