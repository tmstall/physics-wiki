## 2. How resonance breathing works, in one page

**The reflex.** Pressure sensors in the neck and chest tell the brainstem to slow the heart when blood pressure rises and speed it up when pressure falls. That is the **baroreflex**.

**The catch: it's late.** A change in heart rate takes a few seconds to show up as a pressure change. The arteries are a big elastic tank that fills gradually. So the reflex always corrects a few seconds behind.

**The shower picture.** Picture a shower with a long pipe: you turn the knob, nothing happens, then it overshoots, and you end up swinging hot and cold. A controller that reacts late tends to swing with a period of **twice its delay**.

**Breathing is a rhythmic push on the knob.** Breathing in speeds the heart and breathing out slows it (respiratory sinus arrhythmia).

- **Breath period = 2 × the reflex's delay:** the reflex's late correction lands *in step* with the breath's push. They add together, and heart rate swings in a big smooth wave. This is **resonance**. Heart rate peaks as the in-breath ends, and blood pressure moves opposite to heart rate.
- **Breath period = 1 × the delay** (normal breathing, ~12/min): the correction lands *against* the push, and the swing stays small.

**My version.** An 8.7 s breath means my effective reflex lag is about **4.35 s** (half of 8.7).

**Why there's a sweet spot and not a range.** The reflex only works in a middle band of speeds:

- **Too slow** (a 30 s+ cycle): the pressure sensors respond to *change*, and a slow drift barely registers.
- **Too fast**: the arterial "tank" smooths out quick heart-rate wiggles, so there's nothing to react to.
- **Inside the band**, only one rate makes the lag line up with half a breath. That's my 8.7 s.

**Key evidence from the research:**

- In the 2002 blood-pressure study, heart rate swung **4–10× harder than at rest** near 0.1 Hz. In every participant, that was exactly where heart rate and blood pressure moved in perfect opposition.
- The effect also appears with rhythmic muscle tension or pictures shown every 10 s, without changing breathing. So it's a heart-and-vessel property; breathing is just the easiest handle.
- **Resonance rate differs between people.** It ran 4.5–6.5 breaths/min in a 56-person study, averaging 5.6, and was slower in taller people. Mine, at 6.9/min, sits at the fast end. That's plausible, since the 2002 blood-pressure study found peaks up to 0.111 Hz (6.7/min).
- **Age** doesn't move the rate, but it shrinks the size of the effect. The heart-rate-power boost in over-40s was roughly 30–70% of that in under-40s.

## 3. What my test results mean

- **A sharp peak means a strong reflex.** In a simple delayed-loop model, a noticeable drop at just ±0.5 s means the reflex feeds back roughly **80% or more** of each disturbance (loop gain ≳ 0.8). A weak reflex would show a broad, flat hump.
  - *Caveat:* this is an illustrative model, not a fit to my data.
- **The 3.2 / 5.5 in/out split.** The two studies that compared inhale/exhale ratios disagree. One found equal in and out gave the biggest swing; the other found longer exhales gave more relaxation. My resonance was measured *at this ratio*. If I ever change the ratio, re-run the swing test.
- **Sleepiness at 15–20 min** fits a strong parasympathetic shift. Keep separating it from overbreathing (next section).

## 4. Practice rules

1. **Breathe slow and gentle, not deep.** Big breaths blow off CO₂, which shifts the bicarbonate buffer toward alkalosis and *suppresses* the heart-rate swing. Warning signs of overbreathing are tingling, lightheadedness and a "floaty" feeling. The fix is smaller breaths at the same rate.
2. **The steering rule.** At resonance, heart rate rises through the in-breath and **peaks right as the in-breath ends**.
   - Peaks *early* → breathing too slowly → shorten the breath a little.
   - Peaks *late* → breathing too fast → lengthen it a little.
3. **Dose by effect:**
   - 10 min for calm focus.
   - 15–20 min when winding down, since I get sleepy. Maybe not right before focused work.

## 6. The Ohm vs. Polar sync test

Goal: find out how well the lamp's palm sensor and pacing match my chest-strap reference.

1. **Setup.** Polar chest strap → Elite HRV running; hold the Ohm stone in my palm at the same time. Sit still, same time of day, no caffeine just before.
2. **Session A: lamp paces me.** Let the lamp guide for 10 min. Note:
   - where its pace **settles** (seconds per breath): near 8.7 s or near 10 s?
   - the **heart-rate swing** on Elite HRV compared with my usual 8.7 s sessions.
3. **Session B: I pace at 8.7 s** (manual lamp setting if available, otherwise the Elite HRV pacer). Record on both devices.
4. **Compare:**
   - **Swing size:** peak-to-trough heart rate on Ohm vs Polar. Optical sensors often read a little smaller.
   - **Beat timing** (if Ohm exports intervals): line up beats. A few ms of disagreement is good; tens of ms means the palm sensor is struggling. Watch whether errors cluster at a particular point in each breath (the pulse-travel-time effect).
   - **Phase:** do both show heart rate peaking at the end of the in-breath?
5. **Verdict:**
   - Ohm matches Polar and settles near 8.7 s → use the lamp alone.
   - It drifts toward 10 s or reads a much smaller swing → set the pace manually, or keep the Polar as the reference for occasional checks.

## 7. Re-checking my resonance rate

Once in a while, e.g. every few months, or after changing the in/out ratio:

- Polar + Elite HRV, 2–3 min each at **8.2 s, 8.7 s, 9.2 s** (add 7.7 s and 9.7 s if the peak seems to have moved).
- Keep the rate with the biggest, smoothest swing where heart rate peaks at the end of the in-breath.
- Research note: whether resonance rate stays fixed over time is debated. One study found it stable over 10 weeks; another saw a shift of about 1 breath/min. My own re-checks are the real answer.
