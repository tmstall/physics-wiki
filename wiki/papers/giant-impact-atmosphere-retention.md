# Giant Impacts That Keep a Hydrogen Envelope

**One-line summary:** In this energy budget a late giant impact makes a young planet heavier faster than it makes the escaping gas hotter, so the planet is more likely to keep its hydrogen; the radius valley still appears in the no-impact control. Modirrousta-Galian and Korenaga, *Nature Astronomy* (2026).

## Key claims and results

- **Paper:** DOI [10.1038/s41550-026-03006-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-026-03006-1). A population model, not a new observation. One hundred million synthetic planets.
- Escape depends exponentially on how deep the gravity well is compared with the temperature at the sonic point. The collision adds mass and heat in one ledger. Iron sinks and keeps its energy in the core. About 85% of the energy reaches the mantle, and much of that melts rock. Surface temperatures of thousands to about 30,000 K still fail to set the sonic-point temperature unless convection reaches that far. Under the authors’ earlier rule, starlight sets that temperature.
- Hit planets climb over a mass threshold and stay sub-Neptunes. Unhit planets stay lighter and lose their envelopes over billions of years, becoming super-Earths. The run places peaks near 1.3 and 2.4 Earth radii and a gap near 1.75. The hit fraction changes the ratio of the two families.
- Removing impacts still leaves a valley. Stretching the hot phase to billions of years changes little. Impacts act as a mass boost inside this model. The stated prediction — sub-Neptunes have heavier rocky cores — is also what photoevaporation and core-powered loss predict.

## Physical intuition

A collision is usually told as a heater that blows the air off. The missing half is the weight. Binding energy grows faster with mass than the deposited heat does (roughly mass to the 0.75 versus mass to the 0.58 in the analysis’s scaling). If the gas that actually escapes never feels the new surface heat, the extra weight wins and the envelope sticks.

## Limitations and assumptions

- The cold-phase escape fit is used outside the hot, conduction-dominated regime it was built for. That phase sets the threshold.
- The largest impactors might couple the surface to the sonic point. The paper’s regime test lives in a 2026 *Icarus* paper that this analysis did not re-read.
- Photoevaporation is omitted for unimpacted planets. Shock stripping of 10–20% of the envelope is neglected. Hydrogen dissolving into the magma ocean is set aside.
- The match to the radius histogram is visual. Envelope normalization is tuned to the upper peak.
