**Nodeless Nickelate Gap Hands Theorists a Clean s-Wave Clue**  
By locking oxygen in place during transfer and mapping every momentum point, Shen, Zhou, He, Xue, Chen and colleagues deliver the first unambiguous view of a fully gapped superconducting state plus a sharp 70-meV boson kink in ambient-pressure bilayer nickelate films—two direct experimental anchors for pairing symmetry and glue.

**Big-Picture Context**  
Ruddlesden-Popper bilayer nickelates exploded onto the scene in 2023 when bulk La₃Ni₂O₇ showed ~80 K superconductivity under high pressure. Thin films soon followed at ambient pressure, opening the door to surface-sensitive probes that pressure cells had blocked. The central open questions mirrored the cuprate puzzle: is the gap d-wave with nodes, and what actually glues the Cooper pairs—spin fluctuations, phonons, or something else?

This Science paper attacks both questions head-on with ARPES on (La,Pr,Sm)₃Ni₂O₇ films. The authors engineered a cryogenic ultra-high-vacuum quench-and-transfer protocol that preserves oxygen stoichiometry from growth chamber to spectrometer. What they found rewrites the symmetry discussion and supplies a concrete boson energy scale at the same time.

Nickelate films now sit alongside cuprates and iron-based superconductors as a third major high-Tc family where clean spectroscopy can finally test mechanism hypotheses without extreme pressure. The nodeless gap plus visible electron-boson coupling give theorists two hard numbers instead of another set of indirect inferences.

**Necessary Background Crash-Course**  
ARPES works by firing photons at the sample, ejecting electrons, and recording both their kinetic energy and emission angle. From those two numbers you reconstruct the occupied band structure E(k) right up to the Fermi surface. Think of it as a high-resolution traffic camera that photographs every car’s position and speed on every highway lane simultaneously instead of averaging traffic flow.

A superconducting gap opens below Tc when electrons form Cooper pairs and remove spectral weight from the Fermi level. In a nodeless gap the minimum energy cost to break a pair stays finite in every direction on the Fermi surface. Contrast that with d-wave gaps in cuprates, which drop to zero along the Brillouin-zone diagonals—like a fortress wall with open gates at the corners that let unpaired electrons leak through.

A dispersion kink appears when electrons couple strongly to a bosonic mode (phonon, magnon, or plasmon). At the boson energy the quasiparticle velocity suddenly changes because the electron can emit or absorb that boson and scatter. It is the solid-state equivalent of a highway that narrows abruptly at a toll plaza tuned to one resonant vibration frequency of the roadbed; cars slow down and the “speed limit” plot shows a sharp kink exactly at that frequency.

**Core Technical Explanation**  
The team grew compressively strained (La,Pr,Sm)₃Ni₂O₇ films on suitable substrates, then developed a liquid-nitrogen-cooled UHV quenching and rapid-transfer protocol that prevents apical-oxygen loss during the journey from Shenzhen growth lab to Hefei ARPES endstation. Without that step the surface would reconstruct or become non-superconducting and the measurement would be meaningless.

Once in the spectrometer at temperatures well below Tc they map the electronic structure. Along the Brillouin-zone diagonal—the direction where a d_{x²-y²} gap must vanish—they observe a ~18 meV gap that opens with sharp coherence peaks on both sides of the Fermi level. These peaks are the spectroscopic fingerprint of Bogoliubov quasiparticles, the coherent superpositions of electron and hole that exist only inside the superconducting state.

They then rotate the sample and measure the gap at many other momenta across the entire Brillouin zone. The gap stays open everywhere; its minimum never reaches zero. That single fact eliminates pure d-wave symmetry and is consistent with s-wave or s± pairing (the latter allowing a sign change between different Fermi-surface pockets but still nodeless).

At the same time the quasiparticle dispersion itself shows an abrupt change in slope—a kink—at approximately 70 meV binding energy. This kink is the classic self-energy signature of electrons coupling to a bosonic mode whose characteristic energy matches the kink position. The mode could be a high-energy optical phonon involving Ni-O bonds or a magnetic collective excitation; either way the coupling is strong enough to be visible in the single-particle spectrum.

They do both measurements on the same pristine films because the oxygen-preserving transfer finally makes the experiment possible. Previous attempts either lost superconductivity or measured altered surfaces, so the community lacked simultaneous symmetry and coupling data.

**What’s Genuinely New or Clever**  
The decisive technical advance is the cryogenic UHV quench-transfer method itself; it solves a materials problem that had blocked clean spectroscopy on these films. The scientific payoff is the first simultaneous observation of a nodeless gap and a clear boson kink in the same ambient-pressure nickelate samples. That combination directly constrains both pairing symmetry and the energy scale of the putative glue—something pressure-cell studies could not deliver.

**Limitations & Open Questions**  
ARPES is surface-sensitive; whether the observed gap and kink faithfully represent the bulk electronic structure remains to be cross-checked with bulk-sensitive probes. The precise Tc of these particular films is not highlighted in the available summaries, but film Tc values are often lower than the 80 K bulk pressure value. The 70 meV mode energy is high for acoustic phonons and sits in the range of optical oxygen modes or paramagnons—yet the experiment does not identify which boson is responsible or prove it actually mediates pairing rather than merely coexisting with it.

s± versus plain s-wave still needs phase-sensitive tests. Oxygen stoichiometry, strain, and rare-earth doping all affect the electronic structure; this work reports one composition and one strain state. Follow-up work in the next 12–24 months should include oxygen-isotope substitution, resonant inelastic X-ray scattering or neutron scattering to characterize the 70 meV mode, and Eliashberg calculations that feed the measured coupling strength back into Tc predictions.

**Three Crystallized Takeaways**  
1. These ambient-pressure nickelate films form Cooper pairs with a nodeless gap, pointing to s-wave or s± symmetry rather than the nodal d-wave familiar from cuprates.  
2. A clear kink at 70 meV demonstrates strong electron-boson coupling and hands the community a concrete energy scale for whatever collective mode may be helping glue the pairs.  
3. The cryo-transfer breakthrough finally lets ARPES work on these films; nickelate high-Tc research now has direct spectroscopic anchor points instead of only transport and pressure data.

**Detailed Summary & Explanation**  
Shen et al. report angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy on superconducting (La,Pr,Sm)₃Ni₂O₇ Ruddlesden-Popper bilayer thin films. They developed a liquid-nitrogen-cooled ultra-high-vacuum quenching and transfer technique that preserves the delicate oxygen stoichiometry during sample movement between growth and measurement chambers. In the superconducting state they observe a ~18 meV gap that opens with pronounced coherence peaks even along the Brillouin-zone diagonal. Mapping the gap across the full Brillouin zone shows it remains finite everywhere, establishing a nodeless superconducting gap consistent with s-wave (including s±) pairing symmetry.

Concurrently, the quasiparticle dispersion exhibits a sharp kink at ~70 meV below the Fermi level. This kink is the standard spectroscopic signature of electrons coupling to a bosonic mode of that energy. The simultaneous presence of a nodeless gap and clear electron-boson coupling supplies direct experimental input on both the symmetry of the order parameter and the energy scale of the interaction that may drive pairing in these ambient-pressure nickelate superconductors.

The summary above captures the central claims. What it means in plain language is that the authors have removed two major ambiguities that had clouded the nickelate mechanism discussion. The gap symmetry is no longer compatible with a simple nodal d-wave picture, and a bosonic mode at a well-defined energy is now experimentally required to be part of any viable theoretical description. These two facts give theorists concrete targets instead of another round of model-dependent inferences.

**Shorter Summary**  
ARPES on oxygen-preserved (La,Pr,Sm)₃Ni₂O₇ films shows a nodeless ~18 meV superconducting gap everywhere in momentum space together with a 70 meV dispersion kink from electron-boson coupling. The results favor s-wave or s± pairing symmetry and identify a concrete bosonic energy scale likely relevant to the pairing glue in these ambient-pressure bilayer nickelate superconductors.
