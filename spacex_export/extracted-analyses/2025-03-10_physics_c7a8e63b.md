### Understanding the Physics Behind Mach Diamonds

Mach diamonds, often observed in the exhaust plumes of jet engines and rockets, are striking diamond-shaped patterns that reveal the intricate physics of supersonic flows. These phenomena, also known as shock diamonds or thrust diamonds, are named after Ernst Mach, a physicist renowned for his work on supersonic flows and shock waves. Below, we explore what Mach diamonds are, why they form, and the underlying physics driving their appearance.

#### What Are Mach Diamonds?

Mach diamonds are visible, repeating patterns of bright and dark regions in the exhaust plume of a jet engine or rocket, particularly noticeable during high-speed operations such as takeoffs or launches. They occur when the exhaust gases exit the nozzle at supersonic speeds—faster than the speed of sound, corresponding to a Mach number greater than 1. These patterns are not just visually captivating; they are a direct manifestation of complex fluid dynamics at play.

#### Why Do Mach Diamonds Form?

The formation of Mach diamonds is tied to a mismatch between the pressure of the exhaust gases at the nozzle exit and the surrounding ambient pressure. Rocket and jet engine nozzles are designed to expand exhaust gases to optimize thrust, ideally matching the exit pressure to the ambient pressure at a specific altitude or condition. However, when this balance is disrupted—either because the rocket operates at a different altitude or the jet engine runs in an afterburning mode—the exhaust becomes **underexpanded** or **overexpanded**, triggering the conditions for Mach diamonds.

- **Underexpanded Flow**: If the nozzle exit pressure is higher than the ambient pressure (common at high altitudes or in space), the exhaust gases continue to expand after leaving the nozzle. This expansion generates a series of waves that create the diamond pattern.
- **Overexpanded Flow**: If the exit pressure is lower than the ambient pressure (possible at lower altitudes for a nozzle designed for higher altitudes), the exhaust is compressed, also leading to wave formations that can produce diamonds, though the pattern may differ slightly.

While Mach diamonds can occur in both scenarios, they are most prominently associated with underexpanded flows, especially in rockets ascending through the atmosphere or operating in near-vacuum conditions.

#### The Physics Behind Mach Diamonds

The physics of Mach diamonds hinges on the behavior of supersonic flows and the interaction of two key types of waves: **shock waves** and **expansion waves**. Let’s break this down step-by-step:

1. **Supersonic Flow Requirement**  
   For Mach diamonds to form, the exhaust must exit the nozzle at a speed exceeding the local speed of sound (Mach number > 1). This supersonic condition allows the formation of shock waves, which are abrupt changes in pressure, temperature, and density within the flow.

2. **Pressure Mismatch**  
   When the exhaust pressure at the nozzle exit does not match the ambient pressure, the flow adjusts through a series of waves:
   - In an **underexpanded jet**, the high-pressure exhaust expands rapidly upon exiting the nozzle. This expansion occurs via **Prandtl-Meyer expansion fans**, which are regions where the flow spreads out and accelerates.
   - In an **overexpanded jet**, the low-pressure exhaust is compressed by the higher ambient pressure, often forming a shock wave just inside or outside the nozzle.

3. **Wave Interactions**  
   The diamond pattern emerges from the reflection and intersection of these waves:
   - **Expansion Fans**: In an underexpanded flow, expansion fans originate at the nozzle lip and propagate outward. When they reach the free jet boundary (the interface between the exhaust and the surrounding air), they reflect back as **compression waves**.
   - **Shock Waves**: These compression waves can coalesce into oblique shock waves—angled discontinuities that compress the flow. As these shock waves reflect off the jet boundary, they turn back into expansion waves, and the cycle repeats.
   - This alternating process of expansion and compression creates a series of **diamond-shaped cells** along the jet axis, where each diamond represents a region of high or low pressure, density, and temperature.

4. **Visibility of the Pattern**  
   The diamonds become visible due to variations in the exhaust’s properties:
   - **Temperature and Density Changes**: In rockets, the hot exhaust gases emit light, especially in low-light conditions, making the pattern stand out. Bright regions often correspond to high-temperature zones after compression, while darker areas indicate expansion.
   - **Condensation Effects**: In some cases, water vapor in the exhaust can condense, enhancing visibility.
   - **Schlieren Photography**: In laboratory settings, techniques like schlieren imaging exploit density gradients to reveal these patterns clearly.

#### Key Factors Influencing Mach Diamonds

- **Pressure Ratio**: The difference between the nozzle exit pressure and ambient pressure dictates the strength and spacing of the diamonds.
- **Nozzle Design**: The shape and expansion ratio of the nozzle influence how the exhaust adjusts to ambient conditions.
- **Gas Properties**: The specific heat ratio of the exhaust gas affects wave propagation and the resulting pattern.

#### Practical Implications

Beyond their visual appeal, Mach diamonds have engineering significance. The presence of strong shock waves can impact the stability and efficiency of a propulsion system. For rocket designers, understanding these patterns helps optimize nozzle performance across varying altitudes. In jet engines, particularly those with afterburners, Mach diamonds signal the intense, supersonic nature of the exhaust, often seen in military aircraft during high-thrust maneuvers.

#### Conclusion

Mach diamonds are a fascinating demonstration of fluid dynamics in action. They arise from the interplay of supersonic exhaust flows with mismatched pressures, resulting in a repeating pattern of shock and expansion waves. These waves reflect and intersect to form the characteristic diamond shapes, made visible by the glowing exhaust or density variations. Whether observed in a rocket launch or a fighter jet’s afterburner, Mach diamonds offer a window into the physics of high-speed propulsion, blending scientific complexity with striking beauty.

### Explaining the Physics Behind Mach Diamonds in Jet Engine and Rocket Exhaust

Mach diamonds, also known as shock diamonds, are fascinating diamond-shaped patterns that appear in the exhaust plumes of jet engines and rockets under specific conditions. These striking visual phenomena are a direct result of the physics of supersonic gas flow and the interaction between the exhaust gases and the surrounding atmosphere. Below, I’ll explain in detail the underlying principles, focusing on supersonic flow dynamics, shock wave formation, and the conditions that make these patterns visible.

---

#### **What Are Mach Diamonds?**
Mach diamonds are periodic, diamond-like structures observed in the exhaust plume of a jet engine or rocket when the exhaust gases exit the nozzle at supersonic speeds (i.e., faster than the speed of sound). These patterns arise due to a mismatch between the pressure of the exhaust gas at the nozzle exit and the ambient atmospheric pressure. The "Mach" in Mach diamonds refers to their association with supersonic flow, where the Mach number (the ratio of the flow speed to the speed of sound) exceeds 1. The diamond shape emerges from the geometric arrangement of shock waves and expansion fans in the exhaust plume.

---

#### **Basic Principles of Exhaust Flow**
To understand Mach diamonds, let’s start with how jet engines and rockets generate thrust:

- **Jet Engines**: In a jet engine, air is compressed, mixed with fuel, ignited, and expelled through a nozzle at high speed. Depending on the engine design and operating conditions (e.g., whether it’s a subsonic or supersonic aircraft), the exhaust can be subsonic or supersonic. For powerful engines, such as those with afterburners, the exhaust is often supersonic even in subsonic aircraft.
- **Rockets**: Rocket engines burn fuel and an oxidizer, producing hot combustion gases that are accelerated through a nozzle. Rocket nozzles are typically designed to produce supersonic exhaust to maximize thrust, especially since rockets operate across a wide range of altitudes, from sea level to the vacuum of space.

In both cases, the nozzle plays a critical role in accelerating the exhaust gases. When the exhaust speed exceeds the local speed of sound, it becomes supersonic, setting the stage for Mach diamonds to form.

---

#### **Supersonic Flow and Pressure Mismatch**
The key to Mach diamond formation lies in the behavior of supersonic flow exiting the nozzle and its interaction with the ambient air. The nozzle is designed to expand the exhaust gases, ideally matching the exit pressure \( P_e \) to the ambient pressure \( P_a \). This condition is called **perfect expansion**, where the exhaust flow exits uniformly without forming shock waves.

However, perfect expansion is rare in practice:
- **Underexpanded Flow**: If the exit pressure \( P_e \) is higher than the ambient pressure \( P_a \) (common in rockets at low altitudes or jet engines at sea level), the exhaust continues to expand after leaving the nozzle.
- **Overexpanded Flow**: If the exit pressure \( P_e \) is lower than the ambient pressure \( P_a \) (possible in rockets at high altitudes or jet engines designed for specific conditions), the ambient air compresses the exhaust upon exit.

This pressure mismatch triggers a series of adjustments in the supersonic flow, leading to the formation of shock waves and expansion fans—the building blocks of Mach diamonds.

---

#### **Formation of Mach Diamonds**
Here’s how Mach diamonds form step-by-step:

1. **Exit from the Nozzle**:
   - When the supersonic exhaust exits the nozzle in an underexpanded state (\( P_e > P_a \)), it expands rapidly to adjust to the lower ambient pressure. This expansion occurs through **Prandtl-Meyer expansion fans**, which are regions where the flow spreads out and accelerates further.

2. **Reflection at the Jet Boundary**:
   - The expansion fans propagate outward until they reach the **free jet boundary**, the interface between the exhaust gas and the ambient air. At this boundary, the expansion waves reflect back toward the jet axis as **compression waves**.

3. **Formation of Shock Waves**:
   - As these compression waves converge toward the jet axis, they can coalesce into **oblique shock waves**, which are angled discontinuities where pressure, temperature, and density increase abruptly. These shocks cross the jet axis, causing a sudden compression of the flow.

4. **Reflection as Expansion Fans**:
   - When the oblique shock waves reflect off the jet axis, they transform back into expansion fans. This cycle of expansion and compression repeats, creating a periodic structure along the length of the exhaust plume.

5. **Diamond Pattern**:
   - The intersections of these oblique shock waves and expansion fans form a series of diamond-shaped or disk-like cells. In a two-dimensional view, they appear as diamonds; in a three-dimensional axisymmetric jet, they resemble a stack of disks, but from the side, the diamond shape is still apparent.

For **overexpanded flow** (\( P_e < P_a \)), the process begins with oblique shock waves forming just inside or outside the nozzle to compress the flow, followed by expansion fans, and the cycle continues similarly.

---

#### **Visibility of Mach Diamonds**
Mach diamonds become visible due to changes in the exhaust gas properties across the shock waves and expansion fans:
- **Temperature and Pressure**: At the shock wave intersections, the sudden increase in pressure and temperature can cause the exhaust to glow brighter, especially in hot exhausts like those from rockets or afterburning jet engines.
- **Density Variations**: Changes in gas density can refract light or cause condensation if moisture or particles are present, making the patterns visible even in cooler exhausts.
- **Optical Effects**: Techniques like schlieren photography, which highlight density gradients, can reveal Mach diamonds in experimental settings.

In rockets, the exhaust is often extremely hot and contains incandescent particles, making the diamonds vivid. In jet engines, they are most noticeable with afterburners engaged, where the exhaust temperature is high enough to emit visible light.

---

#### **Spacing of Mach Diamonds**
The distance between successive Mach diamonds (the "wavelength" of the pattern) depends on the flow properties:
- It is influenced by the nozzle exit diameter \( D \), the jet Mach number \( M_j \) (the Mach number at the nozzle exit), and the pressure ratio \( P_e / P_a \).
- A simplified empirical relation for the spacing \( L \) in an underexpanded jet is often approximated as proportional to \( D \) and a function of the Mach number and pressure mismatch. One common form is:
  \[
  L \propto D \sqrt{\frac{P_e}{P_a} - 1}
  \]
  However, more precise models involve complex gas dynamics equations, and the exact spacing can be derived numerically or measured experimentally.

---

#### **Real-World Observations**
- **Rockets**: During a launch, you might see a long chain of Mach diamonds near the ground, where the ambient pressure is high, and the rocket nozzle (designed for higher altitudes) produces an underexpanded jet. As the rocket ascends and ambient pressure drops, the number of visible diamonds may decrease.
- **Jet Engines**: In military jets with afterburners, Mach diamonds are often visible during takeoff or high-thrust maneuvers, indicating a pressure mismatch that isn’t perfectly tuned for efficiency but is unavoidable in variable conditions.

---

#### **Summary**
Mach diamonds are striking visual evidence of the physics of supersonic flow. They form when a supersonic exhaust jet, exiting a nozzle with a pressure mismatch relative to the ambient air, adjusts through a repeating cycle of expansion fans and oblique shock waves. These waves intersect to create a periodic, diamond-shaped pattern, made visible by variations in temperature, pressure, or density in the exhaust gases. The phenomenon is rooted in the principles of gas dynamics and supersonic aerodynamics, showcasing the intricate interplay between engineering design and natural physics in jet engines and rockets.

### Analysis of the Paper: "Phase-cycling and double-quantum two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy using a common-path birefringent interferometer"

This research article, published in *Optica* (Vol. 11, No. 12, December 2024), introduces an innovative adaptation to the Translating Wedge-based Identical pulse eNcoding System (TWINS), a common-path birefringent interferometer, to enable phase-cycling capabilities for two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy (2DES). Authored by Daniel Timmer et al. from institutions in Germany and Italy, the paper demonstrates how this modification allows for the isolation of rephasing (R), non-rephasing (NR), zero-quantum (0Q), and double-quantum (2Q) 2DES signals, using a molecular J-aggregate as a test system. Below is a detailed analysis of the paper’s content, methodology, findings, and implications.

---

#### **1. Overview and Motivation**
- **Objective**: The primary goal is to enhance the TWINS interferometer, traditionally limited by its lack of phase control, to perform advanced 2DES experiments. This involves adding phase-cycling capabilities to isolate distinct quantum pathways (R, NR, 0Q, 2Q), which provide insights into coherent and incoherent dynamics in quantum systems.
- **Context**: 2DES is a powerful ultrafast spectroscopy technique that extends transient absorption (TA) by using two pump pulses and a probe pulse to map excitation and detection frequencies in two dimensions. It requires precise phase control to separate quantum pathways, a challenge for simpler setups like TWINS compared to pulse shaper-based methods.
- **Significance**: TWINS is valued for its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and inherent phase stability, but its inability to perform phase-cycling restricted its use in advanced 2DES. This adaptation bridges that gap, broadening its experimental applicability.

---

#### **2. Technical Innovation: Phase-Cycling TWINS (PC-TWINS)**
- **Traditional TWINS**:
  - Uses birefringent alpha-barium borate (α-BBO) crystals (Y-cut block, X-cut and Z-cut wedge pairs) to split an input pulse into two phase-stable, collinear pump pulses with tunable delay (τ).
  - Relies on the birefringence (difference between ordinary \(n_o\) and extraordinary \(n_e\) refractive indices) to create group delays between s- and p-polarized components.
  - Lacks phase-cycling because the relative phase between pulses (\(\Delta \phi\)) remains fixed at zero with polarizers set at 45°.

- **Modification (PC-TWINS)**:
  - An achromatic quarter-wave plate (QWP) is inserted between the first polarizer (\(\text{Pol}_1\)) and the second polarizer (\(\text{Pol}_2\)).
  - The QWP manipulates the absolute phase difference (\(\Delta \phi_0\)) between the two pump pulses by altering their polarization states:
    - QWP at 45°: \(\Delta \phi_0 = 0\) (no phase shift).
    - QWP at 0°: \(\Delta \phi_0 = -\pi/2\).
    - QWP at 90°: \(\Delta \phi_0 = +\pi/2\).
    - QWP at 45° with \(\text{Pol}_2\) at -45°: \(\Delta \phi_0 = \pi\).
  - Jones calculus confirms that \(\Delta \phi_0(\vartheta) = -\arctan\left(\frac{\cos(2\vartheta)}{\sin^2(2\vartheta)}\right) + \phi_2\), allowing continuous phase tuning between \(-\pi/2\) and \(\pi/2\), with \(\text{Pol}_2\) rotation extending the range.
  - Contrast modulation (\(C(\vartheta)\)) remains high (close to 1) at key phase settings, ensuring signal quality.

- **Verification**:
  - Linear autocorrelation traces (Fig. 2a) and Fourier transforms (Fig. 2b) confirm the expected phase shifts (\(\Delta \phi_0 = 0, \pi/2, -\pi/2, \pi\)) without envelope distortion, validated across a broadband range (680-900 nm).

---

#### **3. Experimental Demonstration**
- **Sample**: A molecular J-aggregate thin film (ProSQ-C16 squaraines) is used as an effective three-level system (3LS) with a ground state (\(|0\rangle\)), single-exciton state (\(|X\rangle\)), and two-exciton state (\(|XX\rangle\)).
- **Setup**: PC-TWINS generates pump pulse pairs, followed by a probe pulse, in a pump-probe geometry. Experiments are conducted at room temperature in vacuum, with a pump fluence of ~10 μJ/cm² to stay within the third-order nonlinear regime.
- **Measurements**:
  - **Rephasing (R) and Non-Rephasing (NR)**:
    - Two-step phase-cycling (\(\Delta \phi_0 = 0, \pi/2\)) isolates R and NR spectra (Fig. 3d-g).
    - R spectra show larger amplitudes due to finite inhomogeneous broadening (\(\sigma_{\text{inh}} = 2 \, \text{meV}\)).
    - Sum of R and NR matches the absorptive 2DES map (Fig. 3i), validated against simulations (Fig. 3j-l) with a dephasing time \(T_2^{1Q} = 160 \, \text{fs}\) and two-exciton shift \(\Delta E = 3 \, \text{meV}\).
  - **Zero-Quantum (0Q) and Double-Quantum (2Q)**:
    - Reversed pulse ordering (probe and second pump coincide, first pump trails with \(\tau < 0\)) enables 0Q and 2Q spectra (Fig. 4c-d).
    - 2Q spectra yield a dephasing time \(T_2^{2Q} = 30 \, \text{fs}\), consistent with a Lindblad model prediction (\(T_2^{2Q} \approx T_2^{1Q}/4\)).
    - 0Q spectra indicate a population relaxation time \(T_1 = 140 \, \text{fs}\), limited by the current delay range (~230 fs).

---

#### **4. Key Findings**
- **Phase Control**: The QWP enables full phase-cycling, overcoming a major limitation of TWINS and matching capabilities of pulse shaper-based systems.
- **Quantum Dynamics**: The J-aggregate exhibits:
  - A long single-quantum dephasing time (\(T_2^{1Q} = 160 \, \text{fs}\)) at room temperature, suggesting high coherence.
  - A small two-exciton shift (\(\Delta E = 3 \pm 2 \, \text{meV}\)), indicating weak excitonic interactions.
  - Rapid 2Q dephasing (\(T_2^{2Q} = 30 \, \text{fs}\)) and a population decay (\(T_1 = 140 \, \text{fs}\)), consistent with prior studies.
- **Validation**: Experimental spectra align well with perturbative simulations based on double-sided Feynman diagrams, confirming the accuracy of PC-TWINS.

---

#### **5. Strengths and Limitations**
- **Strengths**:
  - **Simplicity**: Adding a QWP is an easy, cost-effective upgrade to existing TWINS setups.
  - **Versatility**: Supports a wide range of 2DES experiments (R, NR, 0Q, 2Q) across broadband wavelengths.
  - **Stability**: Retains TWINS’ inherent phase stability while adding phase control.
- **Limitations**:
  - **Delay Range**: The current ~1 ps delay (positive \(\tau\)) and ~230 fs (negative \(\tau\)) may not suffice for systems with longer coherence or relaxation times. Solutions include thicker blocks or additional wedges.
  - **Model Simplicity**: Simulations omit excitonic fine-structure, leading to minor deviations in 0Q and 2Q spectra.

---

#### **6. Implications and Future Directions**
- **Scientific Impact**: PC-TWINS democratizes advanced 2DES by offering a simpler alternative to pulse shapers, enabling studies of coherent couplings, many-body interactions, and doubly excited states in diverse systems (e.g., quantum materials, semiconductors, molecular aggregates).
- **Applications**: Potential extensions include 2DES microscopy and combined multidimensional/photoemission spectroscopy.
- **Future Work**: Low-temperature experiments could further extend coherence times, and thicker delay stages could resolve slower dynamics.

---

#### **7. Conclusion**
This paper presents a significant advancement in ultrafast spectroscopy by equipping TWINS with phase-cycling capabilities via a QWP. The successful demonstration on a J-aggregate validates its utility, revealing detailed quantum dynamics with high fidelity. By overcoming TWINS’ phase control limitation, this work enhances its competitiveness with pulse shaper-based methods, promising broader adoption in multidimensional spectroscopy research.
