**1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook**  
**Ultramassive Black Hole Binary Carves a Starless Kiloparsec Void**  
JWST and VLT observations published April 23 in *Astrophysical Journal Letters* show that a puzzling central “gap” in the brightest cluster galaxy of Abell 402 is not dust-hidden stars but a genuine stellar cavity blasted out by the gravitational three-body slingshot of two ~30-billion-solar-mass black holes spiraling toward merger—the heaviest binary caught in the act.

**2. Big-Picture Context**  
Brightest cluster galaxies (BCGs) sit at the bottom of the deepest gravitational wells and grow through repeated mergers. Standard merger trees in ΛCDM predict steep central stellar cusps, yet real BCGs and giant ellipticals show flattened cores. The leading explanation is “core scouring”: when two supermassive black holes (SMBHs) form a bound binary, they harden by flinging stars (and dark matter) out in three-body encounters, excavating a flat density core whose size scales with the final black-hole mass.

This paper takes that mechanism to an extreme. In A402-BCG at z ≈ 0.322 (roughly 4.4 billion light-years away), McDonald and colleagues find both a large-scale flattened core *and* a smaller, sharper kiloparsec-scale cavity superimposed on it. JWST’s mid-infrared leverage plus HST data cleanly prove the cavity contains no stars at all. The authors interpret the whole structure as the dynamical footprint of an ultramassive binary black hole system caught mid-hardening—the most massive such pair yet identified.

The discovery sits at the intersection of several hot topics the target reader cares about: how the most massive black holes grow, how they reshape their host galaxies on observable scales, and what JWST can reveal once dust obscuration is removed.

**3. Necessary Background Crash-Course**  
Stellar core scouring happens when two galaxies merge and their central black holes sink to the center via dynamical friction. Once they form a hard binary, repeated three-body scatterings with passing stars eject those stars on high-velocity orbits, lowering the central stellar density and leaving a flat “core.” Analogy: two giant wrecking balls chained together in a crowded parking lot; every time a car (star) wanders too close, the binary flings it away at high speed, gradually clearing a flat zone around the pair.

Empirical scaling relations link core size (or “break radius”) to black-hole mass: bigger black holes produce bigger cores because they can scatter stars over larger volumes before the binary stalls. Analogy: the “no-parking” exclusion zone around a massive construction crane scales with the crane’s weight and reach.

Dual AGN at kiloparsec separation with a measurable velocity offset signal a bound binary still hardening. The relative velocity and projected separation let observers estimate total mass via Kepler’s third law. Analogy: two lighthouses on opposite sides of a dark harbor; their different Doppler shifts and fixed distance tell you the combined mass of the pair that keeps them orbiting.

**4. Core Technical Explanation**  
McDonald et al. started with deep JWST NIRCam imaging (F150W2 and F322W2 filters) of the central region of A402-BCG. The images immediately showed a sharp, roughly circular ~1 kpc cavity with almost no diffuse stellar light inside it and a bright mid-infrared point source sitting at one edge. They then pulled archival HST ACS and WFC3 optical/near-IR frames. Color maps and surface-brightness profiles across the cavity showed no wavelength-dependent reddening that would be required for dust extinction; the light is simply absent. That rules out the 2018 dust-cloud hypothesis in one clean step.

MUSE integral-field spectroscopy from the VLT (already in hand from 2017–2019) revealed two distinct nuclear emission-line sources straddling the cavity. One is a classic low-ionization nuclear emission-line region (LINER) AGN; the other shows Seyfert-like line ratios. Their line-of-sight velocity difference is 370 km s⁻¹ and their projected separation is ~2 kpc. Treating them as a bound circular binary and applying Kepler’s law gives a total mass of 6 ± 2 × 10¹⁰ solar masses—by far the most massive binary black-hole candidate known.

On larger scales the team measured a break radius of ~2.2 kpc in the stellar light profile. Plugging that size into the well-calibrated core-size versus black-hole-mass relation yields a central black-hole mass of order 5 × 10¹⁰ solar masses, consistent with the binary total. The stellar mass “missing” from the small cavity is ~2 × 10⁹ solar masses within a volume of ~0.5 kpc³—exactly the deficit expected if the binary has been scattering stars for the last few tens of millions of years.

They propose the cavity itself is the short-lived signature of three-body scattering during binary hardening (or possibly a post-merger recoil kick or a dipole instability in the stellar distribution). The observed cavity size matches the theoretical stalling radius for a binary of this mass.

**5. What’s Genuinely New or Clever**  
The decisive move is the multi-wavelength veto of dust: JWST mid-IR plus HST optical/near-IR together prove the cavity is genuinely star-free rather than merely obscured. That turns a morphological curiosity into clean dynamical evidence. Second, they have caught what may be the most massive binary black-hole system yet, still at kiloparsec separation and still actively accreting (dual AGN). Third, they tie the observed cavity size directly to the three-body-scouring prediction in the ultramassive regime where simulations are still sparse.

**6. Limitations & Open Questions (brutally honest)**  
The second AGN is still only a “candidate”; deeper X-ray, radio, or variability data could falsify it or confirm orbital motion. Black-hole masses rest on empirical scaling relations calibrated at lower masses; extrapolating to 5 × 10¹⁰ solar masses carries systematic uncertainty. No direct proper-motion or radial-velocity orbit has been measured—the period is probably tens of millions of years—so chance projection or a recoiling single black hole cannot yet be ruled out at high . The exact formation channel for the cavity (three-body scattering versus recoil kick versus dipole instability) remains model-dependent. Follow-up simulations tuned to this mass and environment would help. In the next 12–24 months the community needs deeper JWST spectroscopy, ALMA gas maps, and continued monitoring for variability or proper motion.

**8. Detailed Summary & Explanation**  
McDonald and colleagues present JWST NIRCam imaging and supporting HST plus MUSE data on the central galaxy of the galaxy cluster Abell 402. They show a sharp ~1 kpc stellar cavity containing roughly two billion solar masses of “missing” stars and, on larger scales, a 2.2 kpc flattened core. A bright mid-infrared point source at the cavity edge coincides with a low-ionization AGN; a second emission-line AGN lies on the opposite side with a 370 km s⁻¹ velocity offset. Treating the pair as bound yields a total black-hole mass of about 60 billion solar masses—the highest yet for any binary candidate. The authors argue the cavity is the direct result of three-body gravitational scattering during binary hardening, exactly as predicted by theory but rarely seen so cleanly.

This matters because it gives the cleanest observational example yet of how supermassive black-hole binaries reshape their host galaxies.
