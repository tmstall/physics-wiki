### Question: Is it possible to "hear" the collision of two black holes, and if so, how?

#### Answer:
When we think about black holes—those mysterious regions in space where gravity is so intense that not even light can escape—we might wonder how we can detect events involving them, especially something as dramatic as two black holes colliding. While black holes themselves don’t emit light or sound in the traditional sense, their collisions produce ripples in the fabric of spacetime called **gravitational waves**. These waves are a key to "hearing" such cosmic events, though not with our ears directly.

**Gravitational waves** are distortions in spacetime caused by massive objects accelerating, like two black holes spiraling toward each other and merging. Predicted by Einstein’s theory of general relativity, these waves travel across the universe at the speed of light, carrying energy away from the collision. When the black holes finally merge, a burst of gravitational waves is released, and a new, more massive black hole forms. Interestingly, the final black hole’s mass is slightly less than the sum of the two original masses because some mass-energy is converted into the energy of these waves.

So, how do we detect these invisible ripples, and what does "hearing" have to do with it? Scientists use incredibly sensitive instruments called **interferometers**, such as those in the **Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO)**. LIGO consists of two L-shaped detectors, each with arms 4 kilometers long. A laser beam is split and travels down these arms, bouncing off mirrors and recombining. Normally, the beams cancel each other out perfectly. But when a gravitational wave passes through Earth, it slightly stretches and squeezes spacetime—by less than the width of a proton! This tiny distortion alters the lengths of the arms, allowing the laser beams to reveal the wave’s presence.

Now, about "hearing" the collision: gravitational waves themselves are not sound waves, which need a medium like air to travel. Space is a vacuum, so there’s no sound in the traditional sense. However, scientists can take the signal detected by LIGO and convert its frequency data into an audio waveform. For black hole collisions, this often results in a distinctive "chirp"—a sound that starts low and quickly rises in pitch as the black holes spiral closer and merge. This chirp represents the increasing frequency of the gravitational waves as the orbit tightens, typically lasting just a fraction of a second for stellar-mass black holes (those with masses a few times to tens of times that of our Sun).

For example, the first gravitational wave detection on **September 14, 2015**, came from two black holes—about 36 and 29 solar masses—merging 1.3 billion light-years away. The signal’s frequency rose from about 35 Hz to 250 Hz, within the human hearing range (20 Hz to 20,000 Hz), making it possible to translate into a sound we can play through speakers. Publicly released audio of this event sounds like a brief, rising whoop—a cosmic symphony from the universe!

This "sound" tells us a lot: the pitch and duration reveal the black holes’ masses and how fast they were orbiting, while the wave’s strength indicates their distance. Larger black holes produce lower-frequency waves, sometimes too low to hear without speeding up the data. So, in a way, we can "hear" these collisions by using technology to translate the universe’s vibrations into something our senses can grasp.

In summary, yes, it’s possible to "hear" the collision of two black holes—not as sound traveling through space, but as an audio representation of gravitational waves detected by instruments like LIGO. This remarkable feat connects us to the dynamics of black holes and the fundamental nature of spacetime itself!

### What Are Baryon Acoustic Oscillations?

Baryon acoustic oscillations are relic patterns imprinted in the large-scale distribution of matter—specifically galaxies—in the universe. They originate from the early universe, a hot, dense plasma of photons, electrons, protons, and neutrons, where gravity and radiation pressure competed in a cosmic tug-of-war. Here’s how they came to be:

- **The Early Universe Plasma**: Before about 380,000 years after the Big Bang (at a redshift of \( z \approx 1100 \)), the universe was a plasma where photons and baryons (protons and neutrons) were tightly coupled via Thomson scattering. Dark matter, unaffected by radiation, clumped under gravity, creating gravitational wells.
- **Sound Waves**: Perturbations in the density of this plasma—caused by quantum fluctuations stretched by inflation—triggered acoustic waves. These waves were pressure waves driven by radiation, traveling at the sound speed in the plasma, approximately \( c_s \approx c / \sqrt{3} \) (where \( c \) is the speed of light), or roughly 58% of light speed.
- **Freeze-Out at Recombination**: When the universe cooled enough for protons and electrons to combine into neutral hydrogen (recombination), photons decoupled from baryons, ending the acoustic oscillations. This happened at a specific time, fixing the distance these sound waves had traveled—known as the **sound horizon**.

The sound horizon is the physical distance a sound wave could travel from the Big Bang to recombination, calculated as:

\[ r_s = \int_0^{t_{\text{rec}}} c_s(t) \, dt \]

where \( t_{\text{rec}} \) is the time of recombination, and \( c_s(t) \) depends on the baryon-to-photon ratio and temperature. In the standard \(\Lambda\)CDM cosmology, this distance is approximately 147 Mpc (megaparsecs) in comoving coordinates, or about 490 million light-years today.

After recombination, the baryons, no longer coupled to photons, settled into the density peaks created by these acoustic waves. This left a characteristic imprint: a slight excess in the density of matter at a fixed scale—the sound horizon—surrounded by a region of lower density. Here’s how this translates to galaxies:

- **Gravitational Collapse**: Over billions of years, dark matter halos grew around these density peaks, pulling in baryonic matter to form galaxies. The initial BAO pattern became a statistical overdensity in the galaxy distribution.
- **Subtle Patterns**: In the large-scale structure of the universe, galaxies are not randomly distributed. The BAO signature appears as a preferred separation between galaxy pairs, roughly 150 Mpc in today’s universe (adjusted for cosmic expansion). This is a subtle effect—an enhancement of about 5% in the likelihood of finding galaxy pairs at this distance compared to a random distribution.

To detect this, astronomers map the positions of millions of galaxies in three-dimensional space, creating a **galaxy redshift survey**. The redshift (\( z \)) of each galaxy, determined from its spectral lines, approximates its distance via Hubble’s law (\( v \approx H_0 d \), where \( H_0 \) is the Hubble constant and \( d \) is distance), though refined with cosmological models.

### Mapping Galaxies to Detect BAOs

Detecting BAOs requires observing vast volumes of the universe to capture enough galaxy pairs for statistical significance. Here’s the process:

1. **Galaxy Surveys**:
   - Large surveys like the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS), Dark Energy Survey (DES), and upcoming projects (e.g., Euclid, DESI) collect positions and redshifts of millions of galaxies.
   - Example: SDSS’s Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS) mapped over 1 million galaxies across 10,000 square degrees up to \( z \sim 0.7 \).

2. **Correlation Function**:
   - Astronomers compute the **two-point correlation function**, \(\xi(s)\), which measures the excess probability of finding galaxy pairs separated by a distance \( s \) compared to a random distribution.
   - The BAO signal appears as a distinct peak in \(\xi(s)\) at \( s \approx 150 \, \mathrm{Mpc} \), reflecting the sound horizon scale stretched by cosmic expansion.

3. **Power Spectrum**:
   - Alternatively, the **power spectrum** \( P(k) \)—the Fourier transform of \(\xi(s)\)—shows oscillations in the distribution of galaxy clustering as a function of wavenumber \( k \). The BAO scale corresponds to a specific \( k \)-value, typically around \( k \sim 0.07 \, h \, \mathrm{Mpc}^{-1} \) (where \( h = H_0 / 100 \)).

### BAOs as Cosmic Rulers

The BAO scale acts as a **standard ruler** because its physical size (the sound horizon, \( r_s \)) is fixed by early universe physics and can be precisely calculated from cosmic microwave background (CMB) data (e.g., Planck measurements give \( r_s = 147.09 \pm 0.26 \, \mathrm{Mpc} \)). By measuring its apparent size in galaxy surveys at different redshifts, we probe the universe’s expansion history. Here’s how:

- **Angular Diameter Distance (\( D_A \))**:
  - In the sky’s transverse direction, the BAO scale appears as an angular separation, \(\theta_{\text{BAO}} = r_s / D_A(z)\).
  - Measuring \(\theta_{\text{BAO}}\) at a redshift \( z \) yields \( D_A(z) \), which depends on the Hubble parameter \( H(z) \) and the universe’s geometry.

- **Line-of-Sight Distance**:
  - Along the radial direction (via redshift), the BAO scale corresponds to a redshift interval, \(\Delta z_{\text{BAO}} = r_s H(z) / c\).
  - This measures \( H(z) \) directly, reflecting the expansion rate at that redshift.

- **Cosmic Expansion History**:
  - Combining \( D_A(z) \) and \( H(z) \) across redshifts maps the expansion rate over time, parameterized as \( H(z) = H_0 \sqrt{\Omega_m (1+z)^3 + \Omega_\Lambda} \) in a flat \(\Lambda\)CDM model (\(\Omega_m\) = matter density, \(\Omega_\Lambda\) = dark energy density).

### Measuring the Universe’s Expansion History

BAOs provide a direct probe of cosmic expansion, independent of supernovae or CMB assumptions beyond \( r_s \). Key applications include:

- **Dark Energy**: At low redshifts (\( z < 1 \)), BAO measurements (e.g., from BOSS) show accelerated expansion consistent with a cosmological constant (\(\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.7\)). Deviations could indicate evolving dark energy.
- **Hubble Constant**: Combining BAO with CMB data constrains \( H_0 \), helping resolve tensions between local (\( H_0 \sim 73 \, \mathrm{km} \, \mathrm{s}^{-1} \, \mathrm{Mpc}^{-1} \)) and early-universe (\( H_0 \sim 67 \, \mathrm{km} \, \mathrm{s}^{-1} \, \mathrm{Mpc}^{-1} \)) estimates.
- **Geometry**: The BAO scale’s consistency across redshifts tests flatness (\(\Omega_m + \Omega_\Lambda = 1\)) versus curvature.

For example, SDSS data at \( z \sim 0.35 \) measured \( D_V(z) = (1 + z) D_A(z) \sqrt[3]{c / (z H(z))} \approx 1370 \pm 64 \, \mathrm{Mpc} \), refining cosmological parameters when paired with CMB priors.

### Challenges and Advances

- **Precision**: The BAO signal is weak, requiring millions of galaxies to reduce statistical noise. Nonlinear effects (e.g., galaxy clustering evolution) can blur the peak, addressed by reconstruction techniques.
- **Redshift Range**: Current surveys cover \( z < 2 \), but future missions like DESI aim for \( z \sim 3 \), probing earlier expansion phases.
- **Cosmic Variance**: Limited survey volumes introduce uncertainty, mitigated by larger sky coverage.

Mapping galaxies reveals BAOs as subtle, statistically significant patterns in their large-scale distribution, a legacy of early universe sound waves. As cosmic rulers, they measure \( D_A(z) \) and \( H(z) \), tracing the expansion history and constraining cosmological models. This powerful tool continues to refine our understanding of the universe’s past, present, and future.

#### Dark Matter and ULDM

### Background Information

#### What is the Central Molecular Zone (CMZ)?
The CMZ is a region about 400 light-years wide near the Milky Way’s center, packed with dense gas (around 150 hydrogen atoms per cubic centimeter—much denser than typical space). It’s a hotbed for studying extreme conditions in our galaxy. #### What is Sub-GeV Dark Matter?
Dark matter is a mysterious substance that affects gravity but doesn’t emit or absorb light, making it invisible. “Sub-GeV” means its particles have masses below 1 billion electron volts (GeV), or roughly the mass of a proton. For comparison, 1 MeV is a million electron volts, so sub-GeV includes very light particles (1 MeV to 1 GeV). These particles might annihilate into pairs of electrons and positrons, releasing energy that could ionize gas. Because they’re light, they’re hard to detect with usual experiments, but their effects might show up in places like the CMZ.

#### What is the 511 keV Line Emission?
This is a gamma-ray signal at 511 keV (a specific energy) seen coming from the Galactic Center. It’s produced when positrons slow down, pair up with electrons to form positronium, and then annihilate, releasing two gamma rays at 511 keV each. (The energy comes from the electron’s mass: \(E = mc^2\), where 511 keV is the rest energy of an electron.) Scientists don’t know what’s making these positrons—possible culprits include exploding stars, pulsars, or dark matter. This paper explores if sub-GeV dark matter could be the source.

#### Why Does Ionization Matter?
Ionization happens when energy knocks an electron off an atom or molecule, like \(\mathrm{H}_2\) becoming \(\mathrm{H}_2^+\). In the CMZ, the high ionization rate suggests a lot of low-energy particles (electrons or positrons) are present. Cosmic rays usually cause ionization, but they’d need unrealistically high energy input to explain the CMZ, so researchers are looking for other sources, like dark matter.

### Technical Details

#### How They Calculated Ionization
The ionization rate (\(\zeta\)) measures how often hydrogen molecules get ionized per second. The authors used this formula:

\[
\zeta = 2 \times 4\pi \int_{E_{\min}}^{E_{\max}} J(E, \mathbf{x}) \sigma(E) [1 + \theta_e(E)] dE
\]

- **\(J(E, \mathbf{x})\)**: The number of electrons or positrons at a given energy and position, like a traffic count of particles.
- **\(\sigma(E)\)**: The likelihood that an electron or positron ionizes \(\mathrm{H}_2\), based on its energy (think of it as a “hit probability”).
- **\(\theta_e(E)\)**: Extra ionizations caused by secondary electrons kicked out in the process.
- **Factor of 2**: Accounts for both electrons and positrons contributing.

They estimated \(J(E, \mathbf{x})\) two ways:
1. **Analytical**: A quick math trick assuming particles lose energy right where they’re made.
2. **Numerical (DRAGON2)**: A simulation tracking how particles spread out, like modeling traffic flow in a city.

For dark matter, the source of these particles is:

\[
Q(E, \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\langle \sigma v \rangle}{2 m_{\chi}^2} \left( \frac{\rho(\mathbf{x})}{m_{\chi}} \right)^2 \delta(E - m_{\chi})
\]

- **\(\langle \sigma v \rangle\)**: The annihilation rate—how often dark matter particles collide and turn into electrons/positrons.
- **\(m_{\chi}\)**: Dark matter mass (e.g., 10 MeV).
- **\(\rho(\mathbf{x})\)**: Dark matter density, which varies with position.

#### Dark Matter Density Profiles
Dark matter isn’t spread evenly—it’s denser near the galaxy’s center. The authors tested different shapes:

\[
\rho(r) = \rho_0 \left( \frac{r}{r_s} \right)^{-\gamma} \left( 1 + \frac{r}{r_s} \right)^{-3 + \gamma}
\]

- **\(\gamma = 1\)**: Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW), a standard model.
- **\(\gamma = 1.25\)**: Contracted NFW (cNFW), a bit steeper, matching other galactic signals.
- **\(\gamma = 1.5\)**: Moore, very steep, with more dark matter at the center.

Steeper profiles (\(\gamma > 1\)) mean more annihilation near the CMZ, boosting ionization.

#### Checking Other Signals
If dark matter makes electrons and positrons, those particles could create:
- **X-rays or Bremsstrahlung**: When they slow down in gas.
- **Inverse Compton**: When they scatter light.
- **Synchrotron**: When they spiral in magnetic fields.

The authors used a tool called HERMES to calculate these and found they’re too weak to detect with current telescopes, which is good—it means their idea doesn’t contradict observations.

#### The 511 keV Connection
They calculated how many positrons dark matter produces and whether they’d make the 511 keV signal. For an annihilation rate of \(\langle \sigma v \rangle = 10^{-35} \, \mathrm{cm}^3 \mathrm{s}^{-1}\), the signal is weaker than observed, especially for \(\gamma = 1.25\), but closer for \(\gamma = 1.5\). Uncertainties—like how positrons form positronium—make it hard to say for sure if the same dark matter causes both effects.

#### Ionization Shape
The ionization rate across the CMZ is “flat”—it doesn’t vary much with distance from the center. Even with a steep Moore profile, diffusion (particles spreading out) smooths it out, matching what astronomers see.

- **How It Works**: Dark matter turns into electrons and positrons, which zap hydrogen molecules, stripping off electrons.
- **Why It’s Cool**: It might also explain a mysterious gamma-ray signal (511 keV), though that’s not certain yet.

### Stage 1: Data Calibration and Ramp Fitting
Stage 1 is all about cleaning and calibrating the raw data from JWST’s detectors. It uses the JWST Science Calibration Pipeline to perform a series of corrections, ensuring the data accurately reflects the light received. Here’s what happens:

- **Data Quality Initialization**:  
  The pipeline starts by inspecting the raw data for issues like bad pixels or unusable data points. These are flagged so they can be dealt with later, ensuring only reliable data moves forward.

- **Electromagnetic Interference (EMI) Correction**:  
  Space telescopes can pick up stray signals, like radio waves from Earth. This step removes that interference, sharpening the data’s focus on the astronomical signal.

- **Saturation Flagging**:  
  If the incoming light is too intense, it overwhelms the detector, creating a “saturated” signal that doesn’t reflect true brightness. Saturated pixels are flagged to be excluded or adjusted.

- **First and Last Frame Flagging**:  
  JWST captures data in sequences called integrations. The first and last frames of each sequence can be unreliable due to instrumental effects, so they’re flagged to avoid skewing the results.

- **Linearity Correction**:  
  Detectors don’t always respond proportionally to light intensity. Interestingly, for K2-18 b, the choice between standard and custom corrections has little impact on the final transmission spectrum, as noted in the research and shown in Figure 7.

- **Reset Switch Charge Decay (RSCD) Correction**:  
  After resetting the detector for a new observation, residual charge can linger and affect early readings. This step removes that artifact, ensuring a clean start.

- **Dark-Current Subtraction**:  
  Even without light, detectors produce a small signal called dark current due to thermal noise. This is subtracted to isolate the true light signal.

- **Ramp Fitting**:  
  As light hits the detector, charge builds up over time, forming a “ramp.” Ramp fitting measures this buildup to calculate the precise rate of incoming light, a key step for accurate brightness measurements.

By the end of Stage 1, the raw data is transformed into a calibrated form, free from major instrumental distortions and noise.

### Stage 2: Flat-Field Correction
Stage 2 takes the calibrated data from Stage 1 and applies a **flat-field correction**. Detectors often have uneven sensitivity across their surface—some areas might detect light more efficiently than others. The flat-field correction uses a sensitivity map to adjust the data, ensuring consistent brightness measurements across the entire image.

Stages 1 and 2 are the groundwork for Stage 3, where the real science happens. Here’s how they configure JWST for those observations:

- **Removing Noise and Artifacts**:

- **Correcting Instrument Effects**:

- **Ensuring Data Consistency**:  
  The flat-field correction in Stage 2 standardizes the detector’s response, so brightness variations reflect the target object, not the instrument.

By the end of Stage 2, the data is clean, calibrated, and uniform—ready for Stage 3, where researchers extract light curves (brightness changes over time) and spectra (light distribution across wavelengths) to study the exoplanet’s atmosphere.

### **Step 1: Converting Flux Units**
The process begins by adjusting the raw brightness measurements, or **flux**, to a standardized form. The pipeline uses a **gain reference file** to convert the flux from **DN s⁻¹** (data numbers per second, a detector-specific unit) to **e⁻ s⁻¹** (electrons per second), a more universal unit. This conversion ensures the data is consistent and comparable for further analysis.

---

### **Step 2: Identifying and Removing Cosmic-Ray Hits**
Next, the pipeline cleans the data by detecting **cosmic-ray hits**—sudden, sharp increases in brightness caused by high-energy particles striking the detector. For each pixel, the team:
- Analyzes the **time series** (brightness over time).
- Subtracts a **running median** calculated over seven integrations (data captures) to smooth out normal variations.
- Flags any data points exceeding **7 standard deviations (7σ)** from this median as cosmic-ray hits.
- Masks these outliers, along with their **neighboring pixels** and any pixels already flagged as problematic in Stages 1 or 2.

This step eliminates random spikes that could skew the results, ensuring the data reflects the true signal from the exoplanet and its star.

---

### **Step 3: Background Correction**
To isolate the light from the star and planet, the pipeline subtracts the **background**—the ambient glow of space unrelated to the target. For each detector column and each integration:
- The **mean flux** is calculated from columns outside the main signal, specifically pixels **11–30** and **44–63**, excluding bad pixels or outliers.
- This mean background value is subtracted from the entire column.

This correction leaves only the light associated with the star and the exoplanet, enhancing the clarity of the signal.

---

### **Step 4: Extracting the Spectrum**
Now, the pipeline extracts the **spectrum**—a detailed map of light intensity across different wavelengths—using a technique called **optimal extraction** (developed by K. Horne in 1986). Here’s how it works:

This step transforms the cleaned data into a spectrum ready for detailed analysis.
