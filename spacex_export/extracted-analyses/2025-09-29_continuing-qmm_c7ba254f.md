As we covered, long-term entanglements aren't fleeting like in lab experiments (e.g., entangled photons that decohere quickly). In the model's Planck-scale lattice, they're stabilized through those "tunnel" links in the matrices—protected by quantum error correction codes and topological invariants (like Chern numbers) that make them resistant to decay. These links form when early universe excitations (e.g., during inflation) imprint correlations between separated Plankton cells. Over cosmic time, as spacetime expands, these cells drift apart, but the entanglement persists as a non-local correlation in their joint density matrices ρ_{ij}, where i and j are distant sites. The von Neumann entropy S = -Tr(ρ log ρ) of these pairs doesn't drop to zero; instead, it hovers at a finite value, encoding a kind of "memory" of the initial interaction.

The paper argues this isn't just abstract math— it has gravitational consequences because, per the ER=EPR conjecture (which they cite heavily), entanglement is dual to spacetime geometry. Distant entangled pairs act like microscopic wormholes, threading the lattice and influencing macro-scale curvature.

### 2. **How This Mimics Dark Energy**
Dark energy, in standard cosmology, is the mysterious component (about 70% of the universe's energy density) driving accelerated expansion—it's like a repulsive gravity that pushes galaxies apart faster over time. The paper flips this: instead of a separate field (like quintessence), dark energy emerges from the *average effect* of these long-term entanglements across the vacuum.

- **Vacuum Fluctuations as Entangled Pairs**: At the Plankton level, the vacuum isn't empty; it's buzzing with virtual particle-antiparticle pairs (excitations in the fields, as we talked about). These pairs are inherently entangled—born together, their states linked (e.g., spin up/down). Normally, they annihilate quickly, but in the lattice model, a fraction leave behind long-term imprints if separated by rapid expansion (like during cosmic inflation). This creates a network of entangled "vacuum threads" between distant cells.
  
- **Repulsive Force from Entanglement Tension**: Here's the key mechanism—the paper models this as an effective energy density. For two entangled cells separated by distance r, the entanglement contributes a negative pressure term in the stress-energy tensor T_{μν} (from general relativity). Mathematically, they derive it via holographic duality: the entanglement entropy S across a cosmic horizon (like the future event horizon in a de Sitter space) translates to an energy E ~ S * (kT)/2, where T is a Hawking-like temperature. But since these are long-term, the entropy doesn't dissipate; it accumulates, creating a uniform, positive energy density ρ_DE ~ (ħ c / L_P^4) * <S>, where L_P is the Planck length, and <S> is the average entanglement entropy per volume.

  This ρ_DE is constant-ish (scale-invariant in the model), matching observations of dark energy's w ≈ -1 equation of state (like a cosmological constant). The "repulsion" comes because entangled pairs resist further separation—think of it as quantum "rubber bands" stretching spacetime, but instead of snapping back, they inflate it outward to minimize free energy. In chaos terms, high Lyapunov exponents at early scales amplify tiny entanglements into global expansion drivers.

- **Disentanglement's Role**: Interestingly, the paper also touches on *disentanglement* as a counterbalance. If entanglements fully resolve (e.g., via measurement or decoherence), it releases energy, but in the vacuum, partial disentanglements (where correlations weaken but don't vanish) contribute to the dark energy budget. They calculate the dark energy density as roughly (1 - P_ent)/2, where P_ent is the probability of full entanglement (drawing from Hardy's quantum entanglement probability ~0.09), yielding ~0.7—spot on with cosmic measurements. This isn't just hand-wavy; it's derived from the matrix trace norms relaxing over time.
