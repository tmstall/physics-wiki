Here is a deeper, no-fluff expansion of exactly that sentence, written for someone who already understands phase transitions, symmetry breaking, and quantum many-body states from QFT and condensed-matter reading but hasn’t lived inside supersolid literature for the last decade.

### What “spontaneously break translational symmetry” actually means
Translational symmetry says the laws of physics are identical if you shift every coordinate by the same vector. A uniform liquid or gas respects this: its average density looks the same no matter where you stand.  

When a system spontaneously breaks translational symmetry, the ground state (or steady state) picks a preferred spacing and a preferred origin even though nothing in the Hamiltonian favored one location over another. The density becomes periodic:

n(r) = n₀ + δn cos(q·r + ϕ)

where q is a wavevector set by interactions and ϕ is a phase that can be chosen anywhere (Goldstone mode). This is exactly what happens in an ordinary crystal: atoms lock into a lattice. In a supersolid the “atoms” are the quasiparticles themselves (polaritons in the paper you asked about), and the lattice they form is visible as a density modulation that appears only above a critical interaction strength or pump power.

In the photonic-crystal experiment the team directly imaged this modulation in both real space and momentum space. Side peaks appeared at ±q in the far-field emission; their spacing gave the lattice constant and their contrast gave the modulation depth (a few parts per thousand). That is the smoking-gun signature of translational symmetry breaking.

### What “preserving off-diagonal long-range order” actually means
Off-diagonal long-range order (ODLRO) is the Penrose-Onsager criterion for superfluidity or Bose-Einstein condensation. It lives in the one-body density matrix:

ρ₁(r, r′) = ⟨ψ†(r) ψ(r′)⟩

For a normal fluid or solid this decays to zero as |r − r′| → ∞. For a superfluid a finite fraction of particles occupies the same single-particle state, so ρ₁(r, r′) → n₀ (a macroscopic constant) even when r and r′ are arbitrarily far apart. The phase of that macroscopic wavefunction is coherent across the whole system; that coherence is what lets you have frictionless flow and quantized vortices.

In ordinary language: the particles are delocalized and phase-locked over macroscopic distances even while their density is periodically modulated.

### Why both orders together are surprising and non-trivial
Crystallization usually kills superfluidity. Once particles localize on lattice sites to form a crystal, their wavefunctions stop overlapping enough to maintain long-range phase coherence. Solid ⁴He was the classic candidate; after decades of work it is still not convincingly supersolid in bulk.  

A supersolid therefore requires a delicate compromise: enough particle-particle repulsion or attraction to force a density wave, yet enough delocalization and coherence for the phase to stay locked across many lattice spacings. In atomic gases this was finally achieved around 2017–2019 with dipolar atoms or spin-orbit-coupled condensates that form droplet crystals or stripe phases while retaining superfluid response.

In the polariton case the compromise is engineered differently. Polaritons are composite bosons with strong repulsive interactions inherited from their exciton component. When the condensate density rises, repulsion spontaneously modulates the density. Because the underlying particles are still delocalized polaritons (half-light), the global phase coherence survives. The bound-state-in-the-continuum trick simply gives that coherence enough lifetime to be measured before photons leak out.

### How the two orders are measured in the same experiment
- Translational symmetry breaking → periodic density modulation visible as real-space fringes or momentum-space side peaks.  
- Off-diagonal long-range order → direct interferometry or homodyne detection that extracts the phase of the polariton wavefunction across different spatial regions of the modulated condensate. The phase stays locked even while density goes up and down.

Both signatures appear together only above a threshold pump power and only when the photonic-crystal loss is low enough. That coexistence is what lets the authors claim a supersolid rather than “just another density wave” or “just another condensate.”

Exciton-polaritons are the quasiparticles that make the supersolid experiment possible. Here is the precise, no-fluff picture of what they are and why they behave the way they do.

### How they are born
Physicists place a thin semiconductor quantum well (typically GaAs or similar III-V material with embedded wells a few nanometers thick) inside an optical structure — in the paper, a photonic-crystal waveguide engineered with a bound state in the continuum. Photons bounce around in this structure while the quantum well hosts excitons: bound electron-hole pairs created when an electron is promoted from the valence to the conduction band.

When the photon energy and momentum nearly match the exciton energy, the two couple strongly. The coupling strength (Rabi energy, usually a few meV) exceeds the decay rates of both the photon and the exciton. The original “bare” photon and “bare” exciton states cease to be eigenstates. New hybrid eigenstates appear instead: the upper and lower polariton branches. These are the exciton-polaritons.

Each polariton is a coherent quantum superposition:

|polariton⟩ = α |photon⟩ + β |exciton⟩

where |α|² is the photon fraction and |β|² is the exciton fraction (Hopfield coefficients). The fractions add to 1 and can be tuned by changing momentum, detuning, or cavity design. At small in-plane momentum the lower polariton branch is mostly photon-like; at larger momentum it acquires more exciton character.

### What each parent contributes
The photon parent gives the polariton two decisive advantages:
- Extremely small effective mass — typically 10^{-4} to 10^{-5} times the free-electron mass. This comes from the light dispersion relation E = ħc k / n (modified by the cavity).
- Fast dynamics and easy optical access. Polaritons propagate ballistically over tens to hundreds of microns, can be created and read out with ordinary lasers, and carry phase information directly in the emitted light.

The exciton parent supplies the interactions that ordinary photons lack:
- Strong, tunable repulsion or attraction arising from Coulomb forces between the underlying electrons and holes.
- Spin and valley degrees of freedom inherited from the semiconductor band structure.
- The ability to be trapped or engineered by material design (quantum wells, photonic crystals, strain, etc.).

Because the polariton is a coherent mixture, it inherits a weighted average of these properties. The effective mass stays photon-like (light and fast), while the interaction energy scales with the exciton fraction |β|². This combination is what lets polaritons form condensates that are both fast and strongly interacting — the exact ingredients needed for a supersolid.

### Why they act like bosons and can condense
Both photons and excitons are bosons (photons have integer spin; excitons are composite bosons made of two fermions). Their hybrid, the polariton, is therefore also a boson. When the density is high enough and losses are low enough, bosonic stimulation drives macroscopic occupation of the lowest-energy polariton state — Bose-Einstein condensation. In the photonic-crystal experiment the bound state in the continuum keeps radiative losses extremely low, so the condensate lives long enough for interactions to organize it into a density-modulated supersolid while the global phase remains coherent.

### Engineering-style analogy that fits your background
Think of a polariton as a hybrid data packet traveling on an optical interconnect inside a III-V chip:
- The “address / momentum” part travels as a photon — light mass, near-light speed, low latency, easy to launch and detect with lasers.
- The “payload / interaction” part is an exciton — real Coulomb forces that let it collide with neighboring packets, repel them, and self-organize.

In normal optical interconnects the packets barely interact. In normal electronic logic the carriers are heavy and slow. A polariton packet combines both: it moves fast like light yet organizes spatially like interacting electrons. When you pump enough of them, they spontaneously form a periodic density pattern (the crystal lattice of the supersolid) while their optical phase stays locked across the entire waveguide (the superfluid coherence). That is exactly the dual order the Italian team measured.

Another performance analogy: imagine a cache-coherent fabric where every cache line carries both a photonic “tag” (momentum and phase) and an excitonic “data payload.” The photonic tag lets lines propagate and interfere coherently at light speed; the excitonic payload makes neighboring lines repel and lock into a regular spatial lattice. The whole fabric still maintains perfect global coherence — zero snoop traffic, zero latency — even though the lines are now periodically arranged. That is a supersolid polariton condensate in processor-speak.

In the Nature paper the polaritons are created in a photonic-crystal waveguide supporting a bound state in the continuum. The low loss gives the condensate lifetime. The exciton-derived interactions drive the spontaneous density modulation (translational symmetry breaking). The photon-derived lightness and coherence preserve the off-diagonal long-range order across that modulation. Remove either parent and you lose one of the two required orders.
