**Color Space Has Potholes: Why Your Brain's Distance Meter Isn't a Ruler** Schrödinger sketched a beautiful geometry of color perception a century ago but left three fatal holes in the foundation — this Los Alamos team patches all three by replacing his flat-ish Riemannian ruler with a genuinely warped, non-Riemannian one, and proves experimentally the fix works. --- ## 2. Big-Picture Context Color perception is fundamentally a measurement problem: given two colors, how *different* do they look to a human observer? Since the 19th century, scientists have treated that question geometrically — imagine all possible colors living in a 3D space, and the perceived difference between two colors is the "distance" between their positions. Riemann (1854) gave mathematicians the framework for curved distance metrics, Helmholtz applied it to color in the 1890s, and Schrödinger formalized hue, saturation, and lightness from that metric in the 1920s. This lineage is so well-established it's baked into the international color standard ΔE used everywhere from display calibration to paint matching. The problem is that Schrödinger's Riemannian framework assumes distances add up cleanly — ten small color steps equal one large color step. But human perception doesn't work that way. Large color differences look *smaller* than the accumulated sum of small steps, a well-documented phenomenon called **diminishing returns**. Bujack's group proved in a landmark 2022 PNAS paper that perceptual color space is fundamentally non-Riemannian for exactly this reason — the triangle inequality is violated. Think of it as the color equivalent of discovering that spacetime isn't flat after decades of treating it as Euclidean. This 2025 CGF paper is the follow-up: now that we know the geometry is non-Riemannian, how do we rebuild Schrödinger's beautiful definitions of hue, saturation, and lightness in that warped space?
