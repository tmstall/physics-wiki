For decades, getting light to exhibit chirality — the property where a field behaves differently to its mirror image — or to develop spin angular momentum in a controllable way has required intervention: either smash the beam into a tight focus, push it through a specially engineered material (metasurface, liquid crystal q-plate, plasmonic interface), or exploit symmetry-breaking at a dielectric boundary. These material-dependent approaches work, but they're fragile. They depend on precise fabrication, are wavelength-specific, and generally lock the chirality in at manufacturing time. The Wits/UEA team (led by the wonderfully named Light Mkhumbuza) asks a cleaner question: what if the topology encoded in the beam's own phase structure could do the work instead? Not topology in a hand-wavy sense — in the precise mathematical sense of a winding number associated with Pancharatnam's geometric phase, a quantity that counts how many times the beam's polarization state wraps around the Poincaré sphere as you sweep azimuthally around the beam. Their answer is startling: a single integer, the Pancharatnam topological (PT) charge ℓp, acts as a master dial for spin-orbit interaction in free space, in the fully paraxial regime (low-NA, no tight focusing). Dial ℓp up or down, and you deterministically control where right-handed and left-handed circularly polarized regions appear in the beam cross-section — concentrically separated rings of opposite chirality emerging from a beam that started with zero chirality everywhere. They call this the **free-space optical Hall effect**, and it arises purely from propagation physics. This is a conceptual shift: topology displaces material engineering as the handle on chirality.
