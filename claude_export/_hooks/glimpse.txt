**The Cocoon Confirmed: JWST's 80-Hour Look Inside a Little Red Dot Proves Super-Eddington Black Holes Are Hiding in Plain Sight** A single ultra-deep spectrum forces five independent lines of evidence to converge on one answer: GLIMPSE-17775 is a modestly-massed black hole eating faster than physics supposedly allows, swaddled in a fog of gas so dense it scatters light like milk glass — and that fog had been tricking astronomers into thinking the black hole was ten times heavier than it actually is. --- ## 2. Big-Picture Context Since JWST launched, one of its most disorienting discoveries has been an overabundance of faint, compact, red objects — dubbed "Little Red Dots" (LRDs) — lurking in the early universe around redshift three to eight (roughly two to three billion years after the Big Bang). They're tiny, stupidly bright in the near-infrared, and show a distinctive "V-shaped" spectral energy distribution: blue at rest-UV, then a sharp upturn into red at rest-optical. They were everywhere, and nobody could cleanly explain them. The first hypothesis was that LRDs are incredibly massive, fully-formed galaxies that somehow assembled an absurd amount of stellar mass while the universe was still a toddler — a scenario that strained or broke standard cosmological predictions. The second hypothesis was extreme dust. Both failed: massive early galaxies should leave signatures in the cosmic microwave background that aren't there, and dusty objects re-radiate heat in the far-infrared, which ALMA would see — and doesn't. Something else was going on. The leading idea crystallizing in 2024–2025 is the "Black Hole Star" (BH*) scenario: each LRD hosts an active galactic nucleus — a rapidly accreting supermassive black hole — but instead of sitting in an ordinary broad-line region with clear sightlines, it's embedded in an exceptionally dense, partially-ionized gas cocoon.
