**The nucleus tells on itself: reading the upbend's magnetism off its own parity bookkeeping** A twenty-year-old question — is the mysterious low-energy rise in nuclear γ-emission electric or magnetic? — gets answered not with a better detector but with a trick: feed the *same* nucleus two different ways and watch which strength survives a selection rule the nucleus enforces on itself. --- ## 2 — Big-Picture Context **Paper Type & Stakes.** This is an experimental nuclear-structure paper (an FRIB/Michigan State–led collaboration) that determines the *electromagnetic multipole character* — electric-dipole (E1) versus magnetic-dipole (M1) — of the low-energy enhancement (LEE) in the γ-ray strength function of $^{70}$Zn. The stakes are twofold: a long-open question in nuclear structure, and a direct input to astrophysical neutron-capture rates that drive heavy-element formation (the r- and i-processes). Every nucleus, held together above its ground state, sheds energy as γ-rays. When the excitation is low, the emitted photons are sharp, discrete lines — you can name each transition. When the excitation is high, the number of quantum levels per unit energy (the *nuclear level density*, NLD) explodes, and the photons blur into a continuous average response as a function of photon energy: the γ-ray strength function (γSF). Think of it as the difference between reading individual cache accesses in a trace versus characterizing an aggregate performance profile once the access pattern is too dense to enumerate. For decades the γSF was understood mainly through its big features — the giant dipole resonance (a huge E1 bump at 12–20 MeV, protons sloshing against neutrons), the pygmy resonance (5–10 MeV, a neutron skin oscillating), the scissors mode (1–4 MeV in deformed nuclei, an orbital M1 mode).
