**A Synchronous Motor Inside a Superfluid: Spinning Single Molecules at Dial-a-Frequency Inside Helium Nanodroplets** For the first time, an optical centrifuge has successfully grabbed and spun molecules embedded in superfluid helium — turning a single molecule into a tunable-frequency stirring rod for probing superfluidity at the atomic scale. ## 2. Big-Picture Context Superfluid helium is the textbook macroscopic quantum fluid, but its microscopic behavior — how it responds to a single moving defect at the atomic scale — is still not fully understood. Helium nanodroplets (here, ~3000 atoms of $^{4}$He at 0.4 K) offer a beautiful laboratory: drop a single molecule inside, and the molecule becomes a nanoprobe of the surrounding quantum liquid. Decades of infrared spectroscopy established that embedded molecules often rotate surprisingly freely, but with renormalized rotational constants — the helium partially "dresses" the rotor, increasing its effective moment of inertia. Crucially, how strongly a molecule couples to the bath depends on whether its rotational energy overlaps the superfluid's collective excitation spectrum, especially the roton region around 180 GHz where the density of states piles up. The problem: IR spectroscopy only sees the lowest rotational states, because at 0.4 K almost everything sits in the ground state. To map the frequency dependence of molecule–superfluid coupling, researchers had to swap in different molecules with different rotational constants — which changes everything else about the probe at the same time. Time-resolved wave-packet methods (femtosecond kick pulses) added dynamics, but suffer a fundamental confound: they can't separate rotational *decoherence* from rotational *energy relaxation*, because both kill the coherent signal.
