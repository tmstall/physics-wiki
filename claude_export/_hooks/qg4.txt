Start with the geometry. You have a positive cosmological constant Λ — a uniform energy density of the vacuum that acts like a repulsive force permeating all space. Einstein's equations with Λ > 0 have a maximally symmetric solution that isn't flat Minkowski space and isn't the hyperbolic AdS space — it's de Sitter space, named after the Dutch astronomer Willem de Sitter. The best way to picture it: imagine a 4D hyperboloid embedded in a 5D flat space. Slice it different ways and you get different coordinate patches — the same geometry described by different observers. The one most useful for physics is the **static patch**: a single observer sits at the origin, surrounded by a spherical cosmological horizon at a fixed coordinate radius. Inside that sphere, the metric looks static — nothing is obviously expanding or contracting. Outside it, the expansion is so rapid that no signal ever makes it back in. The key length scale is the **de Sitter radius** $L_{dS} = \sqrt{3/\Lambda}$. When Λ is small (our universe), $L_{dS}$ is enormous — roughly the Hubble radius, about 14 billion light years. The cosmological horizon sits at this distance from any observer. The crucial difference from AdS: in AdS, the curvature is *negative* (hyperbolic, like a saddle), the space is infinite in all directions but has a conformal boundary at infinity that you can reach causally. In de Sitter, the curvature is *positive* (the space curves back on itself), and the expansion pushes regions apart faster than light can cross them. There's no shared boundary.
