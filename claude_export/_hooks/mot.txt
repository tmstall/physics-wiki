**Catching Hydrogen's Precursor: The First Laser Trap of a Metal Hydride Molecule** Calcium monohydride — a molecule that self-destructs when you shine light on it — has been laser-cooled and trapped for the first time, opening a backdoor route to ultracold hydrogen that direct laser cooling of hydrogen itself cannot provide. --- ## 2. Big-Picture Context **Paper Type & Stakes:** This is an experimental milestone Letter — a single clean demonstration result from the Zelevinsky group at Columbia, establishing CaH as the first metal hydride molecule to be magneto-optically trapped. The stakes are two-layered: immediate (a new molecular species joins the small club of laser-cooled diatomics), and downstream (a credible path toward optically trapped ultracold hydrogen for precision fundamental physics). Hydrogen is the simplest atom in the universe and for that reason one of the most powerful testbeds for QED. The 1S–2S transition has been measured to kilohertz precision, and a whole family of transitions — Lamb shift, 2S–4P, 1S–3S — have been pushed to extraordinary accuracy to pin down the proton charge radius. The limiting factor for further improvement is Doppler broadening: the hydrogen atoms in these experiments are in a beam, so they're moving, and moving atoms see a smeared-out laser frequency. The obvious fix is to trap and cool the hydrogen atoms. The problem is that hydrogen is essentially impossible to laser-cool directly — it lacks the closed optical cycling transitions that make atoms like rubidium or cesium amenable to laser cooling. The workaround the field has converged on: cool a molecule that *contains* hydrogen, trap it, and then photodissociate it near threshold so the hydrogen atom inherits the molecule's low temperature.
