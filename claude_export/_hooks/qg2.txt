Let's go. --- # Why Gravity Won't Quantize: The Real Story ## The non-renormalizability problem — what it actually means, not just the slogan Here's the slogan you've probably heard: "Quantum gravity is non-renormalizable." Here's what that actually means, built from the ground up. ### What renormalization is doing Start with QED, which you know well. When you compute, say, the electron's magnetic moment, you integrate over all momenta of virtual photons that can appear in loops. That integral runs from zero to infinity. The problem: at high momenta, it diverges — you get infinity as an answer for a measurable quantity. Renormalization is the procedure that tames these infinities. The key move: you absorb each infinity into a redefinition of a parameter that already appears in the theory — the electron's mass and charge. You don't add new parameters. You say: "The bare mass $m_0$ I wrote in the Lagrangian isn't the physical mass $m_{phys}$ you'd measure in the lab. They're related by $m_{phys} = m_0 + \delta m$, where $\delta m$ absorbs the divergence." After this shuffling, every prediction comes out finite. You've traded unmeasurable bare parameters for measurable physical ones. The crucial word is **finite number**. QED has exactly two parameters to renormalize: mass and charge. You fix them by two measurements, and then every other prediction — the Lamb shift, $g-2$, Compton scattering at any energy — comes out finite and testable. Two inputs, infinite outputs. That's a renormalizable theory. **Analogy:** Think of it like calibrating a measuring instrument. You have a thermometer with two adjustable knobs — offset and scale. You calibrate it at two known temperatures (0°C and 100°C), and then it reliably reads every other temperature correctly. Two calibration points, infinite reliable readings.
