Abstract
We present the discovery of `A Big Ring on the Sky' (BR), the second ultra-large large-scale structure (uLSS) found in Mg II-absorber catalogues, following the previously reported Giant Arc (GA). In cosmological terms the BR is close to the GA — at the same redshift z ∼ 0.8 and with a separation on the sky of only ∼ 12°. Two extraordinary uLSSs in such close configuration raises the possibility that together they form an even more extraordinary cosmological system. The BR is a striking circular, annulus-like, structure of diameter ∼ 400 Mpc (proper size, present epoch). The method of discovery is as described in the GA paper, but here using the new Mg II-absorber catalogues restricted to DR16Q quasars. Using the Convex Hull of Member Spheres (CHMS) algorithm, we estimate that the annulus and inner absorbers of the BR have departures from random expectations, at the density of the control field, of up to 5.2σ. We present the discovery of the BR, assess its significance using the CHMS, Minimal Spanning Tree (MST), FilFinder and Cuzick & Edwards (CE) methods, discuss it in the context of the GA+BR system, and suggest some implications for the origins of uLSS and for our understanding of cosmology. For example, it may be that unusual geometric patterns, such as these uLSSs, have an origin in cosmic strings.

## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**A 400-Megaparsec Smoke Ring in the Quasar Fog**

Using magnesium absorption shadows cast in the light of thousands of background quasars, the same team that found the Giant Arc reports a second colossal structure — a ring wider than the supposed cosmic homogeneity limit — sitting right next door to the first, and if both are real, the Cosmological Principle has a growing neighborhood problem.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is an observational discovery-and-significance paper — a serendipitous structure detection followed by a battery of post-hoc statistical tests — and what's at stake is whether the largest patterns in the galaxy distribution exceed what standard ΛCDM structure formation can plausibly produce.

The Cosmological Principle (CP) says that if you zoom out far enough, the universe is homogeneous and isotropic — the cosmic web's filaments and voids average out into a smooth mist. A commonly cited estimate puts the ceiling on genuine structure at roughly 370 Mpc: above that scale, gravity hasn't had time since the Big Bang to organize matter into coherent objects, so anything larger shouldn't exist as a real, formed thing. Yet the past two decades have produced a rogue's gallery of claimed violations: the Sloan Great Wall, the Huge Large Quasar Group, the Hercules–Corona Borealis Great Wall, the giant GRB ring, and — from these same authors in 2022 — the Giant Arc (GA), a 1-gigaparsec crescent at redshift 0.8.

This paper adds the Big Ring (BR): a striking annulus of Mg II absorbers roughly 400 Mpc in diameter (present-epoch proper size), at the *same* redshift as the Giant Arc and only about 12 degrees away on the sky. The method is the group's signature technique: intervening Mg II absorption doublets in SDSS quasar spectra trace faint gas associated with galaxies and clusters, giving exquisitely precise redshifts for matter too dim to see directly. The BR was found in the newer DR16Q-based Mg II catalog (Anand et al. 2021) while re-examining the Giant Arc field. The authors run four independent significance methods, check the structure against artefacts, corroborate it with field quasars and DESI clusters, rotate it in 3D (finding it's actually a coil, not a flat ring), argue against a baryon-acoustic-oscillation origin, and float cosmic strings as one speculative explanation.

**Prior Belief Check:** This finding *complicates* rather than overturns consensus, and — importantly — the expert community is genuinely split on whether results of this class are surprising at all. One camp (including the authors) sees an accumulating pattern of CP-violating structures. Another camp argues that human-eye-triggered detections in sparse, inhomogeneous tracer catalogs, assessed post hoc, reliably "find" such structures even in perfectly homogeneous random data — this critique was leveled quantitatively at the Huge-LQG in 2013 and at the Giant Arc itself in subsequent literature. So the honest calibration is: the *observation* is careful and internally well-checked, but its *interpretation* as a genuine CP challenge is contested, not consensus. This is not an incremental confirmation of an accepted picture; it is a provocative data point in an active dispute.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is a single-group result with no independent confirmation of the BR itself — the discovery, member selection, and all significance testing come from Lopez, Clowes & Williger, and the corroborating tracers (field quasars, DESI clusters) were examined by the same team in the same field. Independent confirmation would look like a blind, algorithmic structure search over the full DR16Q/DESI Mg II catalogs by a different group, with pre-registered significance criteria, recovering the BR without knowing where to look; that matters because the central methodological criticism of this research program is precisely that eye-guided, post-hoc selection inflates significance.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Mg II absorbers as matter tracers.** A quasar is a brilliant backlight billions of light-years away. If its beam passes through cool gas hosting singly ionized magnesium — the halo gas of a galaxy or cluster — the gas takes two characteristic bites out of the spectrum: the Mg II doublet at rest wavelengths 2796 and 2803 Å. The observed wavelength of the bite tells you *exactly* how far along the beam the gas sits.

*Analogy:* Each quasar is a laser pointer shone through the universe toward your detector, and every Mg II cloud along the beam clips a two-notch barcode into the light at a position encoding its distance. Thousands of laser pointers give you a 3D point cloud of otherwise invisible matter.
**Breaks when:** you forget that you only get data *where there happens to be a laser pointer*. No background quasar behind a region means that region is dark to you no matter how much matter it contains — the map's completeness is dictated by the probe distribution, not by the matter.

**The homogeneity scale and "uLSS."** Structure formation is hierarchical: small lumps first, big lumps later, and there's a rough ceiling (~370 Mpc) beyond which coherent structures shouldn't have had time to form.

*Analogy:* Look at the ocean from a plane — up close it's all chop and swell, but from altitude it's a flat blue sheet. The homogeneity scale is the altitude above which the cosmic ocean should look flat; the authors' "ultra-large LSS" label is reserved for waves that remain visible from cruising altitude.
**Breaks when:** you treat 370 Mpc as a hard theorem. It's a statistical estimate, and rare, marginal fluctuations of larger extent are not strictly forbidden in ΛCDM — the argument is about probabilities and about how you count "coherent," not about an absolute law.

**SLHC clustering and the linkage scale.** Single-Linkage Hierarchical Clustering connects points into candidate structures if they're closer than a chosen ruler length (the linkage scale, here ~76–80 Mpc, scaled to field density).

*Analogy:* Connect-the-dots with a fixed-length ruler — any two dots closer than the ruler get a line; connected blobs of 10+ dots are candidates.
**Breaks when:** the dot pattern is incomplete. Missing dots (no background probe there) snap one real structure into several fragments — which is exactly what happens: the BR shows up as four or five overlapping SLHC fragments, individually insignificant, rather than one object.

**CHMS versus MST significance.** CHMS shrink-wraps a candidate structure in a convex hull and asks how often randomly scattered points of the same number would occupy so small a volume. The MST test instead asks whether the *average nearest-neighbor spacing* inside the structure is anomalously short.

*Analogy (computer architecture):* CHMS measures the memory footprint of an allocation — total address range spanned — while MST measures cache-line locality — how tightly packed the actual accesses are. A ring is like a data structure with a huge hole in its address range: enormous footprint, but the elements themselves are tightly clustered along the ring. Footprint-based metrics (CHMS) systematically undersell it; locality-based metrics (MST) capture it.
**Breaks when:** pushed to declare one metric "correct." Both encode assumptions about what a structure is; the paper's own spread (2.5σ to 5.2σ across methods and member selections) shows the answer depends on the question you pose.

**The Cuzick–Edwards test.** Borrowed from epidemiology: given cases (absorbers) embedded in an uneven background population (quasar probes), do cases cluster among their k nearest neighbors more than chance allows?

*Analogy:* Testing whether disease cases cluster in a city where population density itself is lumpy — you compare cases against the local population, not against a uniform grid.
**Breaks when:** you ask it about a specific object. It certifies field-level clustering only; it cannot say "the ring is real," and here it delivered the weakest verdict of all the tests (~2σ).

**Central analogy for this paper: mapping fog with scattered headlights.**

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

Here's what the authors actually do, reorganized as a chain of evidence rather than by paper section.

**Step 1 — Find it, then interrogate whether it's even data.** While examining the Giant Arc field in the new Anand21 Mg II catalog, they notice a visually arresting ring of absorbers ~400 Mpc across at z ≈ 0.8, north of the GA. Before any statistics, they verify the raw inputs: all 59 Mg II doublet systems belonging to the visually identified ring and its inner filament are individually inspected in the quasar spectra, and every one is a genuine absorption doublet at the cataloged redshift. So the points are real; the question is whether the *pattern* is.

![Figure 1 from the paper: the tangent-plane Mg II absorber distribution at z = 0.802 ± 0.060, with the Big Ring visible north of center](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.07591v2/x1.png)

*This is the discovery image — smoothed, probe-flat-fielded absorber density contours. The ring sits at top center; the previously found Giant Arc occupies the field to its south. Everything else in the paper is an attempt to decide whether this visual impression survives scrutiny.*

**Step 2 — Rule out the boring explanations.** Because the absorber map is only as good as the probe (quasar) map behind it, they check whether the ring is a ghost of probe inhomogeneity: they image the probe distribution directly, blink it against the absorber image (most probe overdensities do *not* coincide with ring absorbers), and — cleverly — re-map absorption from the *same* probes in the adjacent, non-overlapping redshift slice. If the ring were an artefact of where the quasars sit, it would reappear at other redshifts along the same sightlines. It doesn't: the neighboring slice shows no corresponding structure by either SLHC/CHMS or FilFinder. They also audit the catalogs themselves and surface an uncomfortable fact: the new Anand21 catalog and the older Z&M catalog agree on only ~60% of absorbers from shared probes, with each finding real doublets the other missed.

**Step 3 — Stack four significance methods on four member definitions.** They step through five overlapping redshift slices and find the ring signal peaks at z = 0.802 ± 0.060, with a lower-arc echo persisting into the two higher slices. Then they compute CHMS and MST significances for the SLHC-identified members, the visually identified members (with and without interior absorbers), and the FilFinder-identified members. The headline 5.2σ is the CHMS value for the visually selected "ring plus innards" set — explicitly labeled by the authors as the likely *upper limit*. The MST test, better suited to holey geometries, lands consistently near 4σ across all member definitions (4.7, 4.1, 4.0, 3.6), and an independent Alpha-Hull/Poisson estimate agrees at 4.0σ with a measured overdensity of 0.75. The field-level Cuzick–Edwards test finds only tentative clustering (p ≈ 0.024, about 2σ) — though notably, four comparison fields at the same redshift and declination show essentially none. Averaging everything: 3.88 ± 0.83σ, with the CHMS spread flagged as method-driven volume overestimation.

**Step 4 — Corroborate with independent tracers.** FilFinder (a filament-skeleton algorithm built for interstellar clouds, repurposed here for cosmology) is run on the absorber image with progressively harsher size cuts: the ring is the last filament standing, surviving until the threshold exceeds 4200 pixels. The same procedure applied to the *field quasars* (bright quasars living in the slice, not the background probes) also leaves a ring-like filament coinciding with the BR — a genuinely independent dataset tracing the same shape. DESI photometric cluster contours likewise track the absorber contours, most strikingly along the ring's lower half.

**Step 5 — Rotate it.** Projecting the 51 ring absorbers onto planes perpendicular to various axes reveals the on-sky annulus is a *coil* in 3D — a corkscrew aligned nearly face-on to our line of sight, with a thin, flat, dense central filament. Viewed "from the bottom," it would have looked like an arc, not a ring — a pointed illustration that these discoveries are projections and our vantage point is arbitrary.

![Figure 23 from the paper: the BR absorbers projected at 45 degrees, revealing a coil-like 3D structure](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.07591v2/x23.png)

*The same absorbers seen from a 45° rotated viewpoint: ovoid loops of near and far absorbers connected by the thin inner filament — a coil, not a hoop.*

**Step 6 — Kill the BAO hypothesis, gesture at exotica.** A fitted ellipse gives semi-axes of 184 × 162 Mpc, larger than the ~150 Mpc BAO radius, and a single acoustic shell should be a diffuse spherical density enhancement, not a thin coil. The authors conclude a BAO origin is unlikely and note that cosmic strings — line-like topological defects from the early universe — have been invoked for other anomalous geometric patterns, offering them as one speculative origin. Conformal Cyclic Cosmology gets a passing mention in the same spirit.

### Assumption Audit

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes 5.2σ is *the* significance of the Big Ring. The paper actually says 5.2σ is the upper-limit CHMS value computed on the *visually hand-selected* absorbers including everything inside the ring; the more geometry-appropriate MST test gives ~4σ, FilFinder-based CHMS gives 2.5σ, and the field-level CE test gives 2.0σ. The defensible headline is "roughly 4σ, method-dependent."

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes "a look-elsewhere effect should not be a factor" (the authors' claim) means this was a blind search. The paper actually says they confined analysis to the GA field — but they were looking at that field *because* the GA had already been found there, and the BR was identified by eye before any statistic was computed. The quoted σ values are conditioned on a selection process (human pattern recognition over a favored field) whose trial factor is unquantified and arguably unquantifiable.

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes the two Mg II catalogs are interchangeable measurements of the same underlying absorber population. The paper actually documents only ~60% agreement between Anand21 and Z&M on shared probes — and shows the Giant Arc itself looks qualitatively different in the new catalog (16 of its 51 absorbers missing). The structures survive re-cataloging, but the tracer map itself is soft at the tens-of-percent level.

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes "ring" describes the 3D object. The paper actually shows the ring exists only in our line-of-sight projection; in 3D it's a face-on coil with a flat central filament — which changes what any formation mechanism (BAO, string loop, chance filament alignment) must explain.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Two things stand out as new to the field, not just to the reader. First, the *discovery itself in combination with the Giant Arc*: a second independent uLSS candidate at the same redshift, 12° away, upgrades the question from "is one weird structure a fluke?" to "why is this particular cosmic neighborhood at z ≈ 0.8 so busy?" — a co-location that either points to correlated physics or to a shared systematic, and both options are scientifically interesting. Second, the *project-plane rotation analysis* is a genuinely clever methodological move: by rotating the absorber cloud and showing the ring is a coil that would read as an "arc" from other vantage points, the authors expose — with unusual candor — the projection bias baked into their own discovery channel, and turn it into a tool (re-examine "boring" fields from other angles). The repurposing of FilFinder from parsec-scale interstellar clouds to 100-Mpc cosmology, and the survival-under-size-threshold elimination test, is a smaller but neat transplant; likewise the continued use of the epidemiological Cuzick–Edwards test in cosmology, which they pioneered in the GA paper.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

The visually triggered, post-hoc detection pipeline means the quoted significances do not include a trial factor for human pattern-search over rich catalogs, and the authors' "no look-elsewhere" argument is unpersuasive to much of the field. **(B) Contested** — the authors explicitly defend their confinement to one field, while independent critiques (of the Huge-LQG in 2013, and of the Giant Arc specifically in follow-up literature) demonstrate that comparable structures arise by chance in homogeneous mock catalogs under similar detection procedures; reasonable experts genuinely disagree here. (broader literature)

Whether these structures actually violate ΛCDM even *if* real is unresolved: the ~370 Mpc homogeneity ceiling is itself an estimate, and "connected tracer set" is not the same as "causally formed object." **(B) Contested** — the definitional question of what counts as a structure, versus a percolation feature of the cosmic web, is an active argument in the LSS literature rather than a settled criterion. (broader literature)

The tracer map is soft: only ~60% catalog-to-catalog agreement on shared probes, unexplained algorithmic disagreements between Anand21 and Z&M, and absorber completeness slaved to the accidental geometry of background quasars. **(A) Consensus** — the paper itself documents the disagreement and repeatedly attributes structure fragmentation to dataset incompleteness. (paper §2.1, §3.1)

The significance spread (2.5σ–5.2σ) reflects method mismatch — CHMS volume overestimation on ring/coil geometry — so the statistical case leans heavily on the MST test's ~4σ consistency. **(A) Consensus** — explicitly acknowledged and analyzed in the paper's own summary of significances. (paper §3.2.4)

The cosmic-string origin is a gesture, not a model: no string tension, loop statistics, or predicted absorber kinematics are computed, and no alternative-hypothesis comparison is performed. **(C) Speculative** — the paper offers strings only as a possibility by citation to other anomaly claims, and I'm judging the absence of quantitative modeling as a substantive gap; a specialist may know of string-network predictions that fit better than I credit. (analyst inference)

For the next 12–24 months (from the paper's vantage): DESI spectroscopy should deepen the Mg II catalogs and sharpen morphology; a blind, pre-registered structure search over the full footprint by an independent group is the decisive test; and the GA+BR (and now the 2026 "Giant Ring" claim from the same group, in the same field) system demands either a formation mechanism or a demonstration that the detection pipeline manufactures such systems from noise. **(C) Speculative** on my framing of priorities. (analyst inference)

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

Lopez, Clowes & Williger report the Big Ring: a ~400 Mpc-diameter, annulus-shaped arrangement of Mg II absorption systems at redshift 0.8, discovered while re-examining the Giant Arc field with the newer DR16Q-based absorber catalog. The Mg II technique uses background quasars as backlights: cool magnesium-bearing gas in galaxy halos imprints a distinctive absorption doublet on each quasar spectrum, and the doublet's observed wavelength pins the gas's distance with a precision of tens of kilometers per second — vastly better than photometric methods. Mapping thousands of such absorption events builds a 3D picture of faint matter, with the crucial caveat that you can only see matter where a quasar happens to shine from behind.

The authors' validation program is genuinely thorough within its own frame. Every member absorber was visually confirmed in the raw spectra. The ring does not track overdensities in the background probe distribution, and the same probes produce no corresponding structure in the neighboring redshift slice — the two standard signatures of an instrumental or selection artefact are absent. Bright quasars living *in* the slice (an independent dataset) trace a ring-shaped filament coinciding with the absorber ring when run through the same filament-finding machinery, and DESI galaxy-cluster contours follow the absorber contours as well. Statistically, the ring registers around four standard deviations above random expectation by the minimal-spanning-tree measure across every way of defining its membership, with the often-quoted 5.2 sigma figure being an acknowledged upper limit from a volume-based method applied to hand-picked members, and a field-level epidemiological clustering test delivering a much weaker two-sigma verdict.

The most physically interesting result may be the 3D anatomy: rotating the absorber positions reveals the "ring" is a corkscrew-like coil, viewed nearly face-on, threaded by a thin flat filament. That geometry, plus a fitted size exceeding the baryon-acoustic-oscillation scale, argues against the one conventional mechanism that naturally makes rings, leaving the origin open; the authors float cosmic strings as speculation.

Why frame the summary this way? The key interpretive choice is to separate three claims the paper tends to blend: (i) the absorbers are real (established beyond reasonable doubt), (ii) the pattern is statistically anomalous under the paper's own null model (supported at roughly 4σ, method-dependent), and (iii) the pattern challenges the Cosmological Principle (contested, because the null model does not account for how the structure was found, and because independent groups have argued that similar pipelines find similar structures in homogeneous simulated skies). A reader should hold (i) firmly, (ii) provisionally, and (iii) as an open fight. The co-location with the Giant Arc — and the same group's 2026 claim of yet another ring in the same field — cuts both ways: it's either evidence of correlated exotic physics or a hint that this field-plus-pipeline combination has a shared systematic.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** Section 5 (Discussion and Conclusions) of the paper was truncated in retrieval, so my rendering of the authors' own concluding arguments — particularly any quantitative GA+BR joint-system discussion and the details of their cosmic-string and CCC framing — is reconstructed from the abstract, introduction, and in-text statements rather than read directly; additionally, my characterization of the external critique literature (chance-occurrence arguments against the Giant Arc and similar structures) is from memory of the field rather than from sources fetched in this session, and the specific strength of those counter-analyses deserves independent verification.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. Astronomers found a 400-megaparsec ring of matter — bigger than the scale at which the universe is supposed to look smooth — sitting right next to the Giant Arc they found two years earlier, at the same distance, and one such neighbor might be luck but two starts to look like either new physics or a flaw in the method.

2. The "ring" is only a ring from where we happen to sit: in 3D it's a face-on corkscrew coil, which rules out the standard sound-wave-relic explanation and is a beautiful reminder that every sky map is a projection chosen by our vantage point.

3. The significance depends on how you ask: the same object scores anywhere from 2σ to 5.2σ across five statistical tests, because measuring the "volume" of a hollow ring is genuinely ambiguous — a case study in why single headline sigmas on exotic structures deserve suspicion.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Astronomers have a clever trick for mapping matter too faint to see: use distant quasars as backlights. When a quasar's light passes through gas around an intervening galaxy, magnesium atoms in that gas absorb a distinctive double notch from the spectrum, and the notch's position reveals exactly how far away the gas sits. Mapping thousands of these absorption shadows builds a three-dimensional picture of the dim cosmic web.

Using this method, the team that discovered the "Giant Arc" in 2022 now reports a second colossal structure in almost the same place: the Big Ring, a circular arrangement of absorbers about 1.3 billion light-years across, at the same distance as the Arc and only twelve degrees away on the sky. That size matters because the standard cosmological model expects the universe to look smooth and structureless above roughly 1.2 billion light-years — coherent objects bigger than that shouldn't have had time to form since the Big Bang.

The authors check their discovery carefully. Every absorption signal was verified by eye in the raw data. The ring doesn't mirror quirks in where the background quasars happen to sit, and independent tracers — bright quasars within the same volume and galaxy clusters from a separate survey — follow the same shape. Statistically, the pattern stands about four standard deviations above random chance, though different tests give answers from two to five sigma because measuring a hollow ring's volume is inherently ambiguous. Rotating the data in three dimensions delivers a surprise: the ring is actually a corkscrew-shaped coil seen face-on, which argues against its being a relic sound wave from the early universe. The authors speculate — without detailed modeling — that exotic objects called cosmic strings might create such shapes.

The catch: the structure was spotted by eye first and tested afterward, in a field the team was already studying, and skeptics argue such pipelines can find impressive-looking patterns even in featureless random data. The absorbers are certainly real; whether the pattern truly breaks the universe's large-scale smoothness remains a live and genuinely contested question.
