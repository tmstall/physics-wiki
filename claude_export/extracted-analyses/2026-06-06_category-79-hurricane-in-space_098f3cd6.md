# **Category 79 Hurricane in Space: J2318's Ultraviolet Wind Shatters the Record Books**

*A quasar 11 billion light-years away is blasting carbon and silicon ions outward at 30% the speed of light — and nobody yet knows how those ions survived the trip.*

---

## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**The Fastest UV Quasar Wind Ever Caught in the Act — and It's Growing**

A supermassive black hole more than a billion times the mass of the Sun is hurling gas at 30% the speed of light — and the wind is getting stronger while astronomers watch.

---

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Every massive galaxy hosts a supermassive black hole at its center. When that black hole is actively feeding — gorging on an accretion disk of infalling gas — it becomes a quasar: briefly one of the brightest objects in the observable universe. A quasar outshines its entire host galaxy, which might contain hundreds of billions of stars, from a region no bigger than our solar system. The physics happening in that tiny zone shapes galaxy evolution across cosmic time.

The critical connection is **feedback**: the energy pumped back into the host galaxy by quasar winds. These winds carry mass, momentum, and kinetic energy into the surrounding gas, heating it, disrupting star formation, and sometimes blowing material clean out of the galaxy altogether. Galaxy formation simulations have included this feedback mechanism for decades, but the actual observations to pin it down properly — especially at the extreme high-velocity end — have been scarce. Every record-breaking outflow discovered adds a hard constraint the models must obey.

J2318 now holds the record for the fastest quasar outflow *first detected in the ultraviolet*, with absorption features from carbon (C IV) and silicon (Si IV) ions clocked at 77,000 to at least 90,000 km/s — that's 26–30% of the speed of light. Even faster winds exist in X-rays, but UV outflows are special: they carry information about *ion chemistry*, not just raw velocity, which is what makes J2318 theoretically explosive.

The quasar sits at a cosmological redshift of about 2.68 — meaning we see it as it was roughly 11 billion years ago, when the universe was only about 2.5 billion years old. It lives in the constellation Pegasus and was hiding in plain sight in 20 years of archival Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) data until a sharp-eyed undergraduate spotted it.

## 3. Background Crash-Course

**Quasars and accretion disks.** A quasar is what you get when a supermassive black hole accretes so furiously that the infalling disk glows hotter than a stellar surface across its entire area. Radiation pressure from that blazing disk can then accelerate gas outward — think of a firehose where the water is driven not by a pump but by pure light pressure.

**BAL quasars and absorption troughs.** About 10–15% of quasars show "broad absorption line" (BAL) features — broad, blueshifted notches in their UV spectra where gas moving *toward us* absorbs specific wavelengths of the quasar's own light. The amount of blueshift directly encodes outflow speed. Think of a radar gun: the faster the gas moves toward you, the more its absorption features shift to shorter (bluer) wavelengths. J2318's troughs are shifted so far blue that they're at velocities previously seen only in X-ray ultrafast outflows (UFOs).

**C IV and Si IV as tracers.** Carbon IV (triply ionized carbon, meaning it's lost 3 of its 6 electrons) and Silicon IV (triply ionized silicon) are the workhorses of UV quasar spectroscopy. They produce strong, well-known absorption doublets around 1549 Å and 1394 Å respectively. You can only *see* these ions if the gas is neither too cold (atoms not ionized enough) nor too hot (atoms completely stripped, leaving nothing to absorb). This is the crux of J2318's puzzle — the radiation environment that accelerates gas to 0.3c normally also strips those ions completely.

**The Eddington ratio.** The Eddington luminosity is the point at which radiation pressure on infalling gas exactly balances gravity — it sets a natural "speed limit" on how fast a black hole can feed. Quasars near or above the Eddington limit tend to drive the most powerful winds, because radiation pressure literally overwhelms the infalling matter. J2318 runs at about 45% of Eddington — not a record, but well into the regime where strong winds are expected.

**PDS 456 — the comparison case.** PDS 456 is the most luminous quasar in the nearby universe, running at roughly its Eddington luminosity and hosting exceptionally energetic X-ray winds with power up to 20% of the Eddington luminosity. It's the only other known quasar with a UV-absorbing outflow at 0.3c, making it J2318's closest known twin — and the comparison is unsettling in the best possible way.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**Step 1: Finding the object.** Graduate student Marianna Veltri flagged J2318 as anomalous while still an undergraduate, using SDSS spectral data. After Hall examined the quasar using software developed by undergraduate researcher Zezhou Zhu, the team realized they were looking at something extraordinary. The SDSS spreads quasar light into spectra — like a prism resolving starlight into a rainbow — and practiced eyes learn to spot the unusual absorption pattern signatures. This is not automated pipeline output; it's pattern recognition by trained humans staring at thousands of spectra.

**Step 2: Redshift confirmation — the weak-lined quasar problem.** J2318 is a "weak-lined quasar" (WLQ), meaning its UV emission lines are unusually faint. That creates a redshift ambiguity: if you can't see strong emission lines, you can't easily anchor the spectrum to a known redshift. The team resolved this by obtaining near-infrared spectroscopy with the Gemini North telescope in Hawai'i, which let them detect the H-alpha emission line (hydrogen's red transition, shifted into the infrared by cosmological expansion). This yields a precise redshift of z = 2.6781 ± 0.0004 — the anchor point that then lets them correctly interpret the UV absorbers as C IV and Si IV at extreme blueshift rather than different ions at lower velocity.

This is a non-trivial step. Getting the redshift wrong by even a little would misidentify the absorption troughs entirely. The Gemini confirmation is what makes the velocity measurement trustworthy.

**Step 3: Measuring the outflow velocity.** Once the true redshift is pinned, the C IV absorption troughs appear at wavelengths shifted by an amount corresponding to recession velocities of –77,000 to at least –90,000 km/s. These are *relativistic* velocities requiring a special-relativistic correction to convert observed wavelength shifts into physical speeds. At 30% of the speed of light, the naive "wavelength shift divided by rest wavelength" formula overestimates the velocity by a few percent — the team correctly applies the relativistic Doppler formula.

The "at least" qualifier on 90,000 km/s is important: the blue edge of the trough may be cut off by the edge of their spectral coverage. The true maximum velocity could be higher.

**Step 4: Temporal variability — the outflow is evolving.** The C IV outflow strengthened monotonically over three spectral epochs spanning roughly 2.2 rest-frame years. This is the behavior of gas whose covering fraction (the fraction of the quasar's emission that the absorbing gas intercepts) is growing, or whose column density is increasing, or both. In any case: the wind is not static. It's actively developing.

Meanwhile, a 20-year photometric time series shows peak-to-peak brightness variability of 0.5 magnitudes in the g-band — roughly a factor of 1.6 in optical flux. The quasar breathes. The accretion disk fluctuates in brightness over decades, which directly drives changes in the ionization state of gas being irradiated by it, which in turn changes how visible the absorption troughs are. The growing C IV trough may partly reflect the quasar brightening and re-ionizing gas that was previously over-ionized and invisible.

**Step 5: Black hole mass and Eddington ratio.** They use virial mass estimation — measuring the width of broad emission lines and the luminosity of the continuum to infer how fast gas orbits the black hole's gravitational potential well. This gives:

$$M_{BH} = 1.65 \times 10^9 \, M_\odot$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$M_{BH}$ $M_{BH}$ $M_{BH}$: black hole mass
$M_\odot$ $M_\odot$ $M_\odot$: one solar mass, about 2 × 10³⁰ kg

**What this actually means:** The black hole weighs 1.65 *billion* suns. That's typical for a luminous quasar. The Eddington luminosity for this mass works out to 2.4 × 10⁴⁷ erg/s — roughly the total energy output of 60 trillion suns simultaneously. The Eddington ratio comes out to 0.45, meaning J2318 is feeding at 45% of its theoretical maximum rate. High, but not outrageously so — the extreme *velocity* of the wind is not simply explained by near-Eddington accretion alone.

**Step 6: Energetics — does this wind matter for the galaxy?** The team computes the outflow's kinetic luminosity (the power carried by the moving mass) using conservative assumptions about the geometry and covering fraction.

$$\frac{L_{kin}}{L_{bol}} \geq 0.75\%$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$L_{kin}$ $L_{kin}$ $L_{kin}$: kinetic luminosity of the outflow (power = ½ mass flow rate × velocity²)
$L_{bol}$ $L_{bol}$ $L_{bol}$: bolometric luminosity (total radiation output of quasar)

**What this actually means:** The conventional threshold for AGN feedback to significantly affect host galaxy evolution is 0.5–1% of bolometric luminosity. J2318's *lower limit* sits right at 0.75%, just above that threshold. This is with maximally conservative assumptions. Comparison to PDS 456 suggests the true mass outflow rate and kinetic luminosity ratio could be up to two orders of magnitude larger — meaning the real number could be 75%, not 0.75%. If true, this outflow isn't just flirting with galaxy-scale feedback — it's dominating it.

The mass outflow rate lower limit is 0.82 solar masses per year — the black hole is blowing away at least the equivalent of almost one entire Sun's worth of material every year, at 30% the speed of light.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**Trick 1: The weak-lined quasar as a window.** WLQs have anomalously weak UV emission lines, which has frustrated astronomers for years. Here that weakness actually *helps*: it means the continuum under the absorption troughs is cleaner and easier to normalize, making the BAL troughs easier to measure precisely. The team leverages what is usually a liability as a diagnostic asset. More importantly, WLQs are thought to have unusually soft ionizing continua (fewer extreme-UV photons), which may be *why* C IV and Si IV ions survive at 0.3c in this object when they shouldn't. The WLQ nature of J2318 might not be a coincidence — it might be the physical reason this record-breaking UV outflow is detectable at all.

**Trick 2: Undergraduate-enabled discovery at frontier science.** This is genuinely new as a methodological statement. The SDSS data infrastructure is now rich enough, and the visual pattern-recognition bar for flagging anomalous spectra is trained enough among undergraduates, that a student who hadn't yet finished a bachelor's degree identified the most extreme UV outflow ever detected. The paper explicitly acknowledges this and Hall frames it as a methodological milestone. In an era of survey astronomy, the bottleneck is human attention to anomalous signals — and this paper demonstrates that bottleneck can be democratized.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**The ion survival problem is wide open.** This is the paper's own central theoretical provocation: the existence of such a high-velocity outflow implies that models must either accelerate gas to 0.3c while preserving C IV and Si IV ions, or enable formation of those ions in gas already moving at 0.3c. The paper explicitly cannot resolve this. No current radiation-driven wind model cleanly explains how carbon and silicon survive being pushed to a third of the speed of light by the same photons that strip electrons from atoms. This is the big homework problem handed to theorists.

**Single-object statistics.** J2318 is one object. PDS 456 is one object. Two data points for UV outflows at 0.3c is not a population. The "Fast and Furious" family referenced in the title is effectively a family of two. Whether J2318's properties are typical of a hidden population or a cosmic freak show cannot be determined yet.

**The velocity lower limit.** The true maximum velocity of the C IV trough may be cut off by the spectral coverage edge. The 90,000 km/s figure is almost certainly a floor, not a ceiling. They need bluer coverage.

**Covering fraction uncertainty kills the energetics.** The kinetic luminosity calculation requires assumptions about what fraction of the quasar sky the outflow covers (the geometrical covering factor). This is the single largest source of uncertainty — the true mass outflow rate could be up to two orders of magnitude higher than the conservative lower limit, which means the true kinetic luminosity ratio could range from 0.75% all the way to ~75% of Eddington. That's a 100× range. The paper is honest about this, but it means the feedback conclusions are extremely uncertain.

**Distance to the absorbing gas unknown.** The kinetic luminosity also depends on how far from the black hole the absorbing gas sits — close-in absorption is physically quite different from kiloparsec-scale outflows. J2318's absorber distance is unconstrained.

**12–24 month priorities:** (1) Higher-resolution UV spectroscopy to resolve the trough structure and constrain the covering geometry. (2) X-ray observations to search for a co-existing ultra-fast outflow (UFO) component, as in PDS 456. (3) Theoretical modeling specifically of the weak-lined quasar ionization environment and whether a softer continuum genuinely stabilizes C IV/Si IV at 0.3c.

## 7. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **J2318 holds the UV speed record** — 30% the speed of light for a wind detected through carbon and silicon absorption, the fastest ultraviolet quasar outflow ever found, beating all prior UV detections and rivaling the fastest X-ray outflows.

2. **The wind shouldn't exist as observed** — the same radiation that drives gas to 0.3c should strip the carbon and silicon ions that make the wind detectable in UV, and current theory cannot explain how both happen simultaneously.

3. **Galaxy-scale feedback is on the table** — even the most conservative estimates put J2318's kinetic power at the threshold where black hole winds can reshape an entire host galaxy, and realistic estimates may be 100 times higher.

## 8. Detailed Summary & Explanation

**What they found and how**

Seaton and colleagues report the discovery of the most extreme ultraviolet outflow ever detected from a quasar. The object — officially SDSS J231854.31+243954.2, nicknamed J2318 — sits at a cosmological distance corresponding to a universe roughly 2.5 billion years old. Its central black hole weighs about 1.65 billion solar masses and feeds at roughly 45% of its theoretical maximum rate, putting out energy equivalent to tens of trillions of suns simultaneously.

The key observation is absorption: as J2318's light travels toward us, gas moving rapidly in our direction intercepts specific UV wavelengths and leaves dark notches (troughs) in the spectrum. Carbon ions (triply ionized, having lost three electrons) and silicon ions (also triply ionized) produce these troughs at known rest wavelengths. Because the gas is moving toward us at extreme speed, those troughs appear shifted dramatically toward shorter (bluer) wavelengths. The team measures this blueshift and, after correcting for the fact that 30% of the speed of light requires relativistic math rather than simple proportionality, arrives at outflow velocities between 77,000 and at least 90,000 kilometers per second. For comparison, the speed of light is about 300,000 km/s, so the gas is moving at roughly 26 to 30% of the cosmic speed limit.

The phrase "at least" matters. The fastest edge of the absorption trough appears to run off the edge of the available spectral data. The true maximum velocity is unknown and could be higher.

**The redshift confirmation challenge**

J2318 belongs to a class called weak-lined quasars, whose spectra lack the strong emission features normally used to determine how far away a quasar is. Without a good redshift, you cannot correctly identify which ions are producing which absorption troughs. The team solved this by obtaining near-infrared observations with the Gemini North telescope in Hawai'i, detecting the hydrogen emission line (the transition responsible for the red color of many nebulae, called H-alpha) redshifted out of the visible into the infrared by the universe's expansion. This pinned the distance precisely, confirming that the extreme blueshifted absorption troughs really are carbon and silicon racing toward us at 30% the speed of light — not some other ion at a lower velocity.

**Temporal evolution**

The team has spectra from three epochs spanning about 2.2 years of time as experienced in J2318's reference frame (accounting for time dilation from its cosmological redshift). Across those three epochs, the carbon outflow absorption trough grows monotonically stronger. The wind is not static — it is actively intensifying. Meanwhile, 20 years of photometric monitoring shows the quasar's optical brightness varies by roughly a factor of 1.6 from peak to trough. These brightness fluctuations drive changes in how much the accretion disk ionizes surrounding gas, which in turn changes which ionic species are detectable at which velocities. The growing trough may signal the wind itself strengthening, or gas that was previously over-ionized (and therefore invisible) becoming newly detectable as the ionizing flux shifts.

**The ion survival paradox**

Here is the central puzzle the paper openly cannot resolve. Quasar radiation accelerates gas by pushing on electrons still attached to ions — specifically through a process called line-driven acceleration, where photons at specific wavelengths are absorbed by ions and transfer momentum. This requires those ions to exist. But the very same intense radiation field that does the accelerating also tends to strip those ions completely — photoionizing them to bare nuclei that have no UV absorption transitions left and become invisible. At velocities as extreme as 0.3c, achieving the required momentum transfer while keeping the carbon and silicon ions intact is theoretically very difficult. Either the models need a way to shield the gas from over-ionization during acceleration, or the gas gets ionized during acceleration and re-forms the ions later once it's moved far enough from the source that the radiation field weakens. Neither scenario is cleanly modeled for 0.3c velocities.

**Energetics and galaxy feedback**

The team computes how much power this outflow carries — treating the moving gas as a stream of mass with kinetic energy equal to one-half mass-flow-rate times velocity squared. Using conservative numbers for the geometry (assuming the outflow covers only about 20% of the sky as seen from the quasar, consistent with the fraction of quasars that show BAL features at all), they get a minimum mass outflow rate of about 0.82 solar masses per year and a minimum kinetic power equal to about 0.75% of the quasar's total luminosity. The significance of 0.5–1% is that galaxy formation simulations have long assumed AGN feedback needs to deliver roughly this fraction of quasar luminosity into the surrounding galaxy to explain the observed relationship between black hole mass and galaxy properties. J2318's lower limit sits right at this threshold.

But comparison to the only similar system — the nearby quasar PDS 456, which shows 0.3c outflow in X-rays and whose geometry is better constrained — suggests J2318's true kinetic power could be 100 times higher. The conservative UV-only measurement is almost certainly a floor.

**Why J2318 is detectable when it shouldn't be**

The weak-lined quasar nature of J2318 may be the key. WLQs have softer ionizing continua — they put out fewer extreme-ultraviolet and X-ray photons per unit of visible-wavelength luminosity. A softer radiation field is less efficient at over-ionizing the absorbing gas, which may be precisely why carbon and silicon ions survive at 0.3c here when they would be stripped bare in a more typical quasar. If this interpretation is correct, WLQs may be systematically better places to find extreme UV outflows — a testable prediction for future surveys.

**The bigger picture**

This paper arrives at the same moment as a parallel Nature paper (Liu et al. 2026) showing via JWST that galaxy-scale quasar outflows in the early universe were far more frequent and powerful than at later epochs. J2318 is a case study in the small-scale, high-velocity end of the same feedback phenomenon. Together, these results are tightening the observational bracket on one of the most important unsolved problems in galaxy formation: how supermassive black holes, occupying a region smaller than our solar system, manage to regulate the growth of structures spanning hundreds of thousands of light-years.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Researchers led by York University discovered the fastest ultraviolet wind ever detected near a supermassive black hole, coming from a quasar called J2318 in the constellation Pegasus. The wind is made of carbon and silicon ions moving at 26 to 30% of the speed of light — velocities typically seen only in X-ray observations. The black hole powering this outflow weighs about 1.65 billion times the mass of our Sun and is feeding at roughly 45% of its theoretical maximum rate.

The discovery started with an undergraduate student flagging J2318 as unusual in archival sky survey data. Follow-up observations with the Gemini North telescope in Hawai'i confirmed the record-breaking speed. Monitoring over three spectral epochs shows the wind growing stronger with time, while 20 years of brightness measurements reveal the quasar itself fluctuating significantly in luminosity.

The central mystery is theoretical: the same intense light that pushes the gas to 30% of the speed of light should also strip the carbon and silicon ions that make the wind detectable in ultraviolet light. No existing model cleanly explains how both can happen simultaneously. The fact that J2318 belongs to a class of quasars with unusually weak ultraviolet emission — and therefore a softer radiation field — may be the key to why ions survive where they normally would not.

In terms of impact on its host galaxy, the most conservative accounting of the outflow's kinetic power puts it right at the threshold where black hole winds are expected to significantly reshape surrounding gas and suppress star formation. More realistic estimates suggest the true power could be a hundred times higher. Study co-author Paola Rodríguez Hidalgo describes these extreme outflows as a missing link between the active central region of a galaxy and its overall evolution — the elusive feedback mechanism that galaxy formation models have long assumed but rarely directly observed at this extreme.
