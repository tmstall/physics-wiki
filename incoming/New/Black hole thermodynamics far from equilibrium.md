## 1 — Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Thermodynamics Without Waiting for the End of Time**

The first law of black-hole mechanics — for fifty years a *passive* comparison between two neighboring equilibrium black holes — is rebuilt here as an *active, finite* energy budget for a black hole caught arbitrarily far from equilibrium, and the rebuild forces the hole's entropy onto the horizon you can locate *right now* rather than the one defined by the infinite future.

## 2 — Big-Picture Context

Bardeen, Carter and Hawking (1973) found that stationary black holes obey identities uncannily shaped like thermodynamics. The headline is the first law, $\delta M = (\kappa/8\pi G),\delta A + \Omega,\delta J$: nudge a Kerr hole to a neighboring Kerr hole and its mass change splits into a $(\text{surface gravity}\times\text{area})$ piece and a spin piece. Once Hawking showed black holes radiate at temperature $\kappa\hbar/2\pi$, the analogy hardened into physics: surface gravity *is* temperature, and one-quarter of the horizon area, $A/4G\hbar$, *is* entropy. That identification has driven half a century of quantum-gravity work.

But it was built on two idealizations, and both crack in any realistic setting. **First**, the BCH law is a relation on the *space of equilibria* — it compares two stationary states without any physical process connecting them; there is no infalling matter, no merger, no radiation. **Second**, and worse, the surface it uses is the **event horizon**, which is *teleological*: to know where it sits today you must know the entire spacetime out to the infinite future. Event horizons form and grow inside perfectly flat regions where nothing has happened yet, "anticipating" matter that has not arrived. Numerical-relativity codes can't even locate one until the simulation is over; and in questions like the endpoint of Hawking evaporation, you can't assume you know the far future at all. So the honest question — *what surface carries a dynamical black hole's entropy, and what is its first law under an actual process?* — was genuinely open.

**Paper Type & Stakes.** This is a mathematical-relativity Letter (with a 53-page companion carrying the proofs) that extends a foundational *structural* result — the first law — from equilibrium-adjacent to arbitrarily-far-from-equilibrium, and in doing so pins down what "temperature," "entropy," and the second law *mean* for a black hole mid-process. The stakes are conceptual foundations of black-hole thermodynamics, with direct downstream relevance to numerical relativity (which already runs on quasi-local horizons), to the evaporation/information problem, and to any microscopic account of $A/4G\hbar$. It is an Editors' Suggestion, which signals the community flagged it as notable.

**Prior Belief Check.** The *conclusion* — entropy is the area of a **marginally trapped surface**, not the event horizon — is **not surprising to experts as of 2026**. A strong convergence has formed over the last two years: Hollands–Wald–Zhang (PRD 2024) derived, at first perturbative order, that dynamical entropy equals ¼ the *apparent*-horizon area; Davies–Reall (2024) reached compatible conclusions via effective field theory; Furugori et al. What is **genuinely new and hard** here is doing it *non-perturbatively* — finite changes, arbitrary distance from equilibrium — with a clean uniqueness result for the energy vector field and a *quantitative* second law. So calibrate this as a significant, elegantly-executed advance inside an active and converging program, **not** a paradigm overthrow.

## 3 — Necessary Background Crash-Course

You own the black-hole-thermodynamics big picture (tier 2). What needs building from scratch is the *quasi-local horizon* geometry — a specialized corner of classical GR. I'll route the analogies through computer systems.

**Expansion and the marginally trapped surface (MTS).** Fire a spherical flash of light off a 2-surface and watch the outgoing wavefront's area. Its fractional rate of change is the **expansion** $\theta_{(k)}$ of the outgoing null normal $k^a$ — positive if the bundle spreads, negative if gravity is refocusing it. Deep inside a black hole, gravity is so strong that *even outgoing* light converges: $\theta_{(k)}\<0$. An **MTS** is the knife-edge where the outgoing expansion vanishes, $\theta_{(k)}=0$ — the outermost surface from which light is exactly held stationary.

> **Analogy:** $\theta$ is the *divergence of a vector field* in the exact CS sense — is this cell a net source or a net sink? An MTS is the locus where the outgoing "flow" is momentarily divergence-free.

> **Breaks when:** you assume the MTS is unique and canonical like a level set. It isn't — a single spatial slice can host *many* MTSs, and which one you get depends on how you slice spacetime (the notorious "jumpiness of apparent horizons"). The event horizon, teleological as it is, is at least foliation-independent; the MTS trades that global uniqueness for locality.

**Event horizon vs. quasi-local horizon — the load-bearing contrast.**

> **Analogy:** this is *offline (batch) vs. online (streaming) computation*. The event horizon is a global optimization over the entire execution trace — you cannot compute it until every instruction has retired (all of future null infinity is in hand). A **quasi-local horizon** is computed *online*, at each timestep, from purely local data available now.

> **Breaks when:** you push "online is strictly better." The online object depends on the input *schedule* (the foliation); the offline object is schedule-independent. You are buying locality and causality at the cost of slicing-dependence — a real trade, not a free win.

**The DHS / IHS taxonomy.** Stack the MTSs into a 3-surface (a "quasi-local horizon," topologically sphere × line). Where genuine flux is crossing — matter or gravitational waves pouring in — that stack is *spacelike* and is called a **Dynamical Horizon Segment (DHS)**: the hole is actively changing. Where the flux switches off, the stack goes *null* and settles into an **Isolated Horizon Segment (IHS)**: local equilibrium, though the universe around it may still be busy. A realistic history is a zebra of segments — DHS, IHS, DHS, IHS — as accretion episodes come and go.

> **Analogy:** DHS = a cache line being actively written (dirty bits flipping, state in flux); IHS = the same line quiesced and read-only. A merger is a burst of writes; the final black hole is the line settling to a stable value.

> **Breaks when:** you read "equilibrium" (IHS) as "nothing happening nearby." An IHS permits arbitrarily violent dynamics *just outside* it — only the horizon itself is momentarily stationary.

**Intensive vs. extensive, and the projection.** Kerr equilibria form a mere 2-parameter family — fix areal radius $R$ and horizon spin $J_H$ and you've fixed everything, including the intensive parameters $(\kappa,\Omega)$ (surface gravity, angular velocity). A *non-equilibrium* state is infinite-dimensional (a whole zoo of fields on the surface). The paper's projection map $\Pi$ throws away all that detail except the two numbers $(R,J_H)$ and reads off the *Kerr* $(\kappa,\Omega)$ that those two numbers would imply.

> **Analogy:** a *lossy projection onto a two-column key*. You deliberately forget the full runtime state of a cache line and keep only (address, size); black-hole *uniqueness theorems* guarantee those two fields index exactly one "reference" Kerr equilibrium, so the lookup is well-defined.

> **Central analogy for this paper:** *online per-timestep horizon, not offline whole-history horizon.*

## 4 — Core Technical Explanation

The argument runs in six moves. I've reorganized from the paper's order for clarity.

**Move 1 — Make the first law intrinsic to the horizon.** The textbook law smuggles in $M$ and $J$ measured at *infinity* (ADM charges). That is doubly wrong for dynamics: infinity is "far from the black hole," and ADM $M,J$ are *absolutely conserved* under any physical process, so $\delta M$ literally can't mean "energy that fell in." The authors first rewrite BCH using only **Komar charges evaluated at the horizon**: $Q_{(\mathring k)} ,\hat=, \mathring\kappa A/4\pi G$ and $Q_{(\mathring\varphi)},\hat=,-2J_H$. Now every symbol lives on the horizon, and the door to dynamics is open.

**Move 2 — Build the arena.** Introduce $\mathcal N$ (the infinite-dimensional space of non-equilibrium horizon states) and $\mathcal E$ (the 2-dimensional Kerr equilibrium space), joined by the projection $\Pi:\mathcal N\to\mathcal E$ from Move-3 of §3. An angular-momentum–area inequality makes $\Pi$ single-valued. This is the "conceptual arena that intertwines equilibrium and non-equilibrium" — it lets equilibrium (Kerr) tools reach into the dynamical regime, and it hands *every* wild non-equilibrium state a well-defined temperature and spin.

**Move 3 — The unique energy vector $\xi^a$.** On a Killing horizon, "energy" rides the Killing field. A DHS has no Killing field — but it has a preferred null direction $k^a$ (the one with $\theta_{(k)}=0$). Scale it, $\xi^a ,\hat=, \sqrt2,\|DR\|,f(R),k^a$, leaving a free function $f(R)$. Then impose one demand: whenever the DHS momentarily reaches equilibrium (becomes an IHS), the *dynamical* charge $Q_{(\xi)}$ must equal the *equilibrium* charge $\mathring\kappa A/4\pi G$. Remarkably, that single matching condition pins down **exactly one** $\xi^a$ — the choice $f(R)=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dR}!\left(\mathring\kappa A/2\pi G\right)$. This is the paper's key new element: a *canonical* notion of horizon energy far from equilibrium, which ordinary non-equilibrium thermodynamics simply lacks.

**Move 4 — Integrate the constraints on the horizon itself.** Now use Einstein's constraint equations — but evaluated *on the DHS* (a spacelike hypersurface) rather than on a slice stretching out to infinity. The contraction $(G_{ab}-8\pi G,T_{ab}),\xi^a\hat\tau^b,\hat=,0$ integrates to a **balance law**: the change in $Q_{(\xi)}$ between two MTSs equals the flux of energy (matter + gravitational waves) across the segment between them. The gravitational-wave flux density carries a surprise: $$\mathfrak f^{,\rm gws}_{(\xi)} ,\hat=, \frac{1}{8\pi G},\|DR\|,f(R),\big(\|\sigma\|^2 + 2\|\zeta\|^2\big).$$ The shear term $\|\sigma\|^2$ is the familiar one. The $\|\zeta\|^2$ term is **absent from all perturbative Killing-horizon formulas** — it appears only because a spacelike surface carries **4** gravitational phase-space degrees of freedom (2 configuration $\sigma$ + 2 "momentum-like" $\zeta$), where a null surface carries only 2. Both contribute to energy flux, exactly as in any field theory.

**Move 5 — Recast as a first law.** Define $t^a := \xi^a - \mathring\Omega\varphi^a$ (mirroring $\mathring t^a = \mathring k^a - \mathring\Omega\mathring\varphi^a$ on Kerr), and the balance law becomes the paper's central result:

> **Worked-equation box — the dynamical first law (their Eq. 4)**

> $$\mathfrak F_{(t)}\\ \\ \hat=\\ \\ \Delta!\left(\frac{\mathring\kappa,A}{4\pi G}\right)\\ +\\ 2,\Delta!\left(\mathring\Omega,\mathcal J_{\mathcal H}\right)$$

> **Symbol definitions:** $\mathfrak F_{(t)}$ : total flux of horizon energy (matter + gravitational-wave) across the *finite* segment between two marginally trapped surfaces (mass units, $G=c=1$) $\Delta$ : a *finite* difference, outer surface minus inner, $\Delta X = X\[S_2\]-X\[S_1\]$ — **not** an infinitesimal $\mathring\kappa$ : the Kerr-matched surface gravity assigned to each surface — instantaneous "temperature" up to the $\hbar/2\pi$ factor (inverse length) $A$ : area of the marginally trapped surface (length²) $\mathring\Omega$ : the Kerr-matched horizon angular velocity — the intensive parameter conjugate to spin (inverse length) $\mathcal J_{\mathcal H}$ : the horizon angular momentum on that surface (length²) $\hat=$ : equality holding on the quasi-local horizon

> **What this actually means:** a finite energy budget for a black hole mid-process, read like reconciling an account between two ledger snapshots. The left side is everything that flowed in across the horizon "wall" between snapshot $S_1$ and snapshot $S_2$. The right side is how the hole's own books changed — its (surface-gravity × area) term, which is temperature × entropy, plus a spin term. The crucial twist: $\mathring\kappa$ and $\mathring\Omega$ are *not* constants pulled outside the difference the way the textbook infinitesimal law pulls them out. They **drift** from one surface to the next, exactly as a cache's hit-rate or a server's load drifts during a traffic burst — so you must difference the *whole product*, not the area alone.

**Move 6 — The factor of 2 is not a paradox.** Four things differ from BCH: $\Delta$ is finite; the changes are *fluxes from physical processes* (an *active* law, not a passive comparison); $\mathring\kappa,\mathring\Omega$ are time-dependent and stay *inside* $\Delta$; and there's that factor of 2. The last two are two faces of one thing. Feed Eq. (4) through the **Smarr relation** (the scaling identity $\frac{A\delta\mathring\kappa}{4\pi G}+2J_H\delta\mathring\Omega ,\hat=, -(\frac{\mathring\kappa\delta A}{8\pi G}+\mathring\Omega\delta J)$) and, in the infinitesimal projection back to equilibrium, the "extra" $\delta\mathring\kappa$ and $\delta\mathring\Omega$ pieces recombine and the law collapses cleanly to the familiar $\delta M = (\mathring\kappa/8\pi G)\delta A + \mathring\Omega,\delta J$. The generalization *reduces to BCH* the instant the hole settles down. And since $(\mathring\kappa/8\pi G)\delta A = T,\delta S$ with $S=A/4G\hbar$, combining Eq. (4) with the Ashtekar–Krishnan (2003) area-increase law — area grows monotonically, *quantitatively* equal to the infalling flux — forces the entropy to be $A/4G\hbar$ of the **marginally trapped surface**, which mid-process sits *inside* the event horizon.

> **Worked numbers — the "inside-ness" gap (spherical Vaidya)**

> *Define:* a Schwarzschild hole fed by spherical infalling null dust, mass function $m(v)$ rising from $M_1$ to $M_2$. The MTS sits at $R_{\rm AH}=2Gm(v)$ — it tracks the *instantaneous* mass. The event horizon is a null surface that must reach $R_{\rm EH}=2GM_2$ at the end, and (teleologically) begins swelling *before* the dust arrives, always at or outside the MTS.

> *Tiny example* ($G=c=\hbar=1$): let $M_1=1\to M_2=2$. Entropy $S=A/4=\pi R^2$. • onset: MTS at $R=2$, area $16\pi$, $S=4\pi\approx 12.6$ • equilibrium: MTS at $R=4$, area $64\pi$, $S=16\pi\approx 50.3$ During accretion the event horizon has already swelled toward $R=4$ while the MTS still reads $R=2$, so the two horizons' entropies disagree by **up to a factor of ~4 mid-process** and coincide only at the end. Rigorously, $S_{\rm AH}\le S_{\rm EH}$ always, equality at equilibrium. *Second law with a number:* entropy rises by $\Delta S = 12\pi\approx 37.7$, and the area law says this equals (¼ times) the integrated flux of infalling dust energy — $\Delta M=1$ of infall converts to exactly $\Delta S=12\pi$ of horizon entropy, books balancing surface-by-surface.

> *Generalize:* for *any* process — spinning, wildly distorted, matter + gravitational waves — the same two statements hold. Using the event-horizon area instead would credit the account before the deposit clears; that is precisely the teleology being removed.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** You likely assume "far from equilibrium" means the intensive parameters (temperature, $\Omega$) go *ill-defined*, as in ordinary non-equilibrium thermodynamics. The paper says the **opposite** for black holes: uniqueness theorems let $\Pi$ assign each non-equilibrium state a *unique* $(\mathring\kappa,\mathring\Omega)$. Black holes are, in this one respect, *better*-behaved out of equilibrium than a box of gas.

> **Watch:** You likely read "generalized second law on DHSs" as **Bekenstein's** GSL (black-hole entropy + matter entropy outside). It is **not**. It denotes the Ashtekar–Krishnan *classical* area-increase-with-quantitative-flux theorem. There is no quantum or matter-entropy content anywhere in this Letter — it is pure classical GR.

> **Watch:** You likely assume the marginally trapped surface is unique, like the event horizon. It is famously **not** — MTSs are slicing-dependent and can appear discontinuously. The genericity condition $E:=\|\sigma\|^2+R_{ab}k^ak^b\neq 0$ explicitly removes degenerate cases, and entropy is assigned to a *specific chosen* dynamical horizon. Foliation-dependence is *managed* here, not eliminated.

## 5 — What's Genuinely New or Clever

Two moves stand out, both new to the *field* (not merely to you):

1.  **The uniqueness of the energy vector $\xi^a$.** The "surprise" the authors flag is real: demanding that the dynamical charge agree with the equilibrium charge at *every* momentary-equilibrium surface over-determines the free function $f(R)$ down to a single solution. That is what supplies a *canonical* horizon energy far from equilibrium — the exact ingredient ordinary non-equilibrium thermodynamics never gets, and the linchpin that turns a balance law into a *first law*.
2.  **The $\mathcal N\to\mathcal E$ projection as the conceptual engine.** Reframing the problem as a lossy map from an infinite-dimensional dynamical state space onto the 2-dimensional Kerr equilibrium space is what makes intensive parameters well-defined and lets equilibrium tools probe genuine dynamics. The $\|\zeta\|^2$ gravitational-wave flux term — a real, new physical contribution visible *only* because the horizon is spacelike (4 phase-space dof, not 2) — falls out of this same machinery.

Underneath both: the whole thing is *non-perturbative and finite*, where every prior treatment (Hawking–Hartle, Booth–Fairhurst, Hollands–Wald–Zhang, Davies–Reall) was infinitesimal or first-order.

## 7 — Detailed Summary & Explanation

For fifty years the first law of black-hole mechanics has been a statement about the *space of equilibrium* black holes: compare a stationary hole to a neighboring stationary hole and its mass change divides into a temperature-times-entropy piece and a rotation piece. Two idealizations underwrote it — no physical process actually happens, and the surface it references is the event horizon, whose location depends on the entire infinite future. Both fail for real, dynamical black holes, which accrete, merge, ring down, and (quantum-mechanically) evaporate.

Ashtekar, Paraizo and Shu replace the event horizon with a **quasi-local horizon** built from *marginally trapped surfaces* — the outermost surfaces holding outgoing light stationary, locatable at each instant from local data alone. Where matter or gravitational waves cross, this horizon is a genuinely time-changing "dynamical horizon segment"; where the flux stops, it settles to local equilibrium. Their central technical achievement is threefold. They give every non-equilibrium state a well-defined temperature and angular velocity by *projecting* it onto the unique Kerr equilibrium sharing its area and spin — exploiting the fact that black-hole uniqueness makes two numbers enough. They construct a *unique* horizon energy by demanding it agree with the equilibrium answer whenever the horizon momentarily equilibrates. And they integrate Einstein's constraint equations directly on the horizon to get a **finite** balance law: the energy that crossed a horizon segment equals the change in its temperature-entropy and spin terms — an *active* first law for a hole arbitrarily far from equilibrium, which reduces to the textbook law the moment the hole settles. Combined with a companion area-increase theorem (area grows, quantitatively equal to infalling flux), this forces the entropy of a dynamical black hole to be one-quarter the area of the *marginally trapped surface* — a surface lying inside the event horizon during any dynamical phase, coinciding with it only at equilibrium.

I framed the analysis around the *offline-vs-online* contrast because that is the true conceptual pivot: the entire advance is trading a horizon that requires the whole future for one computable now, then rebuilding thermodynamics on the local object. I foregrounded the convergence context (Hollands–Wald–Zhang and others reaching the same destination perturbatively) because it is the single most important calibration fact — it tells you the *conclusion* is expected and corroborated, so the paper's real weight is in the *non-perturbative, finite* execution and the uniqueness result, not in the surface identification per se. The takeaway: dynamical black-hole thermodynamics is now a finite, process-based, locally-computable subject, and its entropy has a definite home.

> **Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the precise argument by which the consistency condition *uniquely* fixes $\xi^a$ (the Letter asserts uniqueness and hands you the answer $f(R)=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dR}(\mathring\kappa A/2\pi G)$, but the actual over-determination proof lives in the 53-page companion I did not parse line-by-line), and, relatedly, whether my Smarr-based reconciliation of the factor of 2 reproduces their intended route exactly. ## 8 — Three Crystallized Takeaways

1.  **A dynamical black hole's entropy lives on the horizon you can see right now** — the marginally trapped surface — which sits *inside* the event horizon; the two agree only once the hole stops changing. The event horizon's dependence on the infinite future disqualifies it as physical entropy.
2.  **The old first law was a passive snapshot comparison; the new one is an active budget** — real infalling matter and gravitational waves, integrated over a *finite* process, with no assumption that the hole is near equilibrium — and it collapses back to Bardeen–Carter–Hawking the instant equilibrium returns.
3.  **Black holes are, surprisingly, tidier than a box of gas out of equilibrium**: their uniqueness theorems let you hand *any* wildly dynamical state a well-defined temperature and spin by matching it to a single reference Kerr hole — the move that ordinary non-equilibrium thermodynamics can never make.

## 9 — Shorter Summary

The first law of black-hole mechanics has, since 1973, described *equilibrium* black holes: compare two stationary holes and the mass difference splits into a temperature-times-entropy term and a spin term. It relied on the **event horizon** — a surface whose position depends on the entire infinite future, which forms inside empty regions "anticipating" matter, and which numerical codes can't locate until a simulation ends. That makes it a poor home for the entropy of a *real* black hole that accretes, merges, or evaporates.

This paper rebuilds the law on **quasi-local horizons** made of *marginally trapped surfaces* — the surfaces holding outgoing light stationary, findable at each instant from purely local data. Three ideas carry it. First, every out-of-equilibrium state is assigned a definite temperature and rotation rate by matching it to the unique stationary (Kerr) black hole with the same area and spin — possible because black holes are pinned down by just two numbers. Second, a *unique* notion of horizon energy is constructed by demanding it agree with the equilibrium answer whenever the horizon momentarily settles. Third, integrating Einstein's equations directly on the horizon yields a **finite** balance law valid arbitrarily far from equilibrium: the energy crossing a stretch of horizon equals the change in its temperature-entropy and spin terms. This law reduces to the classic one as soon as the hole calms down, and combined with a companion theorem that horizon area always grows in step with infalling energy, it forces a dynamical black hole's entropy to be one-quarter the area of the *marginally trapped* surface — which lies *inside* the event horizon while things are changing and merges with it only at the end.

The conclusion itself won't shock experts: several independent groups reached it over the past two years for small perturbations. The achievement is doing it **non-perturbatively**, with finite changes tied to actual physical processes, and proving the energy definition is unique. The honest caveats: it is entirely classical (entropy is still the Hawking analogy, not a microscopic count); the trapped surface is slicing-dependent and non-unique; and the evaporation regime the authors cite as motivation isn't yet treated. The cleanest way to hold it: swap a whole-history horizon for a right-now one, then rebuild thermodynamics on what you can actually measure.
