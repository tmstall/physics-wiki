## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Too Small for Four Dimensions: Why Every Primordial Black Hole in the "Dark Dimension" Would Be a Five-Dimensional Bead**

If our universe has a single extra dimension about a micron across, as the Swampland-motivated "Dark Dimension" scenario proposes, then the authors show that essentially every black hole born in the early universe is smaller than that dimension, so it lives and dies as a five-dimensional object. That changes how long it survives and what its death throes look like.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Primordial black holes are black holes that formed in the first fraction of a second after the Big Bang, out of overly dense patches of the hot early plasma rather than from collapsing stars. They have been a dark matter candidate for fifty years, and there is one surviving mass window where they could still be *all* of the dark matter: roughly asteroid-mass objects, too light to show up in microlensing surveys and too heavy to have evaporated yet. Almost every study of them assumes they are ordinary black holes of the kind general relativity describes in our three space dimensions plus time.

That assumption fails if gravity can reach into a hidden extra dimension that is not too small. The idea goes back to the 1998 "large extra dimensions" proposals (Arkani-Hamed, Dimopoulos and Dvali, plus Antoniadis), and it was revived in 2022 in a sharper form called the **Dark Dimension Scenario**. There, arguments from the Swampland program, a set of conjectured rules that any theory of quantum gravity must obey, combined with the tiny observed cosmological constant, single out one extra dimension roughly a micron across. Ordinary matter and light are stuck on a three-dimensional "brane" (a membrane-like slice of the five-dimensional space); gravity alone spreads into the extra dimension. A black hole whose horizon is much bigger than a micron does not notice the extra dimension and behaves as usual. One whose horizon is smaller than a micron is a different object: a five-dimensional black hole, with a different size, temperature and lifetime for the same mass.

The same group (Anchordoqui, Antoniadis, Lüst) had earlier assumed that dark-matter black holes in this scenario are five-dimensional and worked out the consequences: they evaporate more slowly, which loosens the observational limits. This paper asks the logically prior question: **if primordial black holes form at all in this scenario, are they four- or five-dimensional?** The authors check the three standard formation routes (inflation, phase transitions in the early plasma, collapsing cosmic-string loops) against quantum-gravity constraints and conclude that, barring new low-energy physics, they are always five-dimensional. Along the way they argue that the universe before about the first microsecond could not have been dominated by ordinary hot plasma, and they connect long-lived five-dimensional black holes to the puzzling ultra-high-energy neutrino recorded by the KM3NeT detector in 2023.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is a short theoretical paper that uses order-of-magnitude scaling arguments inside one specific beyond-Standard-Model framework; at stake is whether every primordial black hole constraint and signature in that framework has to be recomputed for five-dimensional objects.

**Prior Belief Check:** Within the Dark Dimension program this result is supportive and fairly expected; the group's earlier papers already *assumed* five-dimensional black holes, and this paper supplies the justification. Outside the program it is neither surprising nor contradictory to experts: once you grant a micron-sized dimension, it is simple arithmetic that black holes formed when the universe was very hot are tiny, so most are smaller than a micron. The less obvious pieces are the cosmic-string mass ceiling and the claim that the pre-microsecond universe must have been dominated by a rolling field rather than by radiation. The Dark Dimension itself is a contested proposal, not consensus physics, so the paper's significance is conditional on it.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is a single-group result from authors who are among the main developers of the Dark Dimension Scenario, with no independent re-derivation yet. Independent confirmation would mean someone outside the program redoing the formation bounds with the numerical factors tracked, and ideally simulating horizon-scale collapse on a brane in a five-dimensional spacetime, which nobody has done here.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

### Origins & Big Picture: where primordial black holes and the dark dimension come from

**Primordial black holes.** In 1967 Zel'dovich and Novikov noticed that dense regions of the early universe might collapse directly into black holes, and in 1971 Hawking worked out that such objects could be far lighter than any star. The mechanism is simple. At any moment in the early universe there is a largest region that has had time to communicate with itself, the *horizon*. If a horizon-sized patch happens to be denser than its surroundings by a large enough fraction (tens of percent), its own gravity wins against the outward push of the hot plasma's pressure, and it collapses. The resulting black hole has roughly the mass contained in the horizon at that moment. The earlier the moment, the hotter the universe and the smaller the horizon, so very early black holes are very light. Black holes formed when the plasma was at a few GeV temperature (about a microsecond after the Big Bang) come out near a solar mass; ones formed at trillions of electron-volts are asteroid-sized or smaller.

The hard part is making the dense patches. The ordinary ripples left by inflation are a hundred-thousand times too weak, so something special is needed. The three standard candidates are: (1) inflation with a specially shaped potential that boosts ripples at small scales; (2) a phase transition in the plasma, during which pressure briefly drops so collapse becomes easier, like a building that loses its supports for a moment; and (3) cosmic strings, thin threads of trapped field energy left over from a symmetry breaking, whose loops can wiggle down to a size smaller than their own horizon and become black holes. In Hawking's 1971 picture a black hole also slowly evaporates by emitting thermal radiation, faster the lighter it is, so very light primordial black holes would already be gone and only those above about a hundred-billion-kilogram scale survive today in ordinary four dimensions.

**The dark dimension.** Extra dimensions have been part of physics since Kaluza and Klein in the 1920s, but they were assumed tiny, near the Planck length. In 1998 it was realized that gravity could leak into extra dimensions as large as a millimeter without contradicting experiments, because all non-gravitational forces could be confined to a brane, and Newton's law had only been tested down to about a millimeter. Tabletop torsion-balance experiments have since pushed that limit down to tens of microns.

The 2022 Dark Dimension proposal (Montero, Vafa and Valenzuela) came from the opposite direction: from principles about quantum gravity. One Swampland conjecture says that when some parameter in a gravitational theory becomes very small, a whole tower of new light particles must appear. The cosmological constant is the most extreme small number we know. Applying the conjecture to it predicts a tower of particles at a mass around a hundredth to a tenth of an electron-volt, and the only version consistent with experiments is one extra dimension of about a micron, with the tower being the "Kaluza–Klein" copies of the graviton (gravitons carrying momentum around the extra dimension, which look like heavy particles to us). A consequence is that the true strength of gravity is set by a five-dimensional Planck scale around a billion GeV, far below the usual four-dimensional Planck scale. Those massive gravitons can also serve as dark matter.

**Where they meet.** Put the two together and you get this paper's question. A black hole is "four-dimensional" only if its horizon is bigger than the extra dimension; that corresponds to a mass of about the asteroid Vesta (roughly a hundred billion billion kilograms). Primordial black holes formed in the hot early universe tend to be lighter than that, so they would be five-dimensional, which is exactly the asteroid-mass window where they could be the dark matter in standard four-dimensional thinking. This paper's job is to check whether *any* standard formation route could beat that size threshold.

### The extra dimension as a thin layer that small things can see

**Concept:** In the Dark Dimension Scenario space has one extra direction curled up with a size of about one to ten microns. Ordinary particles and light are confined to a three-dimensional brane; gravity spreads through all five dimensions. Gravitational physics at distances much larger than the extra dimension looks four-dimensional (normal Newtonian gravity, normal black holes). At distances much smaller than it, gravity behaves five-dimensionally: it weakens faster with distance, and a black hole smaller than the dimension is a compact five-dimensional object, sitting at the brane. The boundary is set by one comparison: horizon size versus extra-dimension size.

**Vehicle:** A thinned silicon die bonded to a heat spreader of fixed thickness. A small hot spot (a single overloaded functional unit) spreads its heat in three dimensions through the spreader's thickness, so its temperature falls off one way with distance. A hot region much wider than the spreader's thickness can only spread heat sideways, effectively in two dimensions, and its temperature profile follows a different law. Which law applies depends only on comparing the hot spot's size to the layer thickness.

**Analogy:** Spreader thickness ↔ size of the extra dimension. Width of the hot spot ↔ size of the black hole's horizon. Heat flow ↔ gravitational field. "Spreads through the thickness" ↔ five-dimensional gravity; "spreads only sideways" ↔ four-dimensional gravity. Same rule in both: small compared to the layer, the full geometry shows; large compared to it, the extra direction is invisible.

**Breaks when:** you ask what the object *is* in each regime. A hot spot is the same hot spot whatever its size; only its surroundings change. A black hole crossing the threshold changes identity: its temperature, its entropy and its evaporation law all change, and the shift happens through a dynamical instability, not by gradual blending. The thermal picture has no slot for "the source itself reorganizes into a different kind of object."

### What the threshold mass is

The paper's whole case is a series of comparisons against one mass: the mass of a black hole whose horizon is as big as the extra dimension. In words, it is the ordinary Planck mass squared divided by the mass of the lightest Kaluza–Klein graviton.

$$M_{\rm GL} \sim \frac{M_{\rm pl}^2}{M_{\rm KK}}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $M_{\rm GL}$ : the threshold mass; black holes lighter than this cannot stay four-dimensional (GL stands for Gregory–Laflamme, explained below)
- $M_{\rm pl}$ : the reduced four-dimensional Planck mass, about $2.4\times10^{18}$ GeV
- $M_{\rm KK}$ : mass of the lightest Kaluza–Klein graviton, one over the size of the extra dimension; about 0.01–0.1 eV

**What this actually means:** a smaller extra dimension means a heavier first Kaluza–Klein mode and a lighter threshold black hole. For a micron-scale dimension the threshold comes out between about ten and a thousand billion billion kilograms, the mass range of a large asteroid, with a horizon of a tenth of a micron to a micron and a half. Everything heavier can be a normal black hole; everything lighter has to be five-dimensional.

### The Gregory–Laflamme instability: why a thin black string breaks into beads

**Analogy:** A thin stream of water from a tap breaks into droplets once it is long compared to its own width; a droplet chain holds the same water in a lower-energy arrangement. In the extra-dimension picture, a "four-dimensional" black hole is really a black *string*: a horizon wrapped all the way around the curled-up direction. In 1993 Gregory and Laflamme showed that when the string's thickness (its horizon radius) is smaller than the length it wraps, it is unstable and pinches off into a localized, roughly spherical five-dimensional black hole sitting at one point of the extra dimension.

**Breaks when:** you ask *why* it breaks. Water breaks up because surface tension lowers the surface area. A black string breaks up because the localized black hole has more entropy (more horizon area for the same mass), so the second law of black hole thermodynamics drives the transition, and there is no surface tension. The paper's footnote notes the timescale is set by the horizon size, so for micron-sized strings the whole pinch-off is effectively instant.

### Normalcy temperature: how hot the visible world can have been

The brane's hot plasma constantly radiates Kaluza–Klein gravitons into the extra dimension. That leak grows extremely steeply with temperature, as the eighth power. Leaked gravitons behave like dark matter, so if the universe had ever been too hot for too long, it would have made far more dark matter than we see. Working backwards from the observed dark matter density gives the **normalcy temperature**, the highest temperature at which the universe can have been an ordinary four-dimensional hot plasma with the extra dimension at its present size. In the Dark Dimension it comes out around 1 GeV, which is when the universe was about a microsecond old.

**Analogy:** Leakage current in a CMOS chip rises steeply with temperature. If you know the total leakage energy the chip has dissipated over its lifetime, you can bound how long it can have run hot; a steep power law means the hottest moments dominate the total.

**Breaks when:** you look at what leaks. Leakage current is just lost energy, but the leaked gravitons stick around as a measurable dark matter component, so the bound is set by what we see today, not by what the system can tolerate.

### Kination: a universe dominated by a rolling field

Before the normalcy temperature, the size of the extra dimension may have been changing. The field that sets that size (the "radion") would then be rolling, and if its kinetic energy dominated the universe's energy budget, the universe was in a **kination** phase. Kinetic energy of a freely rolling field thins out faster than radiation as space expands, so a kination phase ends on its own, after which radiation takes over. During kination the expansion rate is higher than it would be with radiation alone at the same temperature, so the horizon is smaller and holds less mass. An independent observational analysis (Eröncel and collaborators, 2025) found that kination can last at most about ten e-foldings of expansion, which caps how hot the start of such a phase could be at roughly ten thousand times the normalcy temperature, about 10–20 TeV.

**Analogy:** A turbo boost on a processor: a brief phase where the clock (the expansion rate) runs faster than the thermal budget would normally allow, and it decays by itself.

**Breaks when:** you think of kination as fixed by temperature. Turbo is a policy choice; kination's expansion rate depends on how far the field has rolled, which is why the paper can only bound it, not compute it.

### Swampland limits on the other ingredients

Two Swampland-based constraints enter. First, the "trans-Planckian censorship" conjecture argues against the kind of inflationary potential that would make small-scale primordial black holes, so the authors drop inflation as a mechanism. Second, in the Dark Dimension the energy scale of any axion-like field, and therefore of any cosmic string it forms, cannot exceed the five-dimensional Planck scale (about a billion GeV). That second cap is what limits how heavy a cosmic-string black hole can be.

### Evaporation in five dimensions

A black hole radiates like a hot body whose temperature is set by its size: smaller means hotter. For the same mass, a five-dimensional black hole is *larger* than its four-dimensional counterpart (gravity spreads into more directions, so the horizon sits further out), so it is colder and evaporates more slowly. In four dimensions lifetime scales as mass cubed; in five dimensions it scales as mass squared, divided by the cube of the five-dimensional Planck scale.

$$\tau \sim \frac{M^2}{M_{5D,\rm pl}^3}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\tau$ : evaporation lifetime of a five-dimensional black hole
- $M$ : black hole mass
- $M_{5D,\rm pl}$ : five-dimensional Planck scale, about $10^{9}$ GeV in this scenario, fixed by $M_{5D,\rm pl}^3 \sim M_{\rm pl}^2 M_{\rm KK}$

**What this actually means:** a five-dimensional black hole of a billion kilograms lives about ten trillion years, while a four-dimensional one of the same mass would be gone in about a century. The prefactor depends on how many particle species the black hole can emit, so many emission channels shorten the lifetime.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

The paper's logic is a chain of bounds. Here is the flow before the steps.

```

        |
        v

   +--> inflation: disfavored by Swampland (TCC)  -- dropped

```

*How to read it:* each arrow is an inequality, and the conclusion holds because every branch ends below the threshold mass.

### Step 1 — What the universe was doing before the first microsecond

**The failure it prevents:** most primordial black hole formation happens at temperatures far above the normalcy temperature, where the usual four-dimensional history is not guaranteed, so you cannot simply assume ordinary radiation-dominated expansion there.

**What the authors do:** They argue that before the normalcy temperature the extra dimension must have been *smaller* than today; otherwise the hot brane would have leaked far too many gravitons. They then use two bounds on how the Kaluza–Klein mass could have changed between some early temperature and the normalcy temperature. The first is a speed limit: a field rolling as fast as Hubble friction allows (pure kination) can change the extra dimension's size only so much in a given amount of expansion. The second is the dark-matter budget: if the universe was radiation-dominated, the brane leaks gravitons at a rate that sets a *minimum* Kaluza–Klein mass at each earlier temperature. The two bounds come out with the same temperature dependence (both grow as the cube of temperature), and the dark-matter one sits on the wrong side of the speed limit unless the expansion was kination-like.

**What it buys:** the conclusion that if the brane was hot and in equilibrium before the normalcy temperature, the energy budget must have been dominated by the rolling radion (kination), not by Standard Model radiation. Combined with the ten-e-fold cap on kination, that phase started no earlier than roughly 10–20 TeV. For black holes, kination matters because it speeds up expansion, shrinks the horizon and lowers the horizon mass at any given temperature.

### Step 2 — Black holes from phase transitions

**The failure it prevents:** without a mass comparison you cannot say whether a phase-transition black hole would be four- or five-dimensional.

**What the authors do:** A phase-transition black hole weighs about one horizon mass. Even in the most generous case (radiation alone setting the expansion rate, which is the slowest the universe can expand at a given temperature) the horizon mass falls as the inverse square of temperature. Comparing to the threshold, black holes formed above roughly 5–15 TeV are below it, depending on the dimension's exact size. If the pre-normalcy universe was in kination (Step 1), the horizon is smaller still, the mass falls as the inverse cube of temperature, and the crossover drops to a few hundred GeV, roughly the TeV scale. So a four-dimensional black hole requires a phase transition below about a TeV that produces strong enough overdensities. In the Standard Model the two candidates, the electroweak transition and QCD confinement, are both smooth crossovers rather than sharp first-order transitions. The authors note that during a sharp first-order transition the plasma's sound speed drops to zero and pressure stops resisting collapse; a crossover softens pressure only partly, so it is much less efficient.

**What it buys:** phase-transition black holes are five-dimensional unless new physics produces a first-order transition below about a TeV. Those formed above the crossover may start as four-dimensional black strings that are already unstable, then bead up into five-dimensional black holes via the Gregory–Laflamme instability.

### Step 3 — Black holes from collapsing cosmic strings

**The failure it prevents:** cosmic-string black holes form by a different route (a closed loop of string shrinking inside its own horizon), so the horizon-mass argument does not automatically cover them.

**What the authors do:** A string loop's black hole cannot weigh more than the string's energy per unit length times the horizon size. The energy per length is set by the axion decay constant squared, and in the Dark Dimension that constant is capped by the five-dimensional Planck scale. Before the normalcy temperature that scale was even lower (the extra dimension was smaller), and the authors show that the temperature dependence cancels: the maximum cosmic-string black hole mass is a fixed number, roughly a billion kilograms. That is about a hundred billion times below the threshold. For such black holes to be four-dimensional, the extra dimension would have to be a millionth of a micron across, about a picometer, rather than a micron. Strings formed *after* the normalcy temperature fare no better: they would need the symmetry to break at a few keV or below, which would make the associated axion-like particles couple to photons and gluons so strongly that laboratory and astrophysical searches would already have seen them.

**What it buys:** the paper's cleanest result. Cosmic-string black holes are five-dimensional from birth, at any formation time, with a large margin.

### Step 4 — How long the five-dimensional black holes live

**The failure it prevents:** a five-dimensional population could have evaporated long ago or could be stable forever; the two cases have opposite observational consequences.

**What the authors do:** They plug the maximum masses from Steps 2–3 into the five-dimensional lifetime formula. The heaviest cosmic-string black holes live about ten trillion years, roughly a thousand times the present age of the universe. Because emission rate scales with the number of particle species the black hole can radiate, a scenario with hundreds to thousands of five-dimensional species would bring that down to about the current age. Phase-transition black holes formed above the normalcy temperature would only have evaporated by now if they formed above about ten million GeV, far hotter than the kination window allows and only a hundred times below the five-dimensional Planck scale, so the authors conclude most of them outlive the universe.

**What it buys:** a specific scenario for the KM3NeT event, the single neutrino of about 220 PeV (2×10^8 GeV) detected off Sicily, which is intriguingly close to the five-dimensional Planck scale. A five-dimensional black hole in its final explosion could emit such a neutrino, either directly from the brane or by emitting a bulk particle that converts into a neutrino. Its selling point is that no high-energy photons arrived from the same direction. The authors admit two problems raised by others: in the brane-emission case, quarks and gluons would still produce neutral pions and therefore gamma rays, and neutrino telescopes should have seen extra lower-energy neutrinos in the hours before. They point to a separate mechanism (neutrino oscillations taking "shortcuts" through the extra dimension) that might make a narrow line and suppress the lower-energy events.

### Claim check

Done in Python (`pbh5d_claimcheck.py`), using a reduced Planck mass of 2.4×10^18 GeV, a present-day Hubble rate of 67 km/s/Mpc, and the paper's range of Kaluza–Klein masses (0.01–0.1 eV).

**Normalcy temperature — Reproduced.** The paper's formula gives 0.4–0.8 GeV across the stated range, matching "about a GeV." The five-dimensional Planck scale comes out at 0.4–0.8 billion GeV, matching the quoted 10^9 GeV and supporting the KM3NeT coincidence at the factor-of-a-few level.

**Phase-transition crossover — Reproduced.** The threshold mass is 10^20–10^21 kg. Horizon mass equals it at 5–16 TeV under radiation domination (the paper says "above about 10 TeV") and at 300–600 GeV under kination (the paper says "TeV"). The phase-transition conclusion holds.

**Cosmic-string mass gap — Reproduced, with margin.** The maximum string black hole mass comes out at a hundred million to a few billion kilograms (the paper rounds a prefactor of about six down to one), between a hundredth of a billionth and a few hundredths of a billionth of the threshold. The "dimension would need to be a millionth of a micron" figure is reproduced exactly, and the late-formation condition comes out at roughly 1–5 keV against the paper's "10 keV or below." Rounding factors cannot touch a gap of eleven orders of magnitude.

**Cosmic-string lifetime — Partly reproduced.** The ten-trillion-year figure comes out at one to a hundred trillion years depending on that rounding and the dimension size. It is still well above the current age, so "a few orders of magnitude longer than the universe" is right, but how many species you would need to make the lifetime match the KM3NeT idea is uncertain by about a factor of a hundred.

**Significant finding: the phase-transition lifetime equation has the wrong number.** The paper writes the lifetime as 10^49 Planck times scaled by the inverse sixth power of formation temperature in GeV. The paper's own formula, evaluated with its own inputs, gives about 10^102 Planck times. That number reproduces the paper's stated conclusion (that only black holes formed above about ten million GeV have evaporated by now). If you plugged in the printed 10^49, you would conclude instead that anything formed above about 0.01 GeV, which is every phase-transition black hole in the paper, has already evaporated, which is the opposite of the paper's conclusion. So the text's conclusion is correct and the printed coefficient is a typo, off by about 53 orders of magnitude. It matters because a reader would use that equation directly.

**Minor (one line):** The expression for the dark matter density at recombination is missing a square root (as printed it has the wrong units; the next equation, for the normalcy temperature, is correct), and the sentence placing the start of kination "above about 10 TeV" should say "below," as the paper's own later sections do.

**Sensitivity:** The single input that matters most is the extra dimension's size. The cosmic-string conclusion survives unless the dimension is about a million times smaller than proposed, which would no longer be the Dark Dimension. The phase-transition conclusion is more fragile: it survives unless some first-order transition happens below roughly 0.3–15 TeV, with the range depending on whether kination is assumed. The Step 1 kination argument is the most delicate. Its two competing bounds have *identical* temperature dependence, so the "contradiction" rests entirely on order-one-to-ten numerical prefactors in an order-of-magnitude calculation. Luckily the main black hole conclusion does not depend on it, since the radiation-only bound already gives the ~10 TeV crossover.

**Robustness (theoretical: dimensional analysis and limiting cases):** Every displayed relation was checked for consistent units. All pass except the dark-matter-density expression noted above. In the limit of a vanishing extra dimension the threshold mass goes to zero and every black hole becomes four-dimensional, as it should; with the dimension pushed toward the experimental upper limit of a few tens of microns, the threshold rises and the conclusions only strengthen.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the paper shows primordial black holes exist, or that they make up dark matter, in the Dark Dimension. The paper actually says nothing about abundance. Its claim is conditional: *if* any form, they are five-dimensional. Whether enough form to matter is untouched.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes every black hole here starts four-dimensional and then breaks up. The paper actually distinguishes the two routes: phase-transition black holes may start as already-unstable black strings and bead up, while cosmic-string ones are born five-dimensional, with nothing to convert.

> **Watch:** Reader likely takes "barring exotic new physics at low energies" to mean "barring something wildly unlikely." The paper actually leans on the Standard Model having no first-order transition below a TeV. A first-order electroweak transition is a common feature of extended-Higgs models motivated by matter–antimatter asymmetry and would be probed by future gravitational-wave detectors, so this loophole is a mainstream research target, not fringe physics.

> **Watch:** Reader likely reads the KM3NeT section as a prediction. The paper actually argues compatibility with an earlier proposal, and making the lifetimes fit the present age needs a hand-chosen number of five-dimensional species; it does not compute an expected event rate.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**The cosmic-string mass ceiling.** The neatest step combines two separate limits: the Swampland cap on axion energy scales (tied to the five-dimensional Planck scale) and the horizon-size limit on string loops. Before the normalcy temperature the extra dimension was smaller, which tightens the cap in a way that exactly cancels the temperature dependence of the horizon. The result is a single, formation-time-independent maximum mass for cosmic-string black holes that sits eleven orders of magnitude below the four-dimensional threshold. That is new to the field, and it is robust because the gap is so large.

**Carrying the dark-matter budget backwards in time.** The normalcy temperature was already known. What is new is running the same graviton-leakage budget to earlier times, while letting the extra dimension's size change, and concluding that a hot brane before the first microsecond requires a kination-dominated expansion. That reframes the pre-normalcy era as a regime where the horizon mass is smaller than usual, which strengthens every black hole bound. It is a clever use of an existing constraint; it is more fragile than the cosmic-string result, as the Claim check shows.

New to the reader versus new to the field: the Gregory–Laflamme instability, five-dimensional evaporation and the KM3NeT link are all from earlier work (the last from Anchordoqui, Halzen and Lüst, 2025). The combination into a "no four-dimensional primordial black holes" statement is the paper's contribution.

### Predictive Content Check

**Falsifiable handle:** The sharpest one is negative. In the Dark Dimension with Standard Model physics, the paper implies **no four-dimensional primordial black holes**, and in particular none heavier than the threshold of about 10^20–10^21 kg formed after the normalcy temperature. Black holes formed at GeV-to-TeV temperatures would be planetary-to-solar mass, so a confirmed primordial population in that range (for example a sub-solar-mass merger seen by LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA, which no star can make) would force either a new sub-TeV first-order phase transition or a break with the scenario. A second handle is evaporation: five-dimensional black holes ending now would emit ultra-high-energy neutrinos with suppressed gamma rays, observable by KM3NeT and IceCube; repeated neutrino events with no photons and no lower-energy precursors would support the picture, while gamma-ray counterparts would weaken the bulk-emission version. The paper does not supply a rate for either, so these are directions of exposure, not numerical predictions. The underlying framework is itself testable: tabletop gravity experiments probing deviations from Newton's law near a micron could find or exclude the extra dimension.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**Everything is conditional on the Dark Dimension Scenario.** If there is no micron-sized extra dimension, none of this applies. **(B) Contested** — the scenario is a live proposal with active followers and active critics; its Swampland inputs are conjectures, not theorems. **(broader literature)**

**The phase-transition result leans on "no first-order transition below a TeV."** Many beyond-Standard-Model models make the electroweak transition first-order, and work on the QCD epoch (for example Byrnes and collaborators, 2018) finds that even the QCD crossover's softening of pressure noticeably boosts formation of solar-mass black holes. The paper dismisses the crossover in one paragraph. **(B) Contested** — the paper concedes reduced pressure during QCD but judges it inefficient; others treat the same softening as significant. **(paper §III; broader literature)**

**Inflation is excluded on Swampland grounds.** The trans-Planckian censorship conjecture that disfavors inflationary black hole production is itself contested. **(B) Contested** — the conjecture is cited by its own authors and disputed by others; it is not an observational exclusion. **(paper §I; broader literature)**

**The kination argument rests on order-of-magnitude prefactors.** The two bounds in Step 1 scale identically with temperature, so the claimed contradiction depends on order-one-to-ten factors, including an exponent coefficient the authors take from a separate paper. **(C) Speculative** — this is my reading from the recomputation; a careful treatment with exact coefficients could leave a narrow allowed radiation-dominated window. **(analyst inference)**

**The era before kination is left unspecified.** If radiation domination before the normalcy temperature overproduces gravitons, and kination can only extend back to about 10–20 TeV, the paper does not say what the universe was doing at higher temperatures (possibly not yet hot at all, i.e. a low reheating temperature). The black hole bounds survive because they only use a lower limit on the expansion rate, but the cosmological history is incomplete. **(C) Speculative** — the paper's conclusions mention an alternative (no kination at all, with radiation appearing only near the normalcy temperature), but neither path is worked out. **(analyst inference; paper §VI)**

**Printed coefficient error in the phase-transition lifetime.** The prefactor is off by about 53 orders of magnitude, although the text's conclusion matches the corrected value. **(A) Consensus** — anyone redoing the arithmetic from the paper's own formula and inputs gets the same discrepancy. **(analyst inference — from recomputing the paper's Eq. 22 using its Eqs. 11 and 20)**

**Collapse on a brane in five dimensions is not modeled.** All statements are scaling estimates. Nobody has simulated horizon-size collapse of a brane-confined plasma during kination, including the collapse threshold, how mass divides between brane and bulk, and how the black string beads up. **(C) Speculative** — the estimates are probably right at the order-of-magnitude level, but formation efficiency could differ substantially from four-dimensional intuition. **(analyst inference)**

**The KM3NeT connection is a stretch.** It needs a tuned number of five-dimensional species, and its brane-emission version still predicts gamma rays from pion decay and a lower-energy neutrino precursor that was not seen. The paper acknowledges both and points to a separate oscillation mechanism. **(B) Contested** — Airoldi and collaborators (2025) raise these problems; the authors argue the conclusions of their earlier proposal still stand. **(paper §V)**

**Follow-up in the next 12–24 months:** an independent re-derivation with exact numerical factors; recomputing the asteroid-mass dark matter window constraints for five-dimensional black holes born with the mass spectra implied here; a quantitative KM3NeT/IceCube event rate for a five-dimensional evaporating population; and tighter short-range gravity tests near a micron.

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

The paper asks a simple question in an unusual setting. Suppose the Dark Dimension Scenario is right: space has one extra dimension about a micron across, ordinary matter is stuck on a three-dimensional brane, and gravity's true strength is set by a five-dimensional Planck scale around a billion GeV. Then would black holes formed in the early universe be ordinary four-dimensional ones or five-dimensional ones?

The answer depends on one comparison: horizon size versus the extra dimension's size. A black hole heavier than about 10^20–10^21 kg (a large asteroid) has a horizon bigger than the dimension and can be ordinary. A lighter one is either born five-dimensional or starts as a four-dimensional "black string" that is unstable to beading up (the Gregory–Laflamme instability) and becomes five-dimensional almost instantly.

First the authors pin down the early history. The brane's hot plasma leaks Kaluza–Klein gravitons into the extra dimension at a rate that rises with the eighth power of temperature, and those gravitons act as dark matter. That already caps the ordinary hot era to temperatures below about 1 GeV (the normalcy temperature, about a microsecond after the Big Bang). Pushing the same accounting earlier, the authors argue that if the brane was hot before then, the extra dimension had to be smaller and the expansion had to be driven by the rolling field that controls the dimension's size (a kination era), not by radiation. An independent observational cap on kination then limits that era to starting below roughly 10–20 TeV.

Then they check the formation routes. Inflation is set aside because the needed potentials conflict with Swampland conjectures. Phase-transition black holes weigh about one horizon mass; above roughly 10 TeV that mass is below the threshold even in the most generous case, and with kination the crossover drops to about a TeV. Making four-dimensional black holes would therefore require a first-order phase transition below a TeV, which the Standard Model does not have (both its electroweak and QCD transitions are smooth crossovers). Cosmic-string black holes are the cleanest case: the Swampland caps the strings' energy scale at the five-dimensional Planck scale, and that gives a maximum black hole mass of about a billion kilograms, independent of formation time and a hundred billion times below the threshold. Strings forming late would need axion-like particles so strongly coupled to light that experiments would already have found them.

Finally, five-dimensional black holes evaporate more slowly than four-dimensional ones of the same mass. The heaviest cosmic-string ones live around ten trillion years, which could be brought down to the current age of the universe if there are many particle species in the extra dimension. That makes them candidates for the KM3NeT ultra-high-energy neutrino, whose energy is near the five-dimensional Planck scale and which arrived without a matching gamma-ray signal. Phase-transition black holes mostly outlive the universe.

**How to read this summary.** I framed the paper around the threshold mass because every step is a comparison against it; the paper does the same thing, just in equations. The key interpretive choice is to rank the paper's arguments by robustness. The cosmic-string result is a huge-margin bound and is the solid core. The phase-transition result is solid only given the Standard Model's lack of a sub-TeV first-order transition, and that is a real, actively studied loophole. The kination claim is the most delicate step because it rests on numerical prefactors, but the black hole conclusion does not need it. The evaporation and KM3NeT section is the most speculative part. One printed coefficient (the phase-transition lifetime) is wrong by many orders of magnitude, but the text's conclusion matches the corrected number. What the reader should take away is that, *within* this framework, "primordial black holes are five-dimensional" is a well-supported default, and every constraint on such black holes in the framework should be computed for five-dimensional objects.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the cosmic-string section's two Swampland inputs (that a cosmic string's energy scale is set by an axion decay constant capped at the five-dimensional Planck scale, and that the cap tracks the shrinking dimension before the normalcy temperature). I reproduced the arithmetic from those inputs but could not independently assess the underlying conjectures, which come from Gendler and Vafa's work, and the paper's strongest result rests entirely on them. I am also relying on the authors' inherited graviton-leakage rate and treating the brane-collapse physics at the same order-of-magnitude level they do.

**Revision notes:** the referee pass added the low-energy first-order-transition loophole as an Assumption Audit item, made the minor dark-matter-density and kination-wording slips a single line, tied the kination fragility to the identical temperature scaling, and added the sub-solar-mass merger falsifiable handle.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **Size decides the dimension.** In a universe with a micron-sized extra dimension, a black hole lighter than a large asteroid is a five-dimensional object, and almost every black hole the early universe could make falls below that line.

2. **Cosmic-string black holes can't escape it.** Quantum-gravity limits cap them at about a billion kilograms, a hundred billion times too light to be ordinary, whenever they form.

3. **Five-dimensional black holes die slowly, and maybe loudly in neutrinos.** They live trillions of years instead of about a century, and one exploding now might explain the record-energy KM3NeT neutrino that came without a gamma-ray flash. That link is a possibility, not a prediction.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Some theories propose that space has one hidden extra dimension about a micron across. In the version called the Dark Dimension Scenario, ordinary matter and light are confined to our familiar three dimensions, but gravity spreads into the fourth spatial direction. In that world, black holes come in two kinds. Ones bigger than the extra dimension (heavier than a large asteroid) behave normally. Smaller ones are genuinely five-dimensional objects, with different sizes, temperatures and lifetimes.

Anchordoqui, Bedroya and Lüst ask which kind the early universe would make. Primordial black holes form when a dense patch of the early plasma collapses, and they weigh about as much as the region that can communicate with itself at that moment, which was tiny when the universe was very hot. The authors check the standard ways of making these black holes against limits from quantum gravity. Black holes from early-universe phase transitions come out too light to be ordinary unless a sharp transition happened at energies below about a TeV, which the Standard Model lacks. Black holes from collapsing loops of cosmic string are capped at about a billion kilograms, a hundred billion times too light, whenever they form. The conclusion is that, barring new low-energy physics, every primordial black hole in this scenario is five-dimensional.

Five-dimensional black holes evaporate much more slowly than ordinary ones of the same mass: trillions of years rather than about a century. The heaviest cosmic-string ones could be finishing their lives about now, which the authors connect to an ultra-high-energy neutrino seen by the KM3NeT detector with no accompanying gamma rays.

The cosmic-string result is the paper's strongest point. The phase-transition result depends on the Standard Model having no sharp low-energy transition, which is an active research question. The neutrino link is speculative, and one printed lifetime formula contains a large typo, although the text's conclusion is right. Everything depends on the Dark Dimension being real, which experiments probing gravity near a micron can eventually test.
