# Two Rulers for One Invisible Voltage — and What Their Disagreement Gives Away

*Brindley, N. & Whiter, D. (2026). Identifying Alfvénic modulation of quasi-static potential structures above aurora by optical methods. JGR Space Physics 131, e2026JA035658. DOI 10.1029/2026JA035658*

**Hook:** A pair of ground cameras on Svalbard, measuring nothing but light, can estimate the voltage structure thousands of kilometres overhead two independent ways. Where those two estimates *agree*, the classic static picture of the aurora holds; where they *disagree* — on one side of the arc, flickering about once per second — the paper argues you are watching an Alfvén wave hitching a ride on the accelerator.

---

## Section 2 — Big-Picture Context

The bright, sharp curtains of discrete aurora are made by electrons that arrive at the upper atmosphere with a few thousand electron-volts of energy — far more than they had while drifting in the distant magnetosphere. Something between roughly 1,000 and 13,000 km up accelerates them along the magnetic field. Fifty years of rocket and satellite work have converged on two candidate accelerators that often coexist. The first is a **quasi-static potential drop**: a voltage difference of a few kilovolts held along the field lines for seconds to minutes, typically shaped like a "U" when seen edge-on. The second is the **Alfvén wave**: a travelling electromagnetic disturbance along the field lines which, when it is narrow enough, carries its own small electric field along the field and can kick electrons in short, flickering bursts. Large, steady arcs are textbook quasi-static; fine-scale, fast-moving, flickering aurora is textbook Alfvénic. The interesting territory is the overlap, where both are present at once and nobody can easily tell how much each contributes.

The core observational difficulty is geometric. A spacecraft that measures the energy of falling electrons has to be *below* the accelerator, but the sideways electric fields that define the U-shape exist only *above* it. No single spacecraft sees both at once, and a satellite crossing a 5 km-wide arc at several km/s spends only a second or two inside it. Missions such as Cluster pieced together the structure's evolution over minutes from fortunate multi-spacecraft crossings; the Japanese Reimei satellite paired an auroral imager with particle instruments and recently showed (Hirahara et al. 2024; Chaston 2026) that the poleward edge of an arc can host Alfvénic intensifications riding on top of an inverted-V accelerator. But spacecraft snapshots cannot follow one arc continuously for tens of seconds.

Brindley and Whiter's move is to stay on the ground and stare. The Auroral Structure and Kinetics (ASK) instrument images an 11.5 km square patch of sky at 20 frames per second in narrow color bands, with about 45 m resolution. From these images they build two *independent* estimates of the sideways electric field above the arc: one from how the auroral structures move, and one from how the electron energy varies across the arc. If a pure U-shaped potential is doing the work, both rulers must read the same field. The paper's contribution is to compare them frame by frame over a 25-second case study, find where they disagree, and argue — with a deliberately crude toy model — that the disagreement is the fingerprint of Alfvénic acceleration superimposed on the quasi-static structure.

**Paper type & stakes:** This is an observational methods paper with a single 25-second case study and a toy-model interpretation; what's at stake is whether a cheap, continuous, ground-only diagnostic can separate quasi-static from Alfvénic acceleration in time, something that otherwise requires rare spacecraft conjunctions.

**Prior Belief Check:** The headline finding — counter-flowing arcs are broadly consistent with a U-shaped potential — confirms what the community already believes (Hallinan 1981, Vogt et al. 1999, Chaston 2024 and 2026). It is confirmatory, not surprising. The more interesting secondary claim — that Alfvén waves modulate the structure on its poleward edge at around 1 Hz — is also consistent with a growing consensus that Alfvénic and quasi-static acceleration coexist at arc boundaries, and it parallels Reimei results closely. What is genuinely new is the *method* (two optical rulers cross-checked against each other) and the specific single-wave, two-altitude interpretation of a faint "neighbor" arc. Experts would not be startled by any conclusion here; they would scrutinize whether the optical-flow ruler is truly independent and whether the toy model constrains anything.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is a single group, single event (25 seconds of one arc on 28 February 2017), with no independent replication of the method; convergence comes only indirectly, from Reimei spacecraft observations of a qualitatively similar poleward-edge phenomenon. Independent confirmation would look like the same two-ruler disagreement recurring in other ASK events, ideally with a satellite passing through the same field line to directly detect Alfvénic, low-energy-origin electrons at the moments the disagreement spikes.

## Section 3 — Necessary Background Crash-Course

### Origins & Big Picture: how an auroral arc gets built

An auroral arc is the visible end of an electric circuit the size of a planet. The solar wind streaming past Earth's magnetic field drags on it and drives large-scale circulation of plasma inside the magnetosphere — the bubble of space Earth's field controls. That circulation acts like a generator: it pushes charge across magnetic field lines and builds up electric fields and pressure differences in the distant tail, many Earth radii away. The magnetic field lines that thread that region dip down into the high-latitude atmosphere, near the poles, and they behave like very good wires for current flowing *along* them. So the generator out in space closes its circuit by driving currents down one bundle of field lines, sideways through the conducting upper atmosphere (the ionosphere, around 100–150 km up), and back out along another bundle. These "field-aligned currents" link the magnetosphere and ionosphere into one system.

Here is the bottleneck. Current flowing *up* out of the ionosphere is carried mainly by electrons flowing *down*. Those electrons originate in the thin, hot plasma sheet far out in the tail, and there aren't many of them. Worse, as they stream earthward the field gets stronger, the field lines converge, and the "magnetic mirror" effect bounces most of them back before they arrive. The circuit wants more current than the supply of naturally falling electrons can deliver. Nature's fix is to build a voltage along the field line — typically a few kilovolts, sitting somewhere between about 1,000 and 13,000 km altitude — that accelerates electrons downward hard enough to punch through the mirror and supply the required current. This relationship between how much current is demanded and how much voltage must form is called the Knight relation; it is the basic reason discrete arcs exist at all. The accelerated electrons, now carrying a few keV, plunge into the atmosphere at around 100–120 km and excite oxygen and nitrogen, which glow.

The voltage structure isn't a flat slab. Because the parallel voltage only exists in a limited band of field lines, the equipotential surfaces close off above the ionosphere in a U-shape (picture drawn below). That shape has a direct consequence: above the accelerator, the electric field points sideways, *inward*, toward the arc's center from both sides. In a strongly magnetized plasma a sideways electric field makes the plasma drift sideways, at right angles to the field. Inward fields on the two sides of the arc therefore drive drifts in *opposite* directions along the arc — the poleward side going one way, the equatorward side the other. Because the auroral structures are threaded onto those drifting field lines, the light itself appears to stream in counter-flowing lanes. That counter-flow, visible from the ground, is the optical signature of a U-shaped accelerator.

Alfvén waves enter as the circuit's way of *changing*. Whenever the current system is switched on, modified, or disturbed, the news travels along the field lines as an Alfvén wave — the magnetic equivalent of a pulse running down a cable. The region between the ionosphere and a few thousand kilometres up acts as a resonant cavity (the "ionospheric Alfvén resonator") that traps and rings these waves at roughly 0.1–1 Hz. When the waves are very narrow across the field — a few kilometres or less — electron inertia lets them carry an electric field *along* the field and accelerate electrons in bursts. That's what makes fine-scale flickering aurora.

Where does this paper's arc sit? It was observed on the dawn side, during the recovery phase of a substorm, when the system is relaxing after a large energy release and boundaries are still dynamic. Arcs with clear counter-flow are uncommon in the ASK archive (about 5% of observations), and only around a tenth of those are bright and structured enough for motion tracking — so the method applies to a narrow but physically rich class of arcs where steady and wave-driven acceleration plausibly overlap.

**Analogy:** Think of the auroral arc as a voltage regulator added to a long power-delivery line that can't carry the current the load demands: the regulator (the parallel voltage) forms precisely where supply falls short, and it pulls up current until demand is met.
**Breaks when:** you ask where the regulator sits. A board regulator is a fixed component placed by a designer; the auroral accelerator forms, moves, and re-forms on its own, at altitudes set by the plasma itself, and it can be modulated in seconds by waves arriving along the same "wire."

### Reading voltage from drift speed

In a magnetized plasma that isn't being accelerated along the field, electrons and ions are "frozen" onto field lines, and any sideways electric field makes everything drift together at a speed equal to the field strength divided by the magnetic field strength. Turn that around and a drift speed *is* an electric field measurement: multiply by the (known) magnetic field. At the 100 km reference altitude the field is about 54,000 nT, so a 10 km/s drift corresponds to about half a volt per metre.

**Analogy:** A current-sense shunt resistor: measure the voltage across a known resistance and you know the current. Here, drift speed is the reading, the magnetic field strength is the known shunt, and the electric field is what you infer.
**Breaks when:** the plasma isn't frozen in. In the acceleration region itself — where there is a parallel electric field — the freezing fails, so the "shunt" only works above the accelerator, and the measured motion has to be projected along field lines from there down to the ionosphere where the light is emitted.

### The U-shaped potential and why two paths must agree

> **Concept:** In a purely electrostatic structure the voltage between two points doesn't depend on the path taken between them. For the U-shape, take one point at the top edge of the structure and one at the bottom of the arc's center. Path one goes sideways across the top, through the inward-pointing sideways field, to the center line, then straight down. Path two goes straight down the edge field line (no parallel drop there) and across at the bottom (where the sideways field is negligible). Both must give the same total. The practical consequence: the sideways voltage across the arc, measured high up, equals the parallel voltage drop at the center — which is the energy the electrons arrive with. This holds only while the magnetic field isn't changing in time; a time-varying magnetic field (such as an Alfvén wave) adds an induced voltage around the loop and breaks the equality.

> **Vehicle:** Kirchhoff's voltage law on a circuit board: the sum of voltage drops around any closed loop is zero, so two different paths between the same two nodes show the same voltage. Probe one path with one meter and the other path with a second meter, and they agree — unless a changing magnetic flux threads the loop (a nearby switching inductor, say), in which case an induced EMF appears and the readings differ.

**Analogy:** Sideways voltage across the top of the U ↔ meter on path one. Electron arrival energy at the center ↔ meter on path two. "No changing magnetic field" ↔ "no flux through the loop." An Alfvén wave ↔ the switching inductor whose changing flux makes two otherwise-equivalent paths disagree.

**Breaks when:** you treat the disagreement as purely inductive. On a board, a mismatch has one cause — induced EMF. Here the paper's mismatch could also come from electrons that were *never in the loop*: cold ionospheric electrons accelerated by the wave without passing through the static drop, which have no counterpart in a circuit, where every carrier traverses the same network.

### Auroral color as an energy meter

Faster electrons penetrate deeper into the atmosphere before stopping. Different emissions favor different depths and excitation pathways: the oxygen line at 777.4 nm is relatively stronger for soft (low-energy) precipitation, while the nitrogen band imaged at 673 nm is relatively stronger for harder precipitation. So the *ratio* of the two brightnesses tells you the characteristic electron energy, once you calibrate it against a physics model of electrons streaming into a model atmosphere (here the Southampton Ionospheric Model). Do that pixel by pixel and you get a map of electron energy across the arc, every frame. Under the U-shape logic above, that energy map *is* a map of the parallel voltage drop, and its sideways slope is the sideways electric field.

**Analogy:** Estimating a program's working-set size from two performance counters — one that dominates when data fits in a near cache, another that dominates when it spills to DRAM. Neither counter alone tells you the size; their ratio, read off a calibration curve measured on a known benchmark, does.
**Breaks when:** the "workload" isn't one size. The calibration assumes a single characteristic energy (a Maxwellian spectrum); a real arc with a mix of populations — for example, a few-keV beam plus a burst of wave-accelerated electrons — gets compressed into one number that may represent neither.

### Optical flow: motion vectors from video

Optical flow estimates, for each pixel, how far and in what direction its brightness moved between frames. The version used here (a total-variation method) allows sharp jumps in the motion field — essential for counter-flowing lanes that reverse direction across a narrow shear line.

**Analogy:** Motion vectors in a video codec: the encoder finds where each block moved so it can predict the next frame cheaply.
**Breaks when:** you treat the vectors as truth. An encoder only needs a *good reconstruction*, not the true motion, and so does this algorithm — the authors found runs where flow speeds were 25–50% lower reconstructed the images about as well. Aurora also isn't a rigid object; its brightness changes as structures form and fade, which the method's core assumption (brightness is conserved as it moves) forbids.

### Narrow Alfvén waves and the "bounce" acceleration

An Alfvén wave on a field line is a travelling twist of the magnetic field carrying an electric field. When its width across the field shrinks to a few kilometres (comparable to the distance electrons can respond to before their own inertia matters), it acquires a small electric field *along* the field — a moving voltage step travelling down toward the ionosphere at thousands to tens of thousands of km/s. An electron that meets this moving step can be reflected off it, like a ball off an advancing paddle, and leaves with up to twice the wave's speed. Concrete example: if the wave's front moves at 16,000 km/s and meets an electron at rest, the electron leaves at 32,000 km/s — which is about 3 keV, exactly the energy of this arc's brightest structure. A wave moving at 12,500 km/s would produce about 1.8 keV.

**Analogy:** A voltage pulse travelling down a PCB trace, reflecting off a mismatched termination at the far end (the ionosphere), with the short segment near the load ringing at a characteristic frequency set by its round-trip time — the resonator's ~1 Hz.
**Breaks when:** you ask the pulse to accelerate anything. A trace pulse barely nudges conduction electrons; the auroral wave can hurl electrons to keV energies because the "medium" is collisionless and the wave's parallel field acts on free particles over thousands of kilometres.

## Section 4 — Core Technical Explanation

### The event

**The failure it prevents:** most auroral video is useless for motion tracking — too faint, too diffuse, or changing too fast. **What the authors do:** they pick 25 seconds of one bright, structured arc observed from Svalbard at about 05 magnetic local time during substorm recovery. The arc runs roughly east-northeast to west-southwest, is 3–6 km wide, and drifts slowly equatorward at about 200 m/s. Its poleward side streams east and its equatorward side streams west, with median speeds near 10 km/s on each side (slightly faster and brighter on the poleward side). Folds travel eastward along the arc; at about 6 s one distorts the shear layer, and from about 13.5 s onward the lanes become less distinct. **What it buys:** a textbook counter-flowing arc — the configuration that should be the cleanest test case for a U-shaped potential — with rich enough structure to follow frame by frame.

### Ruler one: motion to field

**The failure it prevents:** you cannot put a voltmeter thousands of kilometres up. **What the authors do:** they compute optical flow on the 673 nm images, assume the motion is the ground-projected drift of plasma above the accelerator, and convert drift speed to electric field using the magnetic field strength. Then they apply a mathematical split that separates the field into a "pure gradient" part (what an electrostatic voltage can make) and a "swirl" part (what it cannot), and keep only the gradient part, so the comparison is like-for-like. The gradient part carries about six times the energy of the swirl part, consistent with a mostly electrostatic accelerator. **What it buys:** a field estimate that depends only on motion and the frozen-in assumption.

Two modifications matter for judging the result. The flow algorithm is **seeded with an initial guess computed from the energy ruler** (ruler two), and its cost function is altered to **reward shear flow**. The authors argue the seed is downsampled to about 81 × 81 pixels before refinement, so the final 256 × 256 field isn't "overly biased," and that the unmodified algorithm gives comparable reconstruction error but speeds that look about half of what the eye sees. The choice between them was made by visual plausibility. Keep this in mind; it bears on how independent the two rulers really are.

The core relation for this ruler, at the ionospheric reference altitude, is simply:

$$E_{\mathrm{OF}} \approx u\,B$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$E_{\mathrm{OF}}$ : sideways electric field inferred from optical flow (V/m)
$u$ : drift speed of the auroral structure, projected to 100 km (m/s)
$B$ : magnetic field strength at that altitude (tesla, about $5.4\times10^{-5}$)

**What this actually means:** speed times field gives field — a shunt reading converted to current. A 10 km/s lane gives about 0.5 V/m; the fastest quartile (20 km/s) gives about 1 V/m.

### Ruler two: energy to field

**The failure it prevents:** brightness alone doesn't tell you the voltage — a bright arc may be bright because of many electrons, not energetic ones. **What the authors do:** they convert the two-color ratio into a characteristic-energy map for every frame, treat that energy as the parallel voltage drop (valid if the electrons started out cold relative to what they gained), smooth it with an edge-preserving filter, and take its sideways slope. The smoothing strength is tuned until the 99th-percentile field stays below 2.6 V/m — the value you get by projecting the strongest sideways fields ever seen by the Freja spacecraft (about 2 V/m near 1,500 km) down to the ionosphere. **What it buys:** a field estimate that depends only on brightness ratios, the atmosphere model, and the U-shape assumption — not on any motion at all.

$$E_{\mathrm{ASK}} \approx \frac{\Delta(\text{energy per unit charge})}{\Delta x}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$E_{\mathrm{ASK}}$ : sideways electric field inferred from the energy map (V/m)
$\Delta(\text{energy per unit charge})$ : change in characteristic electron energy across neighbouring pixels, expressed in volts
$\Delta x$ : pixel spacing at the emission altitude (about 45 m)

**What this actually means:** by Kirchhoff's loop, the sideways voltage across the top of the U equals the vertical drop at the bottom, so the slope of the energy map *is* the sideways field. Steeper energy walls on the arc's flanks mean stronger inward fields above.

### Making the rulers comparable at any altitude

**The failure it prevents:** both fields live at some unknown altitude above the accelerator, where field lines have spread out and the fields have weakened — you'd seem to need that altitude to compare them. **What the authors do:** they show that because magnetic flux through a flux tube is conserved, both rulers scale by the *same* factor with altitude, so their *ratio* is altitude-independent. Directions differ by at most about four degrees across the plausible altitude range. **What it buys:** a comparison free of the biggest unknown. Uncertainty from the assumed emission height is small for keV electrons and only reaches ten-percent level for around 1 keV; the dominant uncertainty is the flow speed (25–50%).

### Result 1: the voltages broadly agree

The headline test cuts a diagonal line across the arc, finds the peak voltage along it in each ruler, and measures the drop to the poleward and equatorward edges.

![Figure 4](figures/brindley-035658_fig4.png)

*Figure 4 (from the paper): (a, b) voltage drop from the arc's peak to its northwest and southeast edges for the energy ruler (blue/black) and the motion ruler (orange/red); (c) energy along the cut versus time, with the shear line in black; (d) the same, re-centred on the shear line; (e) how strongly the energy just poleward of the shear line fluctuates at each frequency over time.* **What to look at:** in (a) and (b), red tracks black closely at 2–3.5 kV for most of the 25 s — two independent rulers agreeing — except a visible divergence around 12.5–16.5 s; in (e), the hot spots near 1 Hz around 1–4 s and 11–16 s mark rhythmic energy pulsing at the resonator's frequency.

Agreement at the kilovolt level is the paper's main support for the U-shaped picture. The energy keogram (panel c) also shows the energy peak tracking the shear line as the arc drifts equatorward, which is what a U-shape predicts.

### Result 2: the asymmetry on the poleward side

**The failure it prevents:** a single agreement statistic can hide a systematic local mismatch. **What the authors do:** they histogram the ratio and angle between the two field estimates as a function of distance from the shear line, across all frames.

![Figure 5](figures/brindley-035658_fig5.png)

*Figure 5 (from the paper): (a, b) energy-ruler versus motion-ruler field components, with lines of slope 0.5, 1 and 2; (c) angle between the two estimates versus distance north of the shear line; (d, e) the component ratios versus distance.* **What to look at:** in (a, b) the cloud hugs the slope-1 line for fields below about 1 V/m but flattens at large values (the motion ruler reads low at the extremes); in (c) there's a clear lobe of large angles — up to anti-parallel — within about 750 m *north* (poleward) of the shear line but not south of it.

Anti-parallel fields just poleward of the shear line mean the energy peak isn't on the flow-reversal line, as a pure U-shape requires, but displaced a few hundred metres poleward. The authors build an asymmetry index (good agreement south versus north) and find it spikes intermittently — around 3–5 s, 12–14 s, and near 20 s — overlapping the 1 Hz energy pulsations. At two later spikes the energy is lower but the brightness is held up by more electrons (a Knight-relation-style trade between energy and number flux), which is why those spikes show weakly in the frequency plot. Visually, the moderate-energy structures follow the flow, while the highest-energy blobs tend to stall and blink on and off rather than drift — exactly what you'd expect if they're driven by something other than the drifting static structure.

### Result 3: a neighbor arc and a single wave hitting two altitudes

**The failure it prevents:** "Alfvén waves did it" is an easy claim to make and hard to test. **What the authors do:** during 12.8–13.1 s, a faint structure appears just poleward of the main arc, with a distinctive spur shape that mirrors a spur in the main arc's intensification. It is about half the size (arm widths around 1.6 km versus 3.2 km), moves at about 8.5 km/s versus more than double that in the main arc, and has about half the energy (1.5 keV versus 3 keV). The authors argue the size difference isn't viewing geometry (the emission heights, 112 and 107 km, are too close). Their hypothesis: one narrow Alfvén wave, travelling down the field line, accelerated electrons twice — first at a high altitude (making the small neighbor, since structures high up map to smaller footprints) and later at a lower altitude (making the big main structure).

![Figure 7](figures/brindley-035658_fig7.png)

*Figure 7 (from the paper): (a) the line of altitude pairs whose footprint sizes match the observed 2:1 size ratio; (b) predicted arrival times of the neighbor's and main structure's electrons for each pair, with the equal-time solution marked; (c) along the chosen path, the wave's group speed (solid), twice that (dotted), the Alfvén speed (dashed), and the speeds matching each structure's energy (red: main; blue: neighbor); (d, e) energy-flux and energy maps over 12.80–13.10 s, arrows marking the shared spur.* **What to look at:** in (b), the only point where both structures' electrons arrive within one camera frame of each other; in (c), the red and blue crosses sit on the dotted "twice the wave speed" curve — the signature of bounce acceleration.

They run two versions of a toy model, holding the wave's frequency (1 Hz) and cross-field width fixed and propagating it through a standard model plasma:

- **Wave acts below the static drop, on cold ionospheric electrons.** The size constraint plus equal arrival times pick out one altitude pair: about 1.42 Earth radii (around 9,000 km) for the neighbor and 0.53 Earth radii (about 3,400 km) for the main structure, with predicted arrival times of 0.390 and 0.397 s. The electron energies match twice the local wave speed at each altitude, implying bounce acceleration of electrons starting near rest, and a wave voltage of roughly 400–750 V. The flaw: such electrons never passed through the sideways fields above the static drop, so nothing explains why the structures drift with the arc. In this case the static drop's bottom must sit above about 9,000 km.
- **Wave acts above the static drop.** Electrons get a wave kick (about 350 eV for the neighbor, 400–550 eV for the main) and then fall through the static drop to their final energies. With four fitted unknowns (two starting speeds, two boundary altitudes) the same altitude pair survives, and the bottom of the static structure lands at about 0.25 Earth radii (around 1,600 km) under the main structure, consistent with an old estimate of about 1,700 km. This version naturally explains the drift.

The model predicts a final sideways separation of about 1 km between the structures; the observed separation is about 3 km. The authors flag this mismatch and call the model crude.

The bounce relation that ties energies to wave speeds is:

$$v_f = 2\,v_g - v_i$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$v_f$ : electron speed after reflecting off the wave's parallel field (km/s)
$v_g$ : the wave's group speed along the field at that altitude (km/s)
$v_i$ : electron speed before the encounter, in the same direction as the wave (km/s)

**What this actually means:** a ball meeting an advancing paddle leaves at twice the paddle speed minus its own. Because the wave slows as it nears the ionosphere in some regions and speeds up in others, the same wave can stamp different maximum energies at different altitudes — 1.5 keV high up, 3 keV lower down.

### Claim check

Done in Python (`claimcheck.py`), using the paper's quoted inputs and values read off Figure 7.

**Altitude pair from the size ratio — Reproduced.** For a dipole-like field, a 2:1 footprint ratio means a fourfold field-strength ratio between the two interaction sites. Starting from 0.53 Earth radii for the main site, this gives 1.43 Earth radii for the neighbor, matching the paper's 1.42.

**Arrival-time match (below-drop case) — Reproduced.** 1.5 keV electrons from 1.42 Earth radii arrive in 0.39 s, as stated. For the main structure to arrive at the same moment, the wave must take about 0.29 s between sites, an average speed of about 19,000 km/s — inside the range of the plotted group-speed curve, so the timing story is self-consistent.

**Energies equal twice the wave speed, and the wave voltage — Reproduced (within figure read-off).** Group speeds read off Figure 7c give 3.1 keV at the main site and 1.8 keV at the neighbor site for electrons starting at rest, against 3.0 and 1.5 keV observed (the neighbor sits within its error bar). The same speeds imply wave voltages of about 440–770 V, matching the paper's 400–750.

**Kilovolt drop from flow speeds — Partly reproduced.** Median drifts of about 10 km/s across 1.5–3 km half-widths give roughly 0.8–1.6 kV; using upper-quartile speeds pushes this to about 2.5–3 kV. The plotted motion-ruler drops of 2–3.5 kV are therefore plausible only if the faster flows dominate, consistent with the ruler reading low at large fields.

**Sensitivity.** The single most influential input to the altitude results is the size ratio of main to neighbor structure. The authors measure it as 2.0 for the model but describe it as "between 2 and 2.5" in the text. At 2.5 the neighbor altitude moves from about 1.4 to 1.8 Earth radii for the same main altitude — a large shift — and the equal-time solution would move with it. The qualitative story (two altitudes, high and low) survives; the specific altitudes, including the "bottom of the potential ≥1.42 Earth radii" constraint, should be read as roughly ±0.4 Earth radii, not to two decimals.

**Robustness (observational: alternative explanations).** The natural rival to "Alfvén wave" for a poleward-shifted energy peak is a *static* asymmetry — a mixed U/S-shaped potential, which the paper's own introduction notes is common. That alternative predicts a persistent offset; contamination by it would look like a steady asymmetry index. What's observed is intermittent spikes coinciding with near-1 Hz energy pulsing, which favors a time-varying driver. A second rival — brightness-change artifacts in the optical flow — would look like asymmetry tied to brightening rather than to energy; the authors show two spikes where energy drops while brightness holds, which partly guards against it. Neither rival is excluded outright.

**Internal consistency — significant findings.**

*(1) The two rulers aren't fully independent.* The motion ruler is seeded with the energy ruler's field, and the modified algorithm was preferred over the unmodified one on visual plausibility rather than on reconstruction error, which was comparable. The paper presents the two estimates as independent, and "agreement implies a U-shaped potential" rests on that independence. Downsampling the seed limits the bias but does not remove it; the reported agreement is therefore an upper bound on true independent agreement. Interestingly, the opposite holds for the *disagreement*: the poleward anti-parallel lobe appears *despite* a seed that pushes toward agreement, which makes the asymmetry finding more robust than the agreement finding.

*(2) "Comparable" to Reimei is a stretch.* The main interaction altitude (about 3,400 km) is called comparable to Hirahara et al.'s estimate of about 2,200 ± 500 km; the difference is about 2.4 times that quoted uncertainty. It doesn't overturn the interpretation, but the cited agreement with the spacecraft result is looser than the text implies.

Minor: one appendix figure reference points to a non-existent figure; "Aflvén" typo in the summary.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the two electric-field estimates are independent measurements. The paper actually seeds the motion estimate with the energy estimate, and adds a reward for shear flow — so agreement is partly built in, while disagreement survives in spite of it.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "fields of 1–2 V/m at 100 km" are ionospheric fields. The paper actually reports *projected* fields: the high-altitude field mapped down along field lines for comparison; in a U-shape these fields never reach the ionosphere at all.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the toy model shows the neighbor arc *was* made by a single Alfvén wave. The paper actually shows only that a single wave *can* reproduce sizes, arrival times, and energies under fixed-width, single-frequency assumptions — and notes that if the wave narrows as the field lines converge (as many simulations assume), no single wave works and the two-wave Reimei explanation fits better.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "energy" in the maps is a measured spectrum. The paper actually infers a single characteristic energy per pixel from a two-color ratio assuming a Maxwellian spectrum; a mixed population (static beam plus wave-accelerated burst) would be averaged into one number.

## Section 5 — What's Genuinely New or Clever

**The two-ruler loop check as a time-resolved diagnostic.** Others have inferred electric fields from auroral motion (Chaston 2026) and energy from color ratios (Whiter et al. 2010). The new idea is to set them against each other through the Kirchhoff-loop logic, making the *ratio* altitude-independent through flux conservation, and to treat the *pattern of disagreement* — where across the arc and when in time — as the signal. That turns a static-structure validation into a detector for time-varying (inductive or non-static) acceleration, at 20 frames per second, continuously, from the ground. This is new to the field, not just new to the reader.

**One wave, two altitudes.** Explaining a half-size neighbor arc as the same wave's earlier, higher-altitude bounce — using flux-tube mapping to turn a size ratio into an altitude ratio, then requiring simultaneous arrival — is a clever way to extract altitude information from a single camera. It contrasts with Hirahara et al.'s two-path explanation and yields specific, testable altitudes.

A smaller craft point: using the physics (the energy-gradient field) as a prior for optical flow is a sensible engineering choice for a badly posed problem — but it's precisely what weakens ruler independence.

### Predictive Content Check

**Falsifiable handle:** The paper offers three exposed predictions. First, a spacecraft on the same field lines during a disagreement spike should see Alfvénic signatures (broadband field fluctuations near 1 Hz, field-aligned bursts of electrons of ionospheric origin) concentrated within under a kilometre poleward of the flow reversal, and none during agreement intervals. Second, the bottom of the static potential should sit near 1,600 km (above-drop case) or above about 9,000 km (below-drop case) — a satellite crossing at, say, 3,000 km would find itself inside the accelerator in one case and below it in the other. Third, if the method is general, other counter-flowing ASK arcs should show the same intermittent, poleward-only disagreement locked to resonator frequencies. All three could come out wrong. The event-specific altitudes are largely retrodictive — fitted to this one event — but the statistical prediction (poleward-only, ~1 Hz-locked disagreement) is genuinely forward-looking.

**Formalism load:** fires for the toy model only. In the above-drop case, four free parameters are fitted to satisfy one condition (equal arrival times within 0.05 s); that a small solution window exists demonstrates viability, not a constraint, and the model doesn't reproduce the observed 3 km separation. The model's genuine work is the flux-tube size-to-altitude mapping and the twice-wave-speed energy match, both of which are simple and checkable (and reproduced above). The rest is scaffolding.

## Section 6 — Limitations & Open Questions

**Single 25-second event.** Everything rests on one arc, and the paper states only about 0.5% of ASK data are suitable. **(A) Consensus** — a single case study cannot establish a method's reliability or the generality of the asymmetry; the authors frame it as a demonstration. **(paper §4)**

**The rulers aren't fully independent.** Seeding optical flow with the energy-derived field, plus a shear reward chosen on visual plausibility, couples the two estimates. **(C) Speculative** — the authors argue the seed's influence is small after downsampling, and the size of residual bias hasn't been tested (e.g., by an unseeded control run); the magnitude of the effect is my inference. **(analyst inference)**

**Optical-flow speeds uncertain by 25–50%, with systematic underestimate at high fields.** **(A) Consensus** — the authors state this explicitly and their own comparison shows the motion ruler flattening at large values. **(paper §6.1)**

**Brightness constancy is violated, and the image is a 2D projection of a 3D structure.** Structures form and fade, emission heights vary across the arc (lower in the center, higher at the edges), and a projection of a curl-free 3D field needn't be curl-free in 2D. **(A) Consensus** — acknowledged in the paper as intrinsic to the method. **(paper §6.1)**

**Energy maps assume a single Maxwellian spectrum and a model atmosphere.** Calibration (about ±20%) and collision-cross-section errors (about ±10%) are systematic; directions are robust but magnitudes aren't. During Alfvénic bursts the spectrum is likely non-Maxwellian. **(A) Consensus** for the stated uncertainties; the non-Maxwellian concern is standard in the field. **(paper §6.2; broader literature)**

**Static asymmetric (mixed U/S) potentials as an alternative.** **(B) Contested** — the intermittent ~1 Hz timing argues against a static cause, but no quantitative test rules out a static asymmetric structure being modulated by something non-Alfvénic. **(analyst inference)**

**Toy model assumptions: fixed wave width, single frequency, 1D geometry.** Under the widely used assumption that wave width narrows with converging field lines, the single-wave explanation fails; the model also gets the sideways separation wrong by a factor of three. **(A) Consensus** — the authors acknowledge both. **(paper §6.3.3)**

**Altitude estimates sensitive to the size ratio.** Changing the measured main/neighbor size ratio from 2.0 to 2.5 shifts the neighbor altitude by about 0.4 Earth radii. **(C) Speculative** — from recomputation using the paper's flux-tube relation and dipole scaling; the paper does not report this sensitivity. **(analyst inference)**

**Only one acceleration mechanism (one-bounce, Fermi-like) considered.** Landau resonance and effective-potential acceleration by the same waves are not modeled. **(A) Consensus** — the paper states it chose the simplest mechanism. **(paper §6.3.3)**

**Open questions (12–24 months):** a statistical survey across many ASK counter-flow events; an unseeded or differently-seeded optical-flow control to quantify independence; a conjunction with a low-altitude satellite (in-situ spectra to replace the Maxwellian assumption) or a high-altitude one (direct field comparison); and a 2D or 3D wave model that allows the wave to narrow and reproduces the observed separation.

## Section 7 — Detailed Summary & Explanation

Brindley and Whiter use a narrow-field, high-speed multi-color imager on Svalbard to study a 25-second stretch of a bright auroral arc whose two sides stream in opposite directions — the classic sign of a U-shaped voltage structure accelerating electrons from a few thousand kilometres up. They build two independent-in-principle estimates of the sideways electric field above the accelerator. One reads it from motion: optical flow tracks the auroral structures, the drift speed is multiplied by the magnetic field strength, and only the electrostatic (gradient) part is kept. The other reads it from color: the ratio of oxygen 777.4 nm to nitrogen 673 nm brightness gives the electron energy at every pixel through an atmosphere model, and the sideways slope of that energy map — through the path-independence of voltage in a static structure — equals the sideways field. Because both estimates scale identically with altitude along a flux tube, their ratio and directions can be compared without knowing where the accelerator sits.

The rulers broadly agree: voltage drops of about 2–3.5 kV across the arc track each other for most of the interval, and field directions agree away from the arc's center. That supports the U-shaped picture already favored by spacecraft studies. But there's a systematic exception: within about 750 m poleward of the flow-reversal line, the two estimates often point in opposite directions, which means the energy peak has slid poleward of where a pure U-shape puts it. This asymmetry comes and goes, peaking during intervals when the energy just poleward of the shear line pulses at around 1 Hz — the ringing frequency of the cavity that traps Alfvén waves above the ionosphere. The highest-energy blobs also stall and blink rather than drift with the flow.

To test whether an Alfvén wave can explain this, the authors focus on a faint "neighbor" structure that appears briefly just poleward of the main arc, mirroring a spur in the main arc's intensification but at half the size and half the energy. They propose one narrow Alfvén wave bounced electrons twice: once high up (about 1.4 Earth radii), making the small neighbor, and once lower (about 0.5 Earth radii), making the large main structure. A toy wave-propagation model finds an altitude pair that matches the size ratio, the simultaneous arrival, and the energies — each structure's energy is close to twice the local wave speed, the signature of electrons bouncing off a moving wave front. If the wave acts above the static drop, the static structure's base is near 1,600 km, consistent with older estimates; if below, the base must lie above about 9,000 km. The model misses the observed sideways separation and works only if the wave keeps a fixed width.

**Explaining the summary.** The framing leans on the loop check because that's where the paper's real value lies: the agreement is confirmatory, but the *structured disagreement* is the new information, and it's more trustworthy than the agreement because the method's built-in bias pushes toward agreement. The toy model is framed as a viability demonstration, not a measurement, because its altitudes are sensitive to an imprecisely measured size ratio and because it fits several unknowns to one condition. The thing to take away is a tool: continuous ground-based cameras can flag *when and where* Alfvénic acceleration intrudes on a static accelerator, which is exactly the targeting information spacecraft conjunction studies need.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the degree to which seeding the optical flow with the energy-derived field inflates the reported agreement — I've argued it makes agreement an upper bound and disagreement robust, but without seeing an unseeded control run I can't size the bias, and a specialist in auroral optical flow might judge it negligible (or larger than I suggest); I'm also relying on read-off values from a log-scale plot for the twice-wave-speed check.

## Section 8 — Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **Voltage around a loop must add up — unless something's changing.** If the aurora's accelerator were purely static, two independent ground-based estimates of its electric field would always agree; this paper uses their disagreement as a fingerprint of waves.

2. **The arc's poleward edge is where the static picture cracks.** In this event, the energy peak repeatedly shifted a few hundred metres poleward of where the static model puts it, pulsing at about once a second — the beat of the Alfvén resonator.

3. **One wave, two altitudes, two auroral copies.** A faint half-size twin beside the main arc can be explained by a single Alfvén wave bouncing electrons first high up and then lower down — a neat idea, but still a viability sketch from one 25-second event.

## Section 9 — Shorter Summary

The bright curtains of aurora are made by electrons accelerated to a few thousand electron-volts somewhere between 1,000 and 10,000 km above the ground. Two accelerators are known: a steady voltage structure shaped like a "U" when seen edge-on, and Alfvén waves — magnetic disturbances travelling along field lines that can kick electrons in short bursts. Telling them apart usually needs a lucky satellite pass.

This study uses only a fast, high-resolution camera on Svalbard watching an arc whose two sides stream in opposite directions — the visible signature of the U-shaped structure. From the same images, the authors estimate the electric field above the arc two ways: from how fast the aurora moves, and from how the electron energy (read from the ratio of two auroral colors) varies across the arc. If the structure is purely static, the two must agree, much as two voltmeters measuring different paths in a circuit must agree unless a changing magnetic field threads the loop.

Over 25 seconds, the two estimates broadly agree, supporting the U-shaped picture. But just poleward of the line where the flow reverses, they repeatedly point in opposite directions, meaning the energy peak has shifted off-center. These episodes coincide with energy pulsing at about once per second — the natural ringing of Alfvén waves trapped above the ionosphere.

A faint half-size copy of the main arc's structure appears briefly beside it. A simple model shows one Alfvén wave could create both by accelerating electrons at two different heights, roughly 9,000 km and 3,400 km, with each structure's energy matching twice the wave's local speed.

The caveats are real: one event, a motion estimate seeded by the energy estimate it's compared against, and a crude model that misplaces the twin. The lasting contribution is a ground-based tool that flags when waves interfere with the steady accelerator — telling future satellite campaigns where and when to look.
