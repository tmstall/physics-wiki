# MEGATRON: One Giant First-Star Explosion, One Fixed-Size Gas Tank, and the Iron Floor of the Smallest Galaxies

**Hook:** The faintest galaxies in the local Universe all sit at nearly the same iron level, and this simulation says that is no coincidence: a single enormous first-generation star explosion wrote one fixed amount of iron into a gas reservoir whose size was pinned by ultraviolet light from the growing Milky Way next door.

*Paper: Rey et al. (31 authors), "MEGATRON: How the first stars can create an iron metallicity plateau in the smallest dwarf galaxies," arXiv:2510.05232v2 [astro-ph.GA], version dated 4 Sept 2026. Clean 20-page full-text PDF read in full, so no retrieval was needed.*

---

## Section 2 — Big-Picture Context

**The puzzle.** Ultra-faint dwarf galaxies (UFDs) are the dimmest galaxies known: fewer than about a hundred thousand Suns' worth of stars, often just a few thousand. They are "fossils." Cosmic reionization (the era when the first galaxies' ultraviolet light heated the intergalactic gas) left them too weakly bound to catch fresh gas, so they stopped forming stars very early and their stars are 10+ billion years old. That makes their chemistry a frozen record of the earliest enrichment.

Chemists' shorthand first. Astronomers report iron content as [Fe/H]: the iron-to-hydrogen ratio on a log scale relative to the Sun, so 0 is solar, and every step of −1 means ten times less iron. A value of −2.5 means roughly 1/300 of the solar iron fraction. It works like decibels: a log scale where equal steps are equal factors.

Now the observation. For larger dwarf galaxies, more stellar mass means more iron, a steady "mass–metallicity relation." Below about a hundred thousand solar masses of stars, that relation stops climbing and flattens at about −2.5. It is a plateau. This is odd because a galaxy with very few stars should have made very little iron, yet these galaxies are iron-richer than simple models predict. Prior explanations include the shape of the stellar mass distribution, the physics of the very first stars, a minimum "threshold" enrichment of the gas, and pollution from neighbors.

**What the paper does.** The authors introduce the MEGATRON simulation suite: a cosmological radiation-hydrodynamics "zoom" of one region that collapses into a Milky-Way-like galaxy by today, run only to redshift 8 (about 650 million years after the Big Bang). Three features make it unusual. It follows radiation and non-equilibrium chemistry for 80+ species, so the gas physics is computed rather than assumed. Its resolution (roughly 3 parsecs, dark matter particles of 25,000 solar masses) is fine enough to place individual first-generation (Population III, "Pop III") stars. And it contains hundreds of faint dwarf galaxies at once. They run four variants that differ only in how later-generation (Pop II) stars behave, all sharing identical Pop III assumptions.

**Paper type and stakes.** This is a numerical-simulation paper proposing a specific mechanism: the iron plateau and a tail of very iron-poor dwarfs come from the microphysics of the first stars (the explosions of very massive Pop III stars called pair-instability supernovae) plus the UV environment they formed in. If right, a census of UFD iron levels becomes a measurement of how massive the first stars were.

**Prior Belief Check.** This complicates and extends the consensus; it does not overturn it. The broad idea that Pop III physics or the early stellar mass distribution shapes the plateau already exists in the literature (the paper cites several such studies). What is new is a self-consistent, population-scale demonstration. Experts will find one piece surprising: dominant pair-instability enrichment in these galaxies runs against the fact that metal-poor Milky Way halo stars show little sign of the distinctive pair-instability chemical pattern. The authors argue that UFDs contribute at most about 1% of the halo's stars, so there is no contradiction, but the point stays contested.

**Replication & Convergence Note.** This is a single-collaboration result from one simulation code and one zoom region (a proto-Milky-Way), tied to companion papers by the same group. No independent group has reproduced it. Real confirmation would be one of two things: another team with a different code and different first-star yields getting the same plateau, or observers measuring the predicted iron-poor tail (and the missing nitrogen) in actual UFDs.

**Posture note.** Because this is a preprint with no journal reference (the paper thanks anonymous referees and uses an open-journal template, but I did not confirm where or whether it was published), I treated it adversarially: assume no load-bearing core until the paper earns it. The core does turn out to be real, but narrower than the abstract's "robust" language suggests (see the Assumption Audit).

## Section 3 — Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Concept 1: Why the first stars were massive.** Stars that form from gas cool by radiating heat away; clumps that cool well fragment into small stars. The earliest gas had only hydrogen and helium, so the only coolant was molecular hydrogen, a weak one. Poor cooling means the gas stays warm, lumps stay big, and stars come out massive. Simulations suggest typical Pop III masses around 100 solar masses (the paper adopts a distribution peaking there).

**Analogy:** It is like a CPU with a tiny heat sink: it can't shed heat quickly, so it ends up running in a coarse, high-temperature regime instead of many fine-grained cool operations. Cooling capacity sets the "grain size."

**Breaks when:** you ask where the exact number comes from. A heat sink has a datasheet; the first-star mass scale does not. Newer simulations (the paper cites several) find that turbulence and fragmentation could push masses lower, which would change everything downstream.

**Concept 2: Pair-instability supernovae (PISNe).** Stars between roughly 140 and 300 solar masses reach core temperatures where light turns into electron–positron pairs. That robs the core of the pressure holding it up, the core collapses, then ignites in a thermonuclear runaway that blows the entire star apart: no black hole is left, and a great deal of iron and other metals is ejected. The iron yield rises steeply with mass. Using the yield tables the paper adopts (Nomoto et al. 2013): about 0.5 solar masses of iron at 140 solar masses, and about 5 solar masses at 180. Stars below ~140 or above ~300 solar masses collapse straight to a black hole and eject nothing.

**Analogy:** A clean shutdown versus a thermal-runaway burn-out. Most massive stars "shut down quietly" into black holes, keeping all their metals locked inside. A PISN is the chip that overheats, destroys the whole die, and scatters its contents.

**Breaks when:** you want a sharp switch. A real chip fails somewhere on a continuum; here the iron yield changes by a factor of ten across a modest range of stellar mass, and the exact boundary mass is still debated (some models start PISNe at 140, others higher).

**Concept 3: The Lyman-Werner (LW) background.** LW photons are ultraviolet light with energies just below what ionizes hydrogen, so they travel far through neutral gas and destroy molecular hydrogen, the first stars' only coolant. In a region with lots of nearby star formation, small halos can no longer cool their gas. Pop III star formation has to wait until a halo is large enough that its gas can still cool despite the destruction.

**Analogy:** A noisy neighbor on a shared cache evicting your lines. To keep your working set resident, you need a bigger cache. Here the "cache size" is halo mass, and in MEGATRON the strong background pushes Pop III formation to halos of roughly 10 million solar masses.

**Breaks when:** you think bigger just means "more room." A bigger halo does two things at once: it dilutes the explosion's metals in more gas, but it also holds on to those metals better because its gravity well is deeper. A cache has no equivalent of "the bigger tank also keeps the contents from leaking."

**Concept 4: Reionization quenching and "fossil" status.** Once cosmic UV heats the intergalactic gas, tiny halos cannot pull in fresh gas, and star formation shuts down. This is why local UFDs are a snapshot of the early Universe, and why a simulation stopping at redshift 8 can be meaningfully compared with galaxies seen today.

**Analogy:** A write-once ROM image. The contents are fixed early, then nothing changes.

**Breaks when:** you forget that the paper's own simulated galaxies at redshift 8 are mostly still forming stars. Only a subset is already quenched, and that subset is small.

**Concept 5: Tagging dark-matter particles to bridge 12 billion years.** The hydrodynamic run stops at redshift 8. To guess each dwarf's fate by today, the authors rerun the same initial conditions with dark matter only (cheaper, at 8× coarser resolution), mark the 5% most gravitationally bound dark matter particles of each redshift-8 halo, and follow those "tags" to today to see whether the dwarf survives, merges, or is shredded.

**Analogy:** Tagging request IDs in a fast, low-fidelity simulator and replaying them through a cheaper model of the full pipeline to predict where each request ends up.

**Breaks when:** the cheap model leaves out something that matters. The dark-matter-only run has no gas, no galactic disc, and no baryonic tidal effects, all of which can change which dwarfs survive.

**Central analogy for this paper:** One fixed payload, one pinned buffer size.

## Section 4 — Core Technical Explanation

**What they simulate.** The zoom region ("Halo 599" from the VINTERGATAN family) is a proto-Milky-Way whose main halo reaches 10^12 solar masses by today. It is evolved with an adaptive-mesh code (RAMSES-RTZ) that solves gravity, hydrodynamics, radiation transport, and non-equilibrium chemistry together. Pop III stars form individually with a lognormal mass distribution peaking at 100 solar masses. Explosion outcomes follow progenitor mass: roughly 10–20 solar masses explode as ordinary core-collapse supernovae (about 1e51 erg), 20–40 as more energetic "hypernovae" (about 2e52 erg), 140–300 as pair-instability supernovae (about 5e52 erg), and everything else collapses to a black hole with no metal release. Once the gas is enriched past one millionth of solar metallicity, star formation switches to Pop II "stellar-population particles" of at least 500 solar masses, with winds, supernovae (including Type Ia), radiation, and a standard mass distribution.

The four variants change only Pop II behavior: "Efficient SF" (baseline feedback), "Bursty SF" (five times more energy per supernova), "Varying IMF" (more top-heavy stars in dense gas), and "HN, high efficiency" (hypernovae plus more efficient star formation). The first-star assumptions are the same in all four. Appendix A shows the realized counts across the galaxies: roughly 3,500 pair-instability explosions, about 90 hypernovae, and no ordinary core-collapse supernovae at all, because the assumed mass distribution favors massive stars.

**Result 1: the plateau and a tail (Figures 2 and 3).** Plotting average iron against stellar mass, three of the four runs reproduce the observed relation across several decades of stellar mass. The "Efficient SF" run overshoots above about a million solar masses because its weak outflows keep iron in the galaxy. So the high-mass end is sensitive to Pop II feedback. At the faint end (below roughly a hundred thousand solar masses), all four runs show the same thing: a pile-up of dwarfs near −2.5 and a long tail down to about −5. In the paper's counts, 78% of faint dwarfs fall between −3 and −2 (the "plateau"), and about 22% fall below −3 (the "tail").

**Result 2: the plateau comes from one big explosion (Figures 4 and 5).** Almost every faint dwarf had exactly one Pop III explosion. A small fraction (about 6%) had two, and about 2% had none (those were enriched from outside). The type of explosion sorts the dwarfs. Plateau dwarfs mostly experienced a high-mass pair-instability supernova (progenitors of 160 to 300 solar masses), which yields a few solar masses of iron. Dwarfs below the plateau have a mix that includes low-mass pair-instability supernovae (140–160 solar masses) yielding only ~0.5 solar masses, a factor of ten less iron. Ten times less iron is exactly one step of 1.0 in [Fe/H], which is the gap between the plateau and the tail's bulk.

**Result 3: why the plateau value comes out the same every time (Figure 7).** Because strong LW light from the central proto-galaxy suppresses molecular hydrogen, Pop III stars mostly form in halos of at least about 5 million solar masses (typically 10–30 million). Halos that large retain metals from explosions above 1e53 erg. So the "tank size" (gas mass) is roughly fixed, and the "payload" (iron from one big pair-instability supernova) is roughly fixed. Their ratio is the plateau. A single pair-instability explosion is not violent enough to expel all the metals from such a halo, so they fall back and are folded into the next stars.

**Worked equation box: the back-of-envelope dilution check.**

The authors dilute one explosion's iron across a halo's gas. Written out:

$$[\mathrm{Fe/H}] \approx \log_{10}\!\left[\frac{M_{\mathrm{Fe}}}{X_{\mathrm{H}}\, f_{\mathrm{b}}\, M_{200}}\Big/\left(\frac{\mathrm{Fe}}{\mathrm{H}}\right)_{\odot}\right]$$

**Symbol definitions:**

- $M_{\mathrm{Fe}}$ : iron mass ejected by the explosion (solar masses)
- $X_{\mathrm{H}}$ : hydrogen mass fraction of primordial gas, 0.76 (dimensionless)
- $f_{\mathrm{b}}$ : baryon (gas) fraction of the halo, here the cosmic fraction of matter that is gas (dimensionless)
- $M_{200}$ : total mass of the halo (solar masses)
- $(\mathrm{Fe/H})_{\odot}$ : the Sun's iron-to-hydrogen mass ratio, about 0.00175 (dimensionless)

**What this actually means:** It is a concentration calculation: one fixed write ("5 solar masses of iron") divided by the capacity of a buffer ("the hydrogen in the halo"), then converted to a log scale. The answer depends on the ratio of two pinned numbers, so it barely changes unless one of them changes by a large factor.

*Hand check with real numbers.* For a 10-million-solar-mass halo, assuming all cosmic baryons are present and my own assumed matter fraction of about 0.30 (the paper states only the baryon density): gas is about 1.6 million solar masses, hydrogen about 1.25 million. Then 5 solar masses of iron gives an iron-to-hydrogen ratio of about 4.0e-6, which is about 1/437 of solar, so [Fe/H] ≈ −2.6. The paper reads off −2.5. For 0.5 solar masses of iron I get −3.6 (paper: −3.5), and for 0.07 solar masses (a core-collapse supernova) I get −4.5 (paper: −4.5). The same arithmetic shows the degeneracy the authors admit: a core-collapse supernova in a 100,000-solar-mass halo gives −2.5 as well. So the plateau value alone does not uniquely identify which explosion and halo mass produced it.

**Result 4: other elements (Figure 6).** Carbon and magnesium agree broadly with observations, with the simulated carbon about 0.5 dex low, within the stated uncertainties of the yield models. Nitrogen is the problem: simulated dwarfs make orders of magnitude too little. This is expected because pair-instability supernovae produce almost no nitrogen (they favor even-numbered elements), and it is the signature that a pair-instability-dominated picture must explain. The authors argue the observed nitrogen detections are few and mostly upper limits, and that real stars have extra nitrogen channels MEGATRON omits (stellar winds, rotation).

**Result 5: the 12-billion-year bridge (Section 5, Figures 8 and 9).** Redshift-8 dwarfs are mostly still forming stars, so the authors select the subset that has already stopped (no new stars for the last 100 million years). For the four runs this leaves 3, 28, 17, and 51 galaxies. A statistical test finds no significant difference between their iron distribution and the full sample (p ≥ 0.1 each), and the plateau and tail persist. For tidal survival, about a quarter of the simulated faint dwarfs would still be intact satellites at redshift 0; the rest would be absorbed into the diffuse halo. The plateau and tail survive in the surviving subset as well. Correcting stellar masses for aging (mass loss) shifts them by 0.2–0.3 dex without changing the shape.

**Assumption Audit.**

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "robust to large variations in Pop II feedback" means the plateau survives modeling uncertainty in general. The paper actually varies only Pop II behavior. All four runs share the same first-star mass distribution, the same explosion yields, and the same UV environment, and the paper itself says the plateau-versus-tail balance depends strongly on those (Appendix A notes a different published yield set would produce a more pronounced tail and a less pronounced plateau). The untested part is the part doing the work.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the simulated galaxies are the observed dwarfs. The paper actually compares star-forming galaxies at redshift 8 with galaxies observed at redshift 0, supported by indirect bridging (the quenched subset, dark-matter tagging). Only the quenched subset is a plausible fossil match, and "no significant difference" from a test on 3–51 galaxies is weak evidence of sameness.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "agreement with data" means the model was fit to the plateau. The authors say the plateau is not fine-tuned, which is plausible since the inputs were fixed in advance. What the paper does not say is whether other first-star mass distributions were tried. The headline mass scale (a peak at 100 solar masses) is an assumption chosen before and motivated by older simulations, not a derived result.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the missing nitrogen would sink a pair-instability picture. The paper actually argues the comparison data are mostly upper limits, the true nitrogen channels are missing from the model, and UFDs contribute at most about 1% of halo stars, so the Milky Way halo's lack of a pair-instability fingerprint is not decisive. That is an argument, not a demonstration.

## Section 5 — What's Genuinely New or Clever

**1. A population, not a handful.** Earlier work on first-star effects in individual or idealized halos could show a mechanism could work. MEGATRON puts radiation transport, 80-species chemistry, and individual Pop III stars into hundreds of faint dwarfs inside one realistic environment, so the plateau and its tail come out as distributions, not as isolated cases. This is new to the field, not just to the reader, in the combination, though individual ingredients are known.

**2. The two-knob diagnostic.** The elegant step is seeing that the plateau and the tail are two different payloads (high-mass versus low-mass pair-instability supernovae) dropped into nearly the same tank. That turns the iron-poor tail into a counting measurement: the ratio of dwarfs on the plateau to dwarfs in the tail tracks the ratio of high- to low-mass pair-instability explosions, which tracks the first-star mass distribution. This is new to the reader and, as a framed measurement idea, reasonably new to the field.

**Predictive Content Check.**

1. **Falsifiable handle (always):** The nearest prediction that can come out wrong is the iron-poor tail: about 20% of ultra-faint dwarfs should have galaxy-averaged iron at or below −3. The plateau itself is a retrodiction because it was observed first. The tail is the real exposure, and currently it is in tension with the data. Among the 38 observed dwarfs the paper uses, none has a galaxy average at or below −3, and the paper's own estimate is that, if the tail were real and these were independent draws, seeing none has a probability of about 0.02% (0.8 raised to the 38th power). (As printed, the sentence says "not observing any dwarfs with [Fe/H] ≥ −3.0," which reads like a sign slip for "≤ −3.0"; the number itself checks out for the tail reading.) The authors resolve this by arguing that galaxy averages from a few measured stars are biased high: one dwarf (Pictor II) sits at −2.99, and a single star at or below −4 was recently found. Their Figure E1 supports it by showing that even plateau dwarfs have individual stars at or below −4. It is a plausible rescue but not yet a confirmation. A second handle, the missing nitrogen, currently disagrees with the few detections. The paper itself says that a complete census with no tail would push the first-star mass distribution toward extremely top-heavy values or point to another origin.

2. **Formalism load:** Not triggered. The simulation machinery is heavy but it generates a result with an empirical handle, rather than dressing up a claim without one.

## Section 6 — Limitations & Open Questions

1. **The first-star physics is the untested knob.** The mass distribution (peak at 100 solar masses), the yield tables, and the explosion-mass boundaries are all fixed, and the plateau-versus-tail balance depends on them. **(A) Consensus** — the paper states this directly and notes competing yield models would shift the balance. **(paper §6, Appendix A)**

2. **One environment.** The sample comes from a single proto-Milky-Way zoom with an unusually strong UV background; isolated dwarfs lose pair-instability metals easily, per earlier work the paper cites. **(A) Consensus** — the authors say a systematic exploration of environments is future work. **(paper §6)**

3. **The link between redshift-8 simulation and redshift-0 data is indirect.** The bridge relies on a small quenched subset (3 to 51 galaxies per run) and a dark-matter-only tagging method that omits baryonic tides and central-density changes. **(B) Contested** — the paper presents the bridge as supportive, but non-rejection by a small-sample test is weak evidence and reasonable experts will weigh it differently. **(analyst inference, with the paper's own caveats in §5.2)**

4. **The predicted iron-poor tail is not yet seen.** The paper's own arithmetic puts the chance of seeing none at roughly 0.02% if observed averages were unbiased. **(B) Contested** — the bias argument is plausible and partly supported (Figure E1, Pictor II, one star at or below −4) but untested at population scale. **(paper §6, Appendix E)**

5. **Nitrogen is off by orders of magnitude and carbon is about 0.5 dex low.** **(B) Contested** — the observational constraints are mostly upper limits and the model lacks wind and rotation channels, but a distinctive pair-instability pattern is also absent from halo stars. **(paper §4.2; broader literature)**

6. **The analytic dilution estimate is a toy.** It assumes every explosion's metals are retained and mixed uniformly, and a core-collapse supernova in a 100,000-solar-mass halo gives the same −2.5, so the plateau value alone is degenerate. **(A) Consensus** — the authors state both points. **(paper §4.3)**

7. **Small internal inconsistency in the split between plateau and tail.** The Figure 4 legend counts (8 above, 876 on, 303 below the plateau, total 1,187) give about 74% on the plateau and 26% below it, while the text quotes 78% and 22%. **(C) Speculative** — probably different sample definitions, but I could not reconcile them from the paper alone. **(analyst inference)**

## Section 7 — Detailed Summary & Explanation

The paper asks why the faintest galaxies near the Milky Way all have about the same iron content, roughly 1/300 of the Sun's, even though they have wildly different (tiny) numbers of stars. Existing models have trouble making them iron-rich enough.

The authors build a simulation of the early Universe's version of the Milky Way's neighborhood, evolved to about 650 million years after the Big Bang, and watch hundreds of future ultra-faint dwarfs form. Their detailed star-by-star treatment of the first stars matters: those stars are assumed to be very massive (a typical mass around 100 times the Sun), and most that explode do so as pair-instability supernovae, which blow the star apart and release a large, mass-dependent amount of iron.

The key observation is arithmetic. Almost every faint dwarf experiences exactly one such first-star explosion. The strong ultraviolet light from the growing central galaxy stops small halos from forming first stars, so those stars form in halos around 10 million solar masses. Those halos are big enough to hold on to the explosion's metals, and their gas mass is roughly the same from one dwarf to the next. A fixed amount of iron (a few solar masses from the heaviest pair-instability explosions) spread into a gas reservoir of about a million solar masses of hydrogen gives an iron level near −2.5. That is the plateau. A smaller explosion, from the lighter end of the pair-instability range, yields about ten times less iron and puts the galaxy about one step lower on the log scale, which populates a tail toward −5.

So the shape is mostly set by two things: how big the gas tank is (set by ultraviolet light and halo gravity) and which kind of explosion dropped metals into it (set by the first-star mass distribution and yields). The four versions of the simulation, which differ only in later-generation star behavior, all give the same faint-end distribution. The high-mass end, in contrast, is controlled by outflows from later stars: the run with the weakest outflows overshoots the data.

The authors then argue the redshift-8 result survives to today. A subset of galaxies has already stopped forming stars, with the same iron distribution as the full sample, and about a quarter of the simulated dwarfs would remain bound satellites around the final galaxy rather than being torn apart, with plateau and tail intact. Their central forecast is that about one in five ultra-faint dwarfs should have average iron at or below −3, which no observed dwarf currently shows. They suggest this is because averages built from a few measured stars lean high, and they point to recent observations of individual iron-poor stars as support.

Why frame it this way? The paper's real contribution is a mechanism that converts a puzzle into a measurable ratio: count dwarfs on the plateau versus in the tail and you learn about the first stars. The risks are in the framing too. The "robustness" the authors emphasize concerns later-generation star assumptions, while the first-star assumptions that do the actual work are held fixed. What to take away is a coherent, quantitative, testable story whose decisive test (the tail) is still pending.

## Section 8 — Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **The iron plateau is division, not magic:** about 5 solar masses of iron from one giant first-star explosion, spread through about a million solar masses of hydrogen, lands at about 1/300 of the Sun's iron fraction.

2. **The radiation set the tank:** ultraviolet light from the neighboring proto-galaxy forced the first stars into larger halos, which both fixed the dilution and kept the metals from escaping.

3. **The decisive test is still pending:** the model says about one in five ultra-faint dwarfs should be much more iron-poor than anything yet measured, and if a complete census does not find them, the first stars were not what this simulation assumes.

## Section 9 — Shorter Summary

The faintest dwarf galaxies around the Milky Way share nearly the same iron content, about 1/300 of the Sun's, even though they have very different numbers of stars. Standard models struggle to explain this "iron plateau," because galaxies with so few stars should have made much less iron.

A large new simulation called MEGATRON follows the early Universe's version of the Milky Way's neighborhood in fine detail, including the very first generation of stars and the radiation they produced. Those first stars are assumed to be very massive, and many of them explode in a way that destroys the star entirely and ejects a large amount of iron.

The simulation shows that nearly every faint dwarf galaxy experienced exactly one of these explosions. Ultraviolet light from the growing central galaxy meant that first stars could only form inside fairly large halos of gas and dark matter, about ten million solar masses. These halos hold on to the explosion's metals, and their gas mass is about the same from galaxy to galaxy. A fixed amount of iron spread through a fixed amount of gas gives the same iron level each time: the plateau. A lighter version of the explosion gives about ten times less iron, which explains a predicted tail of unusually iron-poor dwarfs.

The authors check that this result should survive until today, using the subset of galaxies that had already stopped forming stars and a cheaper dark-matter-only run to estimate which galaxies survive tidal disruption. They also find the result does not depend on how later stars behave.

The weak points are clear. All four simulation variants share identical first-star assumptions, so the key physics is not tested. The model produces far too little nitrogen. It predicts a tail of iron-poor dwarfs that has not been observed yet, though the authors argue current measurements, which average over few stars, would miss it. A complete census of iron in the faintest galaxies, under way with new telescopes, would decide whether the first stars were as massive as assumed.
