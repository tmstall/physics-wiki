# 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**A Universe-in-a-Box Reaches Today — With Black Holes That Actually Wander**

ASTRID, one of the largest cosmological hydro simulations ever run to redshift zero, shows that when you stop teleporting black holes to galaxy centers and instead let dynamical friction drag them in, you get more realistic black-hole diversity — plus a ready-made catalog for PTA, LISA, and large-scale-structure work.

# 2. Big-Picture Context

Modern cosmology has two favorite toys that rarely fit in the same box. **Dark-matter-only** simulations can be enormous and cheap: they paint the cosmic web of halos but say nothing about stars, gas, or the black holes that live in galaxy centers. **Hydrodynamical** simulations add gas, star formation, and feedback — the physics that makes galaxies look like galaxies — but every extra particle and every subgrid recipe costs CPU time, so boxes shrink and resolution suffers. The field’s flagship hydro runs (Illustris/TNG, EAGLE, SIMBA, Horizon-AGN, FLAMINGO, and others) have taught us a lot, yet they still struggle with a quiet crisis at the black-hole end: many of them *reposition* black-hole particles straight to the local potential minimum. That is a numerical convenience. Real black holes must spiral in through **dynamical friction** — the gravitational wake they raise in surrounding stars and dark matter — and that spiral takes time. Shortcuts erase wandering black holes, scramble merger clocks, and often shrink the diversity of black-hole masses at fixed galaxy mass.

ASTRID was built to attack that gap at scale. It is a cosmological hydrodynamical simulation run with the SPH code **MP-GADGET**: a cube about **250 $h^{-1}$ Mpc** on a side (~370 Mpc with their Hubble parameter), filled with **$2 \times 5500^{3} \approx 0.33$ trillion** dark-matter and gas particles, evolved from $z=99$ all the way to **$z=0$** (today). That combination — huge particle count *and* arrival at the present day — puts it among the largest hydro runs of its kind. Earlier ASTRID papers already mined the high-redshift universe (dual AGNs, little red dots, GW forecasts). This paper, led by Yihao Zhou with Tiziana Di Matteo and collaborators, is the **local-universe report card**: massive black holes (MBHs), galaxies, groups and clusters, and how those populations cluster on large scales *today*.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** Peer-reviewed simulation methods / population-results paper (not a new telescope measurement). Stakes: validate ASTRID’s $z=0$ galaxy and MBH demographics against observations, and advertise it as infrastructure for electromagnetic and gravitational-wave science over the next decade.

**Prior Belief Check:** Incremental but load-bearing for users of the simulation. Experts already expected a large, well-tuned hydro box to roughly match the stellar mass function and MBH scaling relations after calibration. What is *usefully* surprising — and what the authors lean on — is that replacing repositioning with a dynamical-friction subgrid model yields **scatter in MBH–galaxy relations closer to the observed diversity**, including room for undermassive black holes in massive galaxies that other large-volume sims often miss. The Coma-scale cluster count and tracer-bias results are expected consequences of volume + resolution, not conceptual shocks.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** Single collaboration’s flagship run (public data at [astrid.psc.edu](https://astrid.psc.edu)). Convergence with the broader simulation literature is conceptual (same Bondi accretion family, two-mode AGN feedback cousins of TNG-style models) rather than a second independent code reproducing these exact $z=0$ catalogs. Observational agreement is multi-probe (MBH mass function, AGN luminosity function, GSMF, colors, cluster stellar budgets) but model-dependent wherever subgrid choices dominate.

# 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Concept 1 — What a cosmological hydro simulation actually is.**

Think of a climate model for the whole observable patch of cosmos you can afford: you discretize a cubic volume into particles (or cells), grow structure from tiny early fluctuations under gravity, and let gas cool, form stars, and explode. Dark matter only feels gravity. Baryons feel gravity *plus* hydro forces and a pile of **subgrid models** — recipes for physics below the resolution limit (star formation, supernova feedback, black-hole accretion disks). Nobody resolves an accretion disk around a $10^{8}\,M_{\odot}$ black hole inside a 370 Mpc box; you *parameterize* it. The output is a synthetic census: halos, galaxies, black holes, and their clustering, which you then compare to surveys.

> **Breaks when:** you treat a good match to one observed scaling relation as proof that every subgrid knob is “correct.” Degeneracies are rampant; different wrong recipes can share the same $z=0$ mean relation.

**Concept 2 — Redshift zero, and why “getting to $z=0$” is a flex.**

**Redshift** $z$ measures lookback: $z=0$ is now; $z\sim 6$–$10$ is the first billion years. Many expensive hydro runs stop at intermediate redshift because late-time clustering and feedback are costly. ASTRID’s claim to fame here is finishing the marathon with enough volume left to sample rare beasts — Coma-like clusters and $10^{11}\,M_{\odot}$ black holes — while keeping resolution high enough for $\sim 10^{4}$–$10^{5}\,M_{\odot}$ seeds. That is why PTA/LISA forecasts and local scaling relations can live in the *same* volume.

> **Breaks when:** you assume finishing at $z=0$ automatically means better high-$z$ physics. High-$z$ results still depend on seeding and early feedback; this paper’s focus is the local census.

**Concept 3 — Dynamical friction vs black-hole repositioning.**

Drop a heavy object into a sea of lighter particles and it raises a wake; the wake pulls backward; the heavy object loses orbital energy and spirals in. That drag is **dynamical friction**. In galaxy mergers it is how black holes sink toward the center and eventually form binaries. Many large-volume sims skip the hard part: each timestep they **reposition** the black-hole particle to the nearby potential minimum. Fast and stable — and fake. You lose realistic sinking times, orbital eccentricities, and a population of **wandering** black holes still stuck in outskirts.

ASTRID instead applies a **subgrid dynamical-friction force** (Tremmel-style, as implemented in their earlier methods papers). Trajectories and velocities become meaningful; mergers can require gravitational binding plus proximity rather than “both got teleported to the same pixel.” A companion trick, the **dynamical mass** $M_{\mathrm{dyn}}$, temporarily fattens brand-new low-mass seeds so they are not violently heated by discrete particle noise until their true mass catches up.

> **Breaks when:** you hear “better dynamics” and assume the *hardening* all the way through the final parsec is resolved. Softening lengths are still ~kpc scale; subgrid friction improves the *unresolved-drag* estimate, not full stellar-loss-cone physics.

**Concept 4 — The MBH–$M_{*}$ and MBH–$\sigma$ relations.**

Empirically, bigger bulges host bigger central black holes. Plot black-hole mass against galaxy stellar mass $M_{*}$, or against stellar velocity dispersion $\sigma$ (how fast stars random-walk in the center), and you get tight-ish correlations that encode coevolution — shared gas supply, mergers, and feedback. Observations show real **scatter**: not every galaxy of a given mass has the same black hole. Many simulations produce relations that are too thin — insufficient diversity. Habouzit and collaborators have flagged this as a generic shortcoming. ASTRID’s headline claim is that their dynamical treatment (plus small seeds and resolution) populates more of the observed scatter, including some undermassive black holes in massive galaxies.

> **Breaks when:** you treat a prettier scatter plot as proof of correct seeding. The authors themselves note that seeding tied to halo mass, missing spin physics, and volume limits still sculpt the scatter.

> **Central analogy for this paper:** ASTRID as a city-scale traffic sim that finally models lane changes and merging delays instead of teleporting cars to the destination GPS pin — statistics of jams and arrivals get more realistic even if the engine still uses coarse grids.

# 4. Core Technical Explanation

**What ASTRID is, in one paragraph.**  
Zhou et al. analyze the $z=0$ snapshot of ASTRID: Planck 2020 cosmology, box $250\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{cMpc}$, particle masses $M_{\mathrm{DM}}\approx 6.7\times 10^{6}\,h^{-1}M_{\odot}$ and $M_{\mathrm{gas}}\approx 1.3\times 10^{6}\,h^{-1}M_{\odot}$, softening $\varepsilon_{g}=1.5\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{ckpc}$. Friends-of-friends halos plus SUBFIND galaxies (they keep galaxies with stellar mass $\gtrsim 10^{8}\,M_{\odot}$ inside twice the stellar half-mass radius). Public particle and merger catalogs are released for reuse.

**Black-hole life cycle in the model (why each knob exists).**

1. **Seeding.** Halos above $M_{\mathrm{halo,FOF}}>5\times 10^{9}\,h^{-1}M_{\odot}$ *and* with enough stars ($M_{*,\mathrm{FOF}}>2\times 10^{6}\,h^{-1}M_{\odot}$) can receive a seed. Seed mass is drawn stochastically between $3\times 10^{4}$ and $3\times 10^{5}\,h^{-1}M_{\odot}$ — deliberately *not* one fixed seed — so identical-mass halos need not start identical. That is how they reach a minimum $\sim 4\times 10^{4}\,M_{\odot}$ and a maximum $\sim 2\times 10^{11}\,M_{\odot}$ by $z=0$ (nearly seven decades).

2. **Dynamics.** Subgrid dynamical friction + binding/separation merger criteria replace repositioning. Full BH histories (position, velocity, accretion) are stored on the fly — the feedstock for dual-AGN and GW papers already in the series.

3. **Accretion.** Bondi–Hoyle rate from local gas, boosted by $\alpha=100$ to compensate for unresolved ISM, capped at twice Eddington. Bolometric luminosity follows a Churazov-style radiatively efficient / inefficient split in Eddington ratio; hard X-rays use Hopkins bolometric corrections for survey comparisons.

4. **Feedback.** Two-mode AGN feedback: thermal (high Eddington) and kinetic (low Eddington), with a mass-dependent switch that becomes important around a **critical mass** $\sim 5\times 10^{8}$–$10^{9}\,M_{\odot}$. Below that scale, thermal-mode growth is efficient; above it, kinetic-mode energy injection throttles further growth. You can *see* that throttle as a bump near $10^{9}\,M_{\odot}$ in the black-hole mass function — a pile-up of black holes whose growth stalled when the kinetic mode turned on.

**Headline $z=0$ results, in physical language.**

- **Black-hole mass function:** Broad agreement with Shankar/Ueda-type observational inferences above $\sim 10^{7}\,M_{\odot}$; most black holes are inactive ($f_{\mathrm{Edd}}$ tiny); luminous subsets track brighter observational samples.
- **AGN luminosity function:** Hard X-ray LF at $z=0.4$, $0.2$, and $0$ sits near Shen and Buchner constraints in the $10^{42}$–$10^{44}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$ window, especially if radiative efficiency $\eta=0.2$ rather than $0.1$.
- **Scaling relations:** Median MBH–$M_{*}$ and MBH–$\sigma$ track empirical fits at the massive end. Low-mass end remains **overmassive** relative to some local relations (a known sim disease; Illustris/TNG show it too, often worse). High-mass end flattens where kinetic feedback clamps BH growth while galaxies keep growing — a feature the authors tie to $M_{\mathrm{crit}}$, not clearly seen yet in sparse observed samples of the heaviest systems.
- **Diversity / scatter:** Comparing to compiled observational KDEs, ASTRID covers more of the observed cloud than many peers, including $10^{6}$–$10^{8}\,M_{\odot}$ black holes in $\sim 10^{10.5}$–$10^{11}\,M_{\odot}$ galaxies that repositioning-heavy runs tend to over-accrete away. They still under-produce some undermassive BHs in the most massive galaxies and discuss volume, seeding, and missing spin physics as culprits.
- **Galaxies:** Stellar mass function is broadly okay; near the “knee” $M_{*}\sim 10^{11}\,M_{\odot}$ they underpredict abundance by up to $\sim 0.5$ dex versus GAMA — possibly kinetic-feedback critical mass set too aggressively (those galaxies also look too star-forming). With dust attenuation along metal columns, the luminosity function and SDSS $g-r$ **color bimodality** (red sequence vs blue cloud) appear.
- **Groups/clusters:** Most massive halo $M_{200c}\approx 1.8\times 10^{15}\,M_{\odot}$ (Coma-like); **seven** halos above $10^{15}\,M_{\odot}$; **9709** above $10^{13}\,M_{\odot}$. Stellar budgets (centrals, satellites, intracluster light) versus halo mass match observational trends well enough to publish power-law fits for reuse.
- **Clustering:** Power spectra and scale-dependent bias for MBHs and galaxies. **MBHs with $M_{\mathrm{BH}}\gtrsim 10^{8}\,M_{\odot}$ and galaxies with $M_{*}\gtrsim 10^{10.5}\,M_{\odot}$** behave as good large-scale-structure tracers (large bias, roughly scale-independent for $k<2\,h\,\mathrm{Mpc^{-1}}$). Wandering black holes are more biased than you might guess — a seeding-dependent prediction relevant to future GW–galaxy cross-correlations.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch — “better dynamics” ≠ resolved orbits.** ASTRID’s selling point is that black holes feel a drag force instead of being teleported. Easy misread: that the code now *integrates* realistic MBH paths down to coalescence. It does not. Drag is still a calibrated subgrid term (Tremmel/Chen-style), gravity is softened on ~kpc scales, and the last parsec before merger stays out of reach. Relative to repositioning, bookkeeping of wandering and bound pairs gets better. Absolute orbital truth does not suddenly appear.

> **Watch — hitting the classic scaling plots is a weak uniqueness test.** Matching median $M_{\mathrm{BH}}$–$M_{*}$ and $M_{\mathrm{BH}}$–$\sigma$ looks like a win for the whole accretion/feedback stack. It is only a weak win for *uniqueness*: Illustris, TNG, EAGLE, Horizon-AGN, and SIMBA land on similar medians with different knobs. Those two relations are closer to “does the throttle roughly work?” than to “this exact recipe is the right microphysics.” Scatter comparisons against published peers in this paper’s figures are useful; they are not a controlled bakeoff on shared ICs.

> **Watch — the mass ladder’s bottom rung is chosen, not discovered.** Marketing language about ~seven decades in $M_{\mathrm{BH}}$ and millions of mergers can sound like an end-to-end ab initio forecast. Seed masses (~$3\times10^{4}$–$3\times10^{5}\,h^{-1}M_{\odot}$, drawn stochastically) are an author choice aimed in part at LISA-relevant territory. Everything above the seed inherits that prior. Count the mergers; do not confuse the seed floor with a prediction from Pop-III or direct-collapse theory.

> **Watch — two features that look like data products are mostly dial fingerprints.** The BH mass-function pile-up near $10^{9}\,M_{\odot}$ tracks the kinetic-mode switch; bright AGN subsets wash it out, so faint stalled holes carry it. The GSMF dip near $M_{*}\sim10^{11}\,M_{\odot}$ is likewise a calibration bruise the authors themselves tie to feedback / star-formation tension — not a tiny plotting quirk.

# 5. What’s Genuinely New or Clever

Strip the ingredients and almost nothing is new in isolation: Bondi accretion with a boost, thermal-vs-kinetic AGN modes, FOF+SUBFIND, even ASTRID as a code family already exist in earlier papers. The clever move is **shipping one volume that is simultaneously huge, finished at $z=0$, and allergic to BH teleportation**. That product layer is what peers usually split across separate runs or never finish.

Two capabilities fall out of that product choice. First, you get a local MBH census with honest-enough scatter for dual-AGN / PTA / LISA adjacent work, including holes that are still sinking rather than glued to halo centers. Second, you can actually *define* a wandering-MBH sample and measure how it clusters — a category repositioning erases by construction, because every hole is forever parked at a potential minimum.

On top of the product layer sits one result that is more than “we finished the run”: black-hole **bias versus abundance is not monotonic** (their §6.2). Mass-selected halos and galaxies usually get more biased as you make the sample rarer. ASTRID’s MBHs go up, down, then up again. Authors’ story: intermediate abundances get diluted by a swarm of low-mass wanderers, then the rare heavy holes dominate again. If that shape survives outside ASTRID, multimessenger cross-correlations have a distinctive fingerprint to hunt; if real catalogs show a boring monotonic rise, this particular wanderer story is in trouble.

**Predictive Content Check**

1. **Falsifiable handle:** Use the paper’s own cuts — $M_{\mathrm{BH}}\gtrsim10^{8}\,M_{\odot}$ and $M_{*}\gtrsim10^{10.5}\,M_{\odot}$ as roughly scale-independent tracers for $k<2\,h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ — and treat the non-monotonic bias curve as a sharp target (monotonic real-world BH bias would fight the wanderer dilution picture). Also watch ordinary validation handles: GSMF near $10^{11}\,M_{\odot}$, hard X-ray LF vs $\eta$, occupation fractions, cluster stellar fractions. Companion GW papers already stress-test the merger catalog against PTA-scale GWB amplitudes (ASTRID tends low).

2. **Formalism load:** Silent. Equations here are engineering controls, not a new theory scaffold. Main epistemic risk is **overfitting subgrid knobs**, not decorative math.

# 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> Late-time black-hole growth is too aggressive: BHAD at $z=0$ sits **>1 dex** above observational compilations (worse than the ~0.5 dex overshoots quoted for Horizon-AGN / Illustris). **(A) Consensus** — authors say feedback is not efficient enough today. **(paper §3.5)**

> Too many light holes: $M_{\mathrm{BH}}<10^{7}\,M_{\odot}$ overshoots constraints, blamed on seed overproduction from the stochastic seed prior. Separately, dwarf / low-mass hosts still tend to carry **overmassive** BHs relative to some local relations — a familiar large-sim bruise. **(A) Consensus**. **(conclusion item 1; §3.3)**

> A corner of the scaling plane is empty: undermassive ($\lesssim10^{8}\,M_{\odot}$) holes inside $M_{*}\sim10^{11}$–$10^{11.5}\,M_{\odot}$ galaxies. Authors float too-efficient DF-driven mergers, resolution, and (via other work) missing spin-dependent feedback — without settling which dominates. **(B) Contested**. **(§3.3)**

> GSMF near $M_{*}\sim10^{11}\,M_{\odot}$ is low by up to ~0.5 dex vs GAMA. Story on offer: kinetic-feedback critical mass set too high. That story was not proven by a re-run with a lower threshold. **(B) Contested**. **(§4.1)**

# 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

Zhou et al. open the present-day chapter of ASTRID: a gigantic hydrodynamical universe box that finally reaches $z=0$ with enough resolution to track black holes from $\sim 10^{4}\,M_{\odot}$ seeds up to $\sim 10^{11}\,M_{\odot}$ monsters. The scientific bet is that **better black-hole traffic rules** — dynamical friction and binding-based mergers instead of teleportation to the potential minimum — produce a more honest joint census of galaxies and MBHs, which surveys and gravitational-wave experiments can both use.

On black holes, the mass function, AGN luminosity function, and median MBH–$M_{*}$ / MBH–$\sigma$ relations land near observational anchors at the well-sampled massive end, while the kinetic-feedback threshold imprints a characteristic stall near $10^{9}\,M_{\odot}$. The distinctive claim is diversity: ASTRID’s cloud of points covers more of the observed scatter than several famous repositioning-based runs, including a population of relatively light black holes in mid-mass galaxies. Weak spots remain honest and quantitative: BHAD overpredicted by more than a dex at $z=0$, too many low-mass seeds, overmassive holes in dwarfs, a thin spot for undermassive holes in the biggest galaxies, and a galaxy stellar mass function that sags near the knee.

On galaxies and clusters, dust-attenuated luminosities and $g-r$ colors recover the familiar red/blue split; group and cluster stellar budgets scale with halo mass in line with observations; seven Coma-class systems sit in the box. On large-scale structure, sufficiently massive black holes and galaxies are well-behaved biased tracers; the standout forward-looking result is the **non-monotonic** black-hole bias-versus-abundance curve attributed to wandering low-mass holes — forecasting material for how future GW catalogs should cluster relative to galaxy surveys.

The interpretive choice for wiki use: treat this less as “a new law of MBH growth” and more as **infrastructure validation** — a public, dynamics-aware $z=0$ laboratory that links dual AGNs, PTA/LISA forecasts, and cluster astrophysics inside one volume. Its most useful content is the balanced report card: what works (coevolution scatter, cluster stellar content, massive tracers) against what doesn’t yet (late-time accretion rates, seed excess, GSMF knee).

**Where I’m least confident in this analysis:** I am least sure how much of the non-monotonic bias shape is forced by the wandering population versus how much is an artifact of ASTRID’s particular seeding + DF settings — the paper’s discussion is clearer on the qualitative picture than on a quantitative split of those contributions, and I did not rebuild Figure 22 from particle data. I am also second-hand on the precise DF kernel and $M_{\mathrm{dyn}}$ implementation details, which this article sketches and earlier methods papers own.

# 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. ASTRID at $z=0$ is a rare combo: enormous hydro particle count, present-day arrival, and black holes that sink by dynamical friction instead of being teleported — built as shared infrastructure for galaxy demographics *and* GW source catalogs.

2. Median MBH–galaxy scaling relations look observationally decent at the massive end, and scatter/diversity is a real selling point versus many peer sims — but the authors are candid that late-time black-hole accretion runs ~10× too high, seeds are overproduced, and the galaxy mass function dips near $10^{11}\,M_{\odot}$.

3. For structure tracers, stick to heavier objects: black holes $\gtrsim 10^{8}\,M_{\odot}$ and galaxies $\gtrsim 10^{10.5}\,M_{\odot}$ track the matter field cleanly — while wandering holes remain a seeding-sensitive bonus probe.

# 9. Shorter Summary

Cosmologists build digital universes to watch galaxies and black holes form. ASTRID is one of the largest such universes yet run all the way to the present day: hundreds of millions of light-years on a side, hundreds of billions of particles, and a black-hole population spanning from lightweight seeds to hundred-billion-solar-mass giants. Unlike many big simulations that cheat by constantly teleporting each black hole to its galaxy’s center, ASTRID approximates the real gravitational drag that makes black holes spiral inward. That change yields more realistic variety in black-hole masses at a given galaxy mass — closer to what telescopes see — while still matching many local benchmarks for galaxy abundances, colors, and giant clusters. The same box supplies clustering statistics useful for mapping large-scale structure and feeds companion forecasts for gravitational-wave observatories. It is not perfect: small galaxies still tend to have oversized black holes, and one slice of the galaxy mass spectrum runs a bit low. Even so, ASTRID at redshift zero is less a single headline discovery than a calibrated public laboratory linking black-hole growth, galaxy evolution, and future multimessenger surveys.

### Q: What do “dark matter only feels gravity” and “subgrid models” actually mean — and why can’t you resolve a black-hole accretion disk in a 370 Mpc box?

That sentence is doing three jobs at once. Unpack them separately.

**1. Dark matter vs baryons in the simulation.**

In ASTRID (and almost every cosmological hydro run), there are two big particle families:

- **Dark matter particles** are treated as collisionless mass. They attract everything through gravity and feel gravity back, but they do not push with pressure, radiate light, form molecules, or explode as supernovae. In code terms: each step, update their velocities from the gravitational field, move them, done.
- **Baryons** (gas, and later stars that form from gas) also feel gravity, **plus** hydrodynamics: pressure gradients, shocks, cooling, heating. Gas can become dense enough to form stars; stars can dump energy and metals back into the gas; black-hole particles can accrete nearby gas and inject feedback energy.

So “dark matter only feels gravity” is not a philosophy claim about the real Universe’s dark sector microphysics — it is an **engineering statement about what equations that particle species obeys in the code**. Baryons run a much richer (and more expensive) physics package on top of the same gravity solver.

**Analogy:** dark matter is like trucks that only follow the road network (gravity). Baryons are trucks that also have engines, brakes, traffic lights, accidents, and cargo transfers (hydro + cooling + feedback). Same map; very different update rules.

**2. What “resolution limit” means here.**

The simulation divides a ~370 Mpc cube among hundreds of billions of particles. Even so, each gas particle in ASTRID represents roughly a million solar masses of gas, and gravity is softened on ~kiloparsec scales. Anything physically smaller or faster than that grid can honestly represent is **unresolved**.

A real accretion disk around a $10^{8}\,M_{\odot}$ black hole is tiny on cosmic scales: the interesting orbits sit at astronomical-unit to light-day sizes — many orders of magnitude below a kiloparsec. You would need absurdly more particles (and tiny timesteps) to evolve that disk *inside* a box big enough to also grow Coma-like clusters. Computers do not allow both at once today.

**Worked scale intuition:**  
1 pc ≈ $3\times 10^{16}$ m. ASTRID’s softening ~1.5 $h^{-1}$ ckpc is thousands of times larger than a whole solar system, never mind an AGN disk. The box is ~$10^{8}$–$10^{9}$ pc across. You are asking one calculation to cover **disk scales and survey scales** simultaneously — that dynamic range is the whole problem.

**3. What a subgrid model is.**

A **subgrid model** is a recipe that says: “I cannot resolve process X, but I know (or guess) how X *should* affect the scales I *can* resolve — so inject that effect by hand.”

Examples in ASTRID’s family of runs:

| Unresolved physics | Subgrid stand-in |
| --- | --- |
| Star-forming molecular clouds | Density/temperature threshold → convert gas mass to star particles at some rate |
| Supernova blast waves (parsec-scale) | Deposit thermal/kinetic energy and metals into neighboring gas particles |
| Black-hole accretion disk + ISM multiphase structure | Bondi–Hoyle formula from *local smoothed* gas density/sound speed, often with a boost factor ($\alpha=100$ in ASTRID) because the smooth density underestimates true clumpy accretion |
| AGN jets / winds | Thermal or kinetic energy injection into nearby gas (the two-mode feedback) |
| Dynamical friction from unresolved stars | Extra drag force on the BH particle (the Tremmel-style term) |

So when the analysis said “nobody resolves an accretion disk… you *parameterize* it,” it means: the code never integrates Navier–Stokes on the disk; it estimates an accretion rate from coarse local gas properties and a formula with tunable factors, then grows $M_{\mathrm{BH}}$ and dumps feedback energy accordingly.

**Breaks when:** you treat a successful $z=0$ match (e.g. MBH–$M_{*}$ relation) as proof that the unresolved disk physics is uniquely correct. Many different subgrid recipes can be tuned to the same local scaling relations. That is why papers like Zhou et al. still argue about scatter, kinetic-feedback critical mass, and seeding — those are fights *among* parameterizations.

**How this connects back to ASTRID’s pitch:** their “better MBH dynamics” claim is itself a subgrid upgrade (dynamical friction instead of repositioning). It does not suddenly resolve disks; it improves one particular unresolved piece (orbital decay) that repositioning was faking.
