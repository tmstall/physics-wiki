## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Stop Faking the Ripples: First Lensing Predictions from Real Fuzzy-Dark-Matter Wave Halos**

Every earlier claim that ultralight "fuzzy" dark matter explains the milliarcsecond-level misplacement of quasar images relied on statistical stand-ins for its wave ripples. This paper runs the actual wave equation on 1,000 halos and finds they match the famous radio quad HS 0810+2554 better than the stand-in does. The catch, which the paper's own appendix shows, is that much of the win comes from the halo's smooth overall shape rather than from the ripples themselves.

---

## 2. Big-Picture Context

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is a computational-methods Letter. It introduces full-wave (Schrödinger–Poisson) forward modeling of strong-lensing image positions for fuzzy dark matter (FDM), and tests it on a single benchmark lens. The stakes are methodological: whether the Gaussian-random-field (GRF) shortcut used by every previous FDM lensing study is accurate enough to draw conclusions about the dark-matter particle.

The problem. If dark matter is an ultralight boson with mass around $10^{-22}$ eV, its quantum wavelength is galactic in size: roughly 100–200 pc inside a Milky-Way-mass halo. The halo then acts less like a smooth cloud of particles and more like a single giant standing-wave pattern. You get a dense "soliton" core in the middle, surrounded by a halo that is **granulated**: blobs of constructive and destructive interference about one wavelength across, which churn continuously. In strong gravitational lensing, a background quasar appears as four images around a foreground galaxy, and image positions can be measured to about a milliarcsecond with VLBI radio interferometry. At that precision, granules near an image nudge its position by a few to tens of mas. Smooth lens models, meanwhile, routinely leave position residuals of about 5–10 mas that measurement error cannot explain.

The benchmark. HS 0810+2554 is a quadruply lensed radio-quiet quasar in which e-MERLIN and European VLBI resolved **two compact jet components**, giving **eight images** with precise astrometry (Hartley et al. 2019). Smooth elliptical models fail to reproduce them. Amruth et al. 2023 (*Nature Astronomy*; two of the same authors are on this paper) argued that FDM granules, modeled as a 2D GRF laid on top of a smooth profile, account for the anomalies in this system and others. Miller & Williams 2025 (*MNRAS* 543, 3952) replied that a cold-dark-matter lens with angular multipoles (lopsidedness, triangularity, boxiness) plus external shear reproduces the positions to about 1.6 mas rms, "so fuzzy dark matter need not be invoked." A recent preprint (arXiv:2608.15554) takes a third route and addresses the same anomalies with CDM subhalos.

What this paper adds. Every earlier FDM lensing prediction used the GRF shortcut, meaning a 2D random field with the right mean and two-point statistics. None projected a halo that had actually been evolved with the wave equation. This paper closes that gap: 1,000 independent Schrödinger–Poisson halos, each projected along 1,000 viewing directions and forward-lensed, compared against 1,000 GRF realizations and a best-fit smooth NFW model.

**Prior Belief Check:** The result is **incremental and confirmatory within the FDM-lensing program**, not a field-shifting surprise. Full-wave halos giving lensing statistics similar to, and somewhat better than, GRF is what most practitioners would expect. The paper's own appendix (A.3) shows that the projected fluctuations are nearly Gaussian and have about the same amplitude and scale as the GRF. The paper does **not** move the larger debate. It never compares against the CDM-with-multipoles or CDM-subhalo explanations that compete for the same system. Its preferred mass, $m_\psi = 10^{-22}$ eV, also sits in a range that Lyman-α forest and dwarf-galaxy constraints (which the paper cites in its introduction) are widely read as disfavoring for FDM as 100% of dark matter. To an expert, the most interesting finding is probably the one the authors put in Appendix A.5: a GRF built on the *wave halo's smooth component* closes much of the gap. That points to the global mass distribution, not wave-specific non-Gaussian structure, as the main driver.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is a **single-group result with no independent replication**. The lineage also overlaps with the GRF study it benchmarks against (Amruth and Lim co-author both). Independent confirmation would look like this: a different group, with a different Schrödinger–Poisson code, including baryons and cosmological halo assembly, applies the *same* search freedom to FDM, GRF, and CDM-with-multipoles/subhalo models on HS 0810+2554, and then on other VLBI quads. Only that head-to-head could show whether FDM is actually preferred, rather than just better than a smooth NFW.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

### 3.1 Fuzzy dark matter and the de Broglie wavelength

FDM treats dark matter as one coherent classical wave field ψ, whose squared magnitude is the mass density. It obeys the Schrödinger equation with self-gravity supplied by Poisson's equation (the "S–P" system). The only new length scale is the de Broglie wavelength, and the paper's Eq. (1) gives it:

$$
\lambda_{\rm dB} \approx 150\left(\frac{10^{-22}\,\mathrm{eV}}{m_\psi}\right)\left(\frac{M_{\rm h}}{10^{12}\,M_\odot}\right)^{-1/3}\ \mathrm{pc}
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$\lambda_{\rm dB}$ : characteristic interference ("granule") size in the halo (parsecs)
$m_\psi$ : mass of the dark-matter particle (eV)
$M_{\rm h}$ : total halo mass (solar masses)

**What this actually means:** lighter particles make bigger granules, and heavier halos make slightly smaller ones, because the particles move faster in deeper wells. Worked numbers for this paper's halo, with $M_{\rm h} = 4\times10^{11}\,M_\odot$ so that $(0.4)^{-1/3} \approx 1.36$:

- $m_\psi = 10^{-22}$ eV → λ ≈ 150 × 1.36 ≈ **204 pc**
- $m_\psi = 10^{-23}$ eV → λ ≈ **2 kpc**, ten times coarser

At the lens redshift (z = 0.89), 1 mas corresponds to about **8.0 pc** (Planck cosmology, computed for this analysis). A 204 pc granule therefore spans about 25 mas, which is the same order as the 5–12 mas image-position anomalies. That match of scales is the whole reason FDM is a lensing suspect.

> **Analogy:** Many waves with random phases add up the way many unsynchronized writers hit a shared memory bank. Some addresses get hammered by constructive overlap and some go nearly quiet. The hot and cold regions have a characteristic "stride" set by the wavelength, not by any single writer.
>
> **Breaks when:** you ask about time. Granules in a real FDM halo form, dissolve, and re-form on dynamical timescales, and they have a self-gravitating soliton core at the center. The memory-bank picture has no counterpart to a pattern that gravitationally feeds back on its own layout.

### 3.2 Why projected fluctuations are ~10%, not ~100% (worked example)

In 3D, FDM granules are order-unity fluctuations: density swings from near zero to about twice the mean. Two unit waves already show this. Adding $1 + e^{i\phi}$ gives a squared magnitude that runs from 0 (φ = π) to 4 (φ = 0), with a mean of 2. Lensing, however, only sees the **projected** column density along the line of sight, and projection averages many roughly independent granules.

The paper's Appendix A.6 gives the numbers: a ~30 kpc sightline through ~200 pc granules crosses about **150** independent coherence elements. Averaging N independent unit-variance contributions shrinks the fractional scatter by $1/\sqrt{N}$:

$$
\frac{\delta\kappa}{\kappa} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{150}} \approx 0.082
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$\kappa$ : convergence, i.e. projected surface mass density in units of the critical lensing density (dimensionless)
$\delta\kappa$ : local fluctuation of κ about its smooth value
$N$ : number of independent granules along the sightline

**What this actually means:** this is the timing-jitter argument you already know. Average 150 independent noisy runs and the jitter drops by about 12×. The ~8% estimate lands right on the **~10% fractional convergence fluctuation** the simulations actually show (Fig. 1; Fig. A2, left panel). By the central limit theorem, it also explains why the projected residuals look almost Gaussian (Fig. A3). The same argument sets its own ceiling: N = 150 is finite and the granules are not identically weighted (the density envelope falls steeply with radius), so the Gaussianity is approximate. Any non-Gaussian signal would sit in the tails.

### 3.3 Strong lensing and position anomalies

A lens maps a source position β to image positions θ through the lens equation, $\boldsymbol\beta = \boldsymbol\theta - \boldsymbol\alpha(\boldsymbol\theta)$, where α is the deflection set by the projected mass. For a quad, four θ solutions exist. You fit a smooth mass model (NFW or power-law ellipse, plus shear) and compare predicted with observed positions. The paper's Eq. (2) defines the **position anomaly** A as the rms offset across the four images:

$$
A = \left[\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{{\rm obs},i} - \boldsymbol{x}_{{\rm pred},i}\right|^2\right]^{1/2}
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$\boldsymbol{x}_{{\rm obs},i}$ : observed sky position of image i (mas)
$\boldsymbol{x}_{{\rm pred},i}$ : model-predicted position of image i (mas)
$A$ : rms image-position mismatch (mas)

**What this actually means:** a root-mean-square residual across four probes. Worked toy: offsets of 3, 1, 2 and 0 mas give $A = \sqrt{(9+1+4+0)/4} = \sqrt{3.5} \approx 1.9$ mas. The HS 0810+2554 astrometric errors are about 1 mas, so the paper treats A < 3 mas as roughly a 3σ match. A smooth model's ~10–14 mas is badly off.

> **Analogy:** the smooth lens model is a low-order regression through four data points. A is the rms residual. If the residual is 10× the measurement error, some structure is missing from the model.
>
> **Breaks when:** you treat the residual as diagnostic of *which* structure is missing. In lensing, many physically different perturbations produce similar position shifts: angular multipoles in the main galaxy, CDM subhalos, line-of-sight halos, baryonic disks or bars, FDM granules. Regression residuals tell you the model order is too low. They don't tell you which higher-order term the universe chose. That degeneracy is exactly the unresolved HS 0810+2554 fight.

### 3.4 The Gaussian-random-field shortcut

A Gaussian random field is fully specified by its mean and its two-point correlation (equivalently, its power spectrum). Amruth et al. 2023 model FDM lensing as: smooth NFW convergence + a 2D GRF with correlation length λ_dB and an amplitude that tracks the local density. It's cheap. Solving S–P in 3D at sub-100 pc resolution for 1,000 halos is not.

> **Concept:** A GRF reproduces the *mean* and *two-point* statistics of the true FDM projected density, but by construction nothing beyond them: no three-point or higher correlations, no coupling between fluctuations and the actual halo shape. It is also always laid on top of an *externally assumed* smooth profile (here, a best-fit NFW), so both the baseline and the fluctuation amplitude are inputs, not outputs.
>
> **Vehicle:** A synthetic-workload generator for a memory-system benchmark. It is tuned to match a real application's average miss rate and reuse-distance histogram, and it runs on top of a baseline access pattern you chose by hand. The alternative is replaying the full recorded trace of the real application, which carries its phase behavior, bursty correlations, and actual working-set layout.
>
> **Analogy:** GRF ↔ synthetic generator. Mean + two-point statistics ↔ miss rate + reuse-distance histogram. Assumed NFW baseline ↔ hand-chosen baseline access pattern. S–P wave halo ↔ full recorded trace. Higher-order correlations and mode coupling ↔ bursty, phase-correlated behavior the histogram can't encode.
>
> **Breaks when:** you forget that the "real trace" here is itself a simulation of an idealized halo (five colliding Gaussian wave packets in a 40 kpc box, no baryons), not a recording of the actual lens galaxy. The vehicle's full-trace replay is ground truth by definition. The S–P halo is only *more complete* than the GRF, not *true*.

### 3.5 Procrustes alignment and pairwise-distance invariants (tier-1, colleague speed)

The paper compares simulated and observed four-image configurations in a way that ignores where the configuration sits and how it is rotated on the sky. It uses two tools. (i) The six sorted pairwise distances between the four images act as a rotation- and translation-invariant fingerprint, which the paper uses to pick the best source position. (ii) **Orthogonal Procrustes** (an SVD of the 2×2 cross-covariance of centered point sets, $R = UV^{\rm T}$, reflections allowed) finds the optimal rotation/reflection plus translation before the residual A is computed. Four points in 2D have 8 coordinates. Removing 3 rigid-motion degrees of freedom leaves 5 intrinsic ones, so six distances overdetermine the shape. You'll recognize this as point-cloud registration and canonical hashing.

> **Analogy:** Sorted pairwise distances are a canonical hash of a shape. Procrustes is the register-alignment pass that lines two shapes up before you diff them.
>
> **Breaks when:** you forget that the hash discards labels, since sorting distances loses which image is which, and allowing reflections treats mirror configurations as identical. Both are defensible here: the observed configuration has no absolute orientation, and a mirrored halo is an equally valid realization. They do, however, add freedom to the matching step, which matters for §6.

**Central analogy for this paper:** *synthetic benchmark trace vs. full recorded workload replay*

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### 4.1 Building 1,000 wave halos

They solve the S–P equations (Eq. A1) with a global Fourier pseudospectral method, using a split-step "kick–drift–kick" integrator (Eq. A7). This is the same Strang splitting pattern as a symplectic N-body leapfrog. The kinetic step is exact in Fourier space, and the potential step is an exact phase rotation in real space. The time step is limited by whichever phase rotation is faster (Eq. A9).

Setup: a periodic 40 kpc box, 512³ grid (cell size $\Delta x = 40\,000/512 \approx 78$ pc), total dark-matter mass $4\times10^{11}\,M_\odot$ (chosen to reproduce the lens's Einstein radius), and evolution for about 3.3 Gyr, under the ~6.3 Gyr cosmic age at z = 0.89. The initial conditions are **five randomly placed 3D Gaussian wave packets** that fall together, merge, and relax. That is the standard idealized "soliton + granular halo" construction (Schwabe et al. 2016; Mocz et al. 2017), not a cosmological zoom-in. They run 1,000 independent realizations at $m_\psi = 10^{-22}$ eV, plus a set at $10^{-23}$ eV.

The relaxed halos look right (Fig. 2). Each has a soliton core that fits the standard soliton profile, and an outer envelope that fits NFW beyond the Einstein radius. The core is visibly larger at $10^{-23}$ eV, as expected from the tenfold larger wavelength. The projected maps (Fig. 1) show ~10% granular fluctuations wrinkling the critical curve, the locus of high magnification where lensed images form.

**Resolution check (worked):** λ ≈ 204 pc from Eq. (1), or ≈ 120 pc as the paper adopts in Appendix A.3, spread over 78 pc cells gives **~1.5–2.6 cells per wavelength**. The paper says "several grid cells per de Broglie wavelength." Nyquist sampling needs 2. This is marginal, and it is worst in the soliton core, where local velocities are highest and local wavelengths shortest. The paper reports no convergence test at 1024³. Under-resolution in a spectral code tends to alias or suppress small-scale power rather than inflate it, so the likely bias would *weaken* the granule signal, not fake it. It's still an unquantified systematic (§6).

### 4.2 Forward-modeling lensed images from a 3D field

This is the Letter's genuinely new pipeline (Appendix A.2, Fig. A1):

1. **Principal axis.** Compute the mass-weighted second-moment tensor of the 3D density and take its largest eigenvector.
2. **Viewing directions.** Fix the line of sight along z and rotate the halo instead, sampling **1,000 orientations** of the principal axis in a quarter-circle of the x–z plane (θ from 0 to π/2). Other orientations are argued to be equivalent up to in-plane rotation or reflection, which Procrustes absorbs.
3. **Project and lens.** Integrate the density along z to get $\Sigma(x,y)$ and convergence $\kappa = \Sigma/\Sigma_{\rm crit}$. Then use *lenstronomy* to solve the lens equation for **10,000 source positions**.
4. **Pick the best source.** For each orientation, keep the source whose image configuration has the smallest sorted-pairwise-distance mismatch with the observed quad.
5. **Score.** Procrustes-align that configuration to the observed one, and compute the anomaly A (Eq. A27). Across orientations, keep the best (orientation, source) pair for that halo.

Each halo therefore contributes the *best* of about 10⁷ trial configurations (10³ orientations × 10⁴ sources), and Fig. 3 histograms these per-halo minima.

### 4.3 The results

**Anomaly distributions (Fig. 3):**

| Model | Radio1 median A | Radio2 median A | Best cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| S–P wave halos, $10^{-22}$ eV | ~12 mas | ~6 mas | some realizations < 3 mas (≈3σ) |
| GRF on best-fit NFW, $10^{-22}$ eV | ~12 mas | ~16–17 mas | best ~7 mas |
| Smooth best-fit NFW (single model) | ~14 mas | ~10.5 mas | — |
| S–P wave halos, $10^{-23}$ eV | ~50 mas | ~50 mas | — (≈4× worse) |

The wave halos give a narrower distribution with a lower floor than the GRF, most strikingly for radio2. The best realizations reach A < 3 mas, which neither the GRF (~7 mas) nor NFW (~12 mas) manages. The lighter $10^{-23}$ eV particle gives anomalies about 4× too large: the granules are ten times bigger and push images too far. So the position data genuinely discriminate between particle masses at fixed halo mass.

**Image clouds (Fig. 4):** for 300 best-matching halos, predicted image positions of both S–P and GRF form "clouds" overlapping all eight observed 3σ ellipses. The S–P clouds are more compact around the observations. The smooth NFW prediction sits ≳5σ from all but one image.

**The appendix twist (A.3–A.6).** This is the part to read slowly.

- **A.3:** the S–P and GRF projected fluctuations have broadly the *same* annular rms amplitude near the Einstein radius (Fig. A2), and the S–P residuals pass Gaussianity tests: skewness near 0, kurtosis near 3, Kolmogorov–Smirnov p ≈ 0.86 (Fig. A3). The wave-evolved fluctuations are, to the tested order, Gaussian.
- **A.5:** they build a GRF using the S–P halo's **own smooth elliptical component** and fluctuation-amplitude map instead of an analytic NFW. The GRF's radio2 median then improves from **~16 to ~12 mas** and its floor from ~7 to ~6 mas (Fig. A5), and the central over-concentration in the residual maps disappears.
- **A.6:** the authors conclude that the S–P advantage comes from (a) the self-consistent *total* mass distribution, including a soliton core that NFW lacks, and (b) possibly small non-Gaussian tail structure that the two-point statistics can't capture, which they leave for future higher-order-statistics work.

Put through the central analogy: once you swap the synthetic generator's hand-picked baseline for the real trace's baseline, most of the performance gap closes. The residual gap is where the bursty higher-order behavior *might* matter, and the paper doesn't isolate it.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "no best-fitting procedure" (paper §3) means the wave halos weren't tuned to the data. **The paper actually** fixes halo mass to reproduce the Einstein radius, then *selects* the best of ~10⁷ (orientation × source position) trials per halo, and histograms those minima. That is a discrete search, which is functionally a fit over a projected-shape parameter space. It is a different kind of fit from NFW's continuous parameter minimization, but it is not the absence of one. The paper doesn't say whether the GRF realizations, which are intrinsically 2D, got equivalent orientation freedom. If they didn't, part of the S–P advantage could be search-space size rather than physics.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the S–P advantage over GRF shows that wave-specific, non-Gaussian interference structure matters for lensing. **The paper actually** shows the projected fluctuations are close to Gaussian with GRF-matched amplitude (A.3), and that feeding the S–P smooth halo into a GRF recovers much of the gain (A.5). The dominant driver appears to be the global mass distribution (soliton plus non-NFW shape). Non-Gaussian contributions are proposed but not isolated.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes this strengthens FDM over CDM as *the* explanation for HS 0810+2554's anomalies. **The paper actually** benchmarks only against smooth NFW and GRF-FDM. It cites, but never compares against, the CDM + multipoles model (Miller & Williams 2025, ~1.6 mas rms) or subhalo models, both of which also fit this system.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes one simulated halo reproduces all eight images within 3σ. **The paper actually** reports that some realizations reach A < 3 mas "for both radio sources," where A is computed per radio component over its four images. It reaches the all-eight-images claim by an independence argument: granules are smaller than image separations, so realizations "can in theory be combined." That argument concerns the probability that one halo matches all eight at once, and the paper doesn't demonstrate that such a realization exists.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**1. First lensing predictions from projected full-wave FDM halos (new to the field).** Every earlier FDM lensing study, for position, flux-ratio, or time-delay anomalies (Chan et al. 2020; Laroche et al. 2022; Amruth et al. 2023; Hou et al. 2026), used the GRF approximation. Running 1,000 S–P halos to lens-ready form and closing the loop to observed VLBI astrometry is a real computational step. It also yields a direct, apples-to-apples test of whether the GRF shortcut is good enough. The paper's answer, stated fairly: good for moderate precision; for high precision, the smooth baseline matters more than GRF users assumed.

**2. Orientation-free, coordinate-free image matching (new to this application).** Conventional nearest-neighbor matching penalizes a simulated configuration for sitting at a different sky position or rotation, even when its intrinsic geometry is identical. Sorted pairwise distances for source selection, followed by Procrustes alignment for scoring, removes that artificial penalty. The paper shows it produces narrower GRF anomaly distributions with lower floors than the standard method. The linear algebra is textbook (Schönemann 1966). Applying it to compare a simulated halo that has no preferred sky frame against an observed quad is a clean, reusable idea.

**Worth naming, though not claimed as a headline:** the A.5 control experiment, a GRF built on the wave halo's own smooth component, is the most informative single test in the paper. It decomposes "wave halo beats GRF" into a baseline effect and a residual fluctuation effect.

### Predictive Content Check

1. **Falsifiable handle:** **mostly retrodictive, with one real discriminating handle.** The main result reproduces *already-measured* image positions and compares models by how well they do so. The genuine falsifiable content is the **particle-mass scaling of the anomaly distribution**. At fixed halo mass, $10^{-22}$ eV predicts median anomalies of ~6–12 mas with a tail reaching < 3 mas, while $10^{-23}$ eV predicts ~50 mas, which the data reject. This generalizes to every VLBI-resolved quad: at $10^{-22}$ eV, *every* such lens should show residuals of ~5–15 mas relative to its best smooth model, with a distribution shape matching Fig. 3a. Lenses that are systematically clean at the ~1 mas level after multipole modeling would count against it. One implication follows directly and the paper doesn't state it: FDM masses at the Lyman-α-favored bound (≳ 2×10⁻²⁰ eV) would shrink λ_dB about 200-fold, to ~1 pc, making granule-induced position anomalies negligible. **FDM can explain these anomalies only in a mass window other probes already disfavor.** Flux ratios, time delays, and arc morphology are named as future predictions but not yet made.
2. **Formalism load:** not triggered. The simulation and lensing machinery plainly generates every number.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**L1. The favored particle mass sits in tension with independent constraints.** Lyman-α forest analyses (Iršič et al. 2017; Rogers & Peiris 2021, m ≳ 2×10⁻²⁰ eV) and dwarf-galaxy dynamics (Dalal & Kravtsov 2022) disfavor $m_\psi \sim 10^{-22}$ eV for FDM as all of the dark matter. The paper cites these in its introduction but never reconciles them with its preferred mass. **(A) Consensus** — multiple independent probes converge on this bound, though FDM as a sub-dominant component, or systematics in the Lyman-α modeling, keep a minority position alive. **(broader literature)**

**L2. No baryons.** The halos are pure dark matter, yet inside the Einstein radius of a massive lens galaxy, stars typically contribute a large fraction of the projected mass and shape the macro-model. The effect of baryons on the soliton, the granules, and the lensing predictions is untested. **(A) Consensus** — the authors list baryonic components as explicit future work. **(paper §4)**

**L3. Idealized, non-cosmological halos.** Five merging Gaussian wave packets in a periodic 40 kpc box, a single halo mass, and 3.3 Gyr of isolated evolution. There is no cosmological accretion history, no environment, and no line-of-sight structure, and the box is much smaller than a real halo's virial radius. The halos' shapes are therefore not tied to the real lens galaxy. **(A) Consensus** — this is the standard idealized construction in the field, and the paper flags larger boxes and more efficient methods as future work. **(paper §4, A.1)**

**L4. "No best-fitting" understates the selection freedom, and the search spaces may be asymmetric.** Each halo's reported A is the minimum over ~10⁷ orientation × source trials. The paper doesn't document equal orientation freedom for the 2D GRF realizations. A larger search space alone narrows and lowers a distribution of minima. **(C) Speculative** — this is my reading of the methods as described; the authors may have matched search effort in ways the Letter doesn't spell out. **(analyst inference)**

**L5. Comparison set omits the live competitors.** The paper beats smooth NFW and NFW-based GRF but doesn't test CDM + multipoles, which reaches ~1.6 mas rms on this same system (Miller & Williams 2025), or CDM subhalos (arXiv:2608.15554). Until they are compared at equal flexibility, "wave FDM fits HS 0810+2554" says nothing about FDM vs. CDM. **(B) Contested** — whether the multipole solution is physically plausible, or itself an overfit with too many free parameters, is an active disagreement in the literature. **(broader literature)**

**L6. Marginal grid resolution with no convergence test.** A 78 pc cell against a 120–204 pc de Broglie wavelength gives ~1.5–2.6 cells per wavelength, near or below Nyquist, especially in the soliton core. Positional shifts of ~3 mas correspond to ~24 pc in the lens plane, well below one cell. Deflections are integrated quantities, so this isn't necessarily fatal, but no 1024³ check is reported. **(C) Speculative** — my estimate from the paper's own Eq. (1) and grid parameters; the direction of any bias is more likely suppression than inflation of the signal. **(analyst inference)**

**L7. One lens, positions only, and the non-Gaussian claim is not demonstrated.** A single system tested on image positions only, with no flux ratios, time delays, or arcs. The proposed higher-order (three- and four-point) contribution to the S–P advantage is suggested, not measured. **(A) Consensus** — the authors explicitly defer these to follow-up work. **(paper §4, A.6)**

**12–24 month agenda:** (i) add baryons and cosmological assembly; (ii) run the same Procrustes pipeline on CDM + multipole and CDM-subhalo ensembles at matched search freedom; (iii) extend to flux-ratio anomalies, where FDM's small-scale magnification perturbations should be more distinctive than position shifts; (iv) apply the method to the growing set of VLBI-resolved quads; (v) run the bispectrum/trispectrum analysis to isolate any genuine wave-specific signal beyond the smooth-baseline effect.

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

**Summary.** Fuzzy dark matter, an ultralight boson around $10^{-22}$ eV, turns galaxy halos into giant standing-wave patterns: a dense soliton core surrounded by granules a couple of hundred parsecs across. Projected along a line of sight, the granules become ~10% ripples in the lensing mass, enough to push quasar images by several to tens of milliarcseconds. Previous work used a statistical stand-in for these ripples (a Gaussian random field on top of a smooth NFW halo) and argued FDM explains the ~10 mas image-position mismatches in HS 0810+2554, a quad whose two radio jet components give eight precisely measured images.

This paper replaces the stand-in with the real thing. The authors evolve 1,000 halos with the wave equation, project each along 1,000 viewing directions, lens 10,000 background source positions through each projection, and score the best match to the observed image geometry with an alignment method that ignores sky position and orientation. At $10^{-22}$ eV, the wave halos give median anomalies of about 12 and 6 mas for the two radio components, better than the GRF and better than a smooth NFW fit. The best realizations reach under 3 mas, which is roughly the measurement-error level. At $10^{-23}$ eV the anomalies are four times too large, so image positions do constrain the particle mass.

The appendix complicates the headline. The wave-evolved fluctuations turn out to be almost Gaussian, with about the same amplitude as the GRF's. And when the authors rebuild the GRF on the wave halo's own smooth shape instead of NFW, most of the GRF's disadvantage vanishes. The wave halo wins mainly because its overall mass distribution, soliton core included, is a better lens model than NFW. Any contribution from wave-specific structure beyond the Gaussian description is left for future work.

**Explanation of the framing.** I framed the paper around the synthetic-trace-vs-full-replay analogy because its real contribution is diagnostic: it tests the shortcut the whole FDM-lensing literature relies on. The key interpretive choice was to give the Appendix A.5 result equal weight with the headline, because it changes what "wave simulations do better" means. The other framing choice was to keep the FDM-vs-CDM question explicitly outside the paper's scope. The paper never engages the CDM-with-multipoles fit that reaches ~1.6 mas on this same lens, and its preferred particle mass is disfavored by other probes. The take-away: the paper shows GRF is a decent but baseline-sensitive approximation, and offers a reusable forward-modeling pipeline. It does not show that HS 0810+2554 favors fuzzy dark matter.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** whether the GRF comparison realizations were given search freedom equivalent to the S–P halos' 1,000 viewing orientations. The Letter describes the orientation search only for the 3D fields, and my L4 concern rests on that gap in the description. If the authors matched effective search effort in a way not spelled out, L4 weakens substantially. A secondary uncertainty: my λ_dB ≈ 204 pc comes from the paper's Eq. (1), while its appendix adopts ~120 pc, so the cells-per-wavelength figure in L6 spans a range rather than a single value.

---

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **Real wave halos lens a bit better than fake ones, but mostly because of their shape.** Simulating fuzzy dark matter with the actual wave equation fits a famous eight-image radio quasar better than the statistical shortcut does. Much of that gain comes from the halo's overall mass distribution, not from the wave ripples.

2. **The ripples are nearly Gaussian after projection, so the shortcut is roughly right.** Averaging ~150 granules along a sightline shrinks 100% density swings to ~10% lensing ripples, and the central limit theorem makes them look almost Gaussian. That's why the cheap approximation works for moderate-precision studies.

3. **This result can't settle fuzzy vs. cold dark matter.** The favored particle mass is one that Lyman-α and dwarf-galaxy data already disfavor, and a cold-dark-matter lens with angular multipoles fits this same system to about 1.6 mas. The deciding test is a head-to-head comparison at matched flexibility, which hasn't been run.

## 9. Shorter Summary

If dark matter is made of extremely light particles, around $10^{-22}$ eV ("fuzzy" dark matter), its quantum waves are hundreds of light-years long. A galaxy's dark halo then becomes a churning interference pattern: a dense core surrounded by blobs of higher and lower density. When a distant quasar is lensed into multiple images by such a galaxy, these blobs should nudge the images by tiny amounts, a few thousandths of an arcsecond. Radio telescopes can measure shifts that small.

The quasar HS 0810+2554 is the showcase. Its two radio jet spots appear as eight images, and simple smooth lens models miss their positions by about ten times the measurement error. Earlier studies claimed fuzzy dark matter explains this, but they faked the wave blobs with a statistical approximation rather than simulating them.

This paper simulates them. The authors evolved 1,000 dark-matter halos with the quantum wave equation, viewed each from 1,000 directions, and searched for the lensing geometry that best matches the real images. With the $10^{-22}$ eV particle, the simulated halos come closer to the observed positions than either the statistical approximation or a smooth model, and some reach the level of the measurement error. A ten-times-lighter particle makes the blobs so large that the images miss by far too much, so the data do constrain the particle's mass.

There are important caveats. The simulated wave blobs turned out to look statistically almost identical to the approximation. When the authors gave the approximation the simulated halo's overall shape, most of its disadvantage disappeared, so the improvement comes mainly from the halo's global mass distribution. The simulations contain no stars or gas. The favored particle mass conflicts with other astronomical constraints. And a competing study shows ordinary cold dark matter, with a slightly lopsided galaxy shape, fits this same system just as well.

Bottom line: a useful new simulation-to-observation pipeline and a real test of a common shortcut, but not yet evidence that dark matter is fuzzy.
