## 1. A white dwarf that borrows a second support beam

**Hook:** For ninety years the 1.4-solar-mass Chandrasekhar limit has been treated as a hard ceiling on white dwarfs — this paper runs a real stellar-evolution code and finds that if a strong internal magnetic field is switched on as the star is squeezed, an accreting white dwarf can keep growing to about 2.4 solar masses, but the answer depends heavily on how that field is prescribed.

---

## 2. Big-picture context

A white dwarf is what remains when a star like the Sun, or up to about eight times heavier, runs out of fuel and sheds its outer layers. The leftover core is about the size of Earth but holds roughly the mass of the Sun. Nothing is burning inside it. What holds it up is a quantum effect: electrons packed this tightly cannot all sit in the same low-energy state, so they are forced into higher and higher momentum states, and that motion pushes outward. In 1935 Chandrasekhar showed that this support has a ceiling. Add enough mass and the electrons approach the speed of light, their push stops growing fast enough, and no stable star exists above about 1.4 solar masses.

That ceiling matters far beyond white dwarfs. A white dwarf in a binary that steals gas from its companion can grow toward the limit, ignite carbon in its core, and blow itself apart as a Type Ia supernova. Because these explosions start from nearly the same mass, they have nearly the same brightness, which is what made them the "standard candles" that revealed the accelerating universe. A handful of Type Ia supernovae are too bright for that story, though. The best known, SN 2003fg, seemed to need roughly 2 to 2.8 solar masses of exploding white dwarf. Either the brightness inference is wrong, or something lets white dwarfs exceed the limit.

Mukhopadhyay's group has argued since 2013 that strong internal magnetic fields can be that something. They call the result "B-WDs". The idea is not new in itself (a magnetic field adds pressure, and pressure can hold up mass), but earlier work built static model stars directly from the equations of structure with a field included. Critics answered that such stars might be unstable, that the required fields might be unphysical, and that nobody had shown how a real star would end up in that state. This paper's contribution is to put a field into a full stellar-evolution code, the Cambridge STARS code, and follow stars from the hydrogen-burning main sequence all the way to accreting white dwarfs.

**Paper type and stakes:** this is a computational stellar-evolution paper that tests whether a previously proposed class of object (super-heavy magnetized white dwarfs) can arise through an ordinary evolutionary path, with stakes for the over-bright supernovae and, indirectly, for how standard the standard candle really is.

**Prior belief check:** the paper sits on the contested side of a long-running argument. The mainstream view is that white dwarfs are bounded by roughly 1.4 solar masses for any realistic field, that the over-bright supernovae are more likely explained by other effects (an off-center or asymmetric explosion, interaction with surrounding gas, or a merger of two white dwarfs that never forms a stable heavy star), and that the internal fields this proposal requires are far stronger than anything measured. Several groups have published critiques, which the authors cite and say were themselves mistaken. The result here does not overturn that consensus. It is a step in the proposers' own program: it shows that a stellar-evolution code, once modified to include a prescribed field, will follow a star to super-Chandrasekhar masses without crashing. Experts will find it unsurprising given the model choices, and the interesting questions are about those choices.

**Replication and convergence note:** this is a single-group result from the research program that originated the B-WD idea, using a one-dimensional code with the field put in by formula; no independent group has reproduced it. Real confirmation would mean a self-consistent multi-dimensional magnetic calculation by another group reaching similar maximum masses, or an observed white dwarf with a measured mass well above 1.4 solar masses.

## 3. Background crash-course

### Why there is a limit at all

Think of the electrons in a white dwarf as filling seats in a theater, one per seat, cheapest seats first. Squeeze the star and the theater shrinks, so the last electrons are pushed into ever more expensive seats — higher momentum — and they push back harder. For a light white dwarf this pushback grows faster than gravity's squeeze as the star shrinks, so the star finds a balance point. But momentum cannot buy unlimited speed. Once the electrons move close to the speed of light, their pushback grows only at the same rate as gravity's squeeze. From then on, shrinking the star no longer helps it. At that point there is exactly one mass that balances, and that mass is the Chandrasekhar limit.

```
How hard each side pushes as the star is squeezed (sketch, not to scale)

  slow electrons:    pressure grows like density^(5/3)   -> wins, star settles
  light-speed ones:  pressure grows like density^(4/3)   -> ties with gravity
  gravity:           squeeze grows like density^(4/3)

   squeeze harder  ->  slow regime: pressure gains faster than gravity
                   ->  fast regime: both gain at the same rate
                       => only one mass can balance: ~1.4 solar masses
```

*Analyst-built illustration (not from the paper).* What to look at: the 5/3 versus 4/3 growth rates. The limit appears exactly when the electrons' growth rate falls to gravity's.

**Concept:** Electron degeneracy pressure supports a white dwarf. As mass rises the star shrinks and the electrons approach light speed, which lowers how fast their pressure grows with compression until it only matches gravity. The mass at which that happens, about 1.4 solar masses for a carbon-oxygen star, is a true maximum for a non-rotating, weakly magnetized white dwarf. A second, independent source of pressure that keeps growing under compression can raise that maximum.

**Vehicle:** A server whose throughput scales with clock speed. Under light load, raising the clock buys more headroom than the load grows. But the clock has a hard cap. Near the cap, each extra unit of load still demands the same increase, while the clock can no longer deliver more than proportional gains. There is a single load level the box can just sustain. Adding a second, independent power rail that keeps scaling under load raises the sustainable load.

**Analogy:** Electrons ↔ the processor. Their momentum ↔ clock speed. The speed of light ↔ the clock's hard cap. Gravity's squeeze ↔ the incoming load. The Chandrasekhar limit ↔ the maximum sustainable load. The magnetic field ↔ the second power rail.

**Breaks when:** you ask what happens at the limit. A server past its limit queues work and slows down; a white dwarf past its limit has no slow mode — it either ignites carbon and explodes or collapses to a neutron star. The vehicle has no category for "the system ceases to exist", which is the whole reason the limit matters.

### What a magnetic field adds

A magnetic field stores energy, and squeezed field lines push back like a compressed spring. That push is called magnetic pressure. If the field is "frozen" into the hot, highly conducting gas, compressing the gas packs the field lines tighter, so the field strengthens as the star shrinks. Measured in units of Gauss: Earth's field is about half a Gauss, a fridge magnet about a hundred, and the strongest observed white-dwarf surfaces reach about a billion. The paper needs the *center* of the star to reach about a hundred trillion Gauss (ten to the fourteenth), which is roughly a hundred thousand times stronger than any measured white-dwarf surface field. Nobody can see inside a white dwarf, so that interior value is untestable directly; it is the crux of the whole proposal.

Fields also have direction, and direction matters. A *toroidal* field wraps around the star like lines of latitude. A *poloidal* field runs from pole to pole like a bar magnet. Both alone are unstable; a mix is needed, and simulations by Braithwaite suggest a mix with the wrapped (toroidal) part dominating is the long-lived one. A wrapped field mostly pushes sideways — it can make a star fatter — while a pole-to-pole field tends to squeeze it.

**Analogy:** Magnetic pressure is like an extra layer of compressed packing foam inside a box. Squeeze the box and the foam pushes back on its own, independent of what else is in there.

**Breaks when:** you treat the field as foam that pushes equally in all directions. Magnetic field lines also have tension, like stretched rubber bands. A wrapped field curving around a sphere tends to pull inward (a "hoop stress"), so the extra support is direction-dependent and partly self-cancelling. The paper's one-dimensional code treats it closer to foam (see Section 6).

### The mass–radius curve

Every white dwarf of a given composition and temperature has a definite radius for its mass: heavier ones are smaller. Plot radius against mass and you get the "mass–radius relation". For the textbook cold white dwarf, the curve plunges toward zero radius as the mass approaches 1.4. The paper's central picture is that a magnetic field bends this curve outward: at any mass the star is bigger, and the curve runs on past 1.4 before ending at a new, higher maximum. The paper also uses this curve for ordinary-mass white dwarfs, where a hot star is a little puffier than a cold one, and asks whether the field adds to that puffiness.

### Origins and big picture: where white dwarfs, magnetic ones, and heavy ones come from

Every white dwarf starts as an ordinary star burning hydrogen in its core. For a star below about eight solar masses, that burning eventually runs out at the center. The core contracts and heats while hydrogen burns in a shell around it, and the outer layers balloon into a red giant. Helium then ignites in the core and is turned into carbon and oxygen. When the core helium is gone, the star becomes an "asymptotic giant branch" star: a dense carbon-oxygen core wrapped by two burning shells and an enormous, cool, loosely held envelope. Strong winds strip that envelope over tens of thousands of years. The ejected gas glows briefly as a planetary nebula, and the naked core, no longer burning, becomes a white dwarf that cools for billions of years. A three-solar-mass star in the paper's runs ends as a 0.45-solar-mass white dwarf with some leftover helium; an eight-solar-mass star ends as a carbon-oxygen white dwarf of about one solar mass. The heaviest progenitors can burn carbon too and leave oxygen-neon white dwarfs.

This standard path never makes a white dwarf above about 1.3 solar masses on its own. To get closer to the limit, a white dwarf must gain mass afterwards, and that requires a companion. In a close binary, the heavier star evolves first and becomes a white dwarf, often after swapping much of its envelope onto the companion. Later the companion swells and spills gas back. If that gas lands at the right rate, the white dwarf grows steadily; too slowly and the accreted layer ignites in nova flashes that blow much of it away; too fast and the gas piles up into a puffy envelope. This "single-degenerate" route is one classic way to make a Type Ia supernova. The other is the "double-degenerate" route, in which two white dwarfs spiral together through gravitational-wave emission and merge.

Magnetic white dwarfs are common: roughly one in five nearby white dwarfs shows a measurable surface field, from a few thousand up to about a billion Gauss. Where those fields come from is still debated. One idea is "fossil" fields: some hot main-sequence stars (the magnetic A and B stars) carry fields of a few thousand Gauss, and shrinking such a star to Earth size concentrates the field enormously if the field lines stay attached to the gas. Another idea is that fields are generated afresh by a dynamo during common-envelope evolution or a white-dwarf merger, which would explain why magnetic white dwarfs are often heavier than average and why few of them have close companions. The paper adopts the fossil picture and assumes the surface field simply follows from conserved magnetic flux.

Where does this paper fit? It takes the binary single-degenerate path, starts from an ordinary magnetic white dwarf, and asks what happens to its interior field as accretion squeezes it. Its claim is that a field too weak to matter in the young white dwarf (the paper calls it "dormant") is amplified by the rising central density until it becomes a real second support beam, letting the star pass 1.4 solar masses. The big-picture payoff the authors want is a stellar origin for the over-bright supernovae and a population of hidden heavy white dwarfs. The broader community has other candidate explanations for those supernovae, so this paper is best read as one proposed origin story for a contested class of object, not a settled one.

**Central analogy for this paper:** a second power rail raising the load limit.

## 4. What the authors actually do

```
Analyst-built concept diagram (not from the paper):

 main-sequence star (3, 5 or 8 solar masses)
   field set by formula: grows with density, weak at low density
        |
        v
 giant -> envelope stripped by winds -> hot white dwarf
   (0.45 He-CO / 0.82 / 1.02 CO solar masses)
   central field ~10^12 G: "dormant", follows the normal curve
        |
        v
 accretion at 1e-9 solar masses per year (spherical)
   center gets denser -> formula raises the field toward 10^14 G
        |
        +--> no field:   stops near 1.4 (carbon ignites)
        +--> with field: radius larger, keeps growing
                 -> new maximum 1.8 to 2.4 depending on profile
                 -> He-rich star: helium flash cuts it off earlier
```

One-line reading guide: the field is never computed from physics inside the code; it is looked up from the local density at each step, so the "amplification" in the middle box is the formula doing its job.

### Step 1: putting a field into a code that has no field

**The failure it prevents:** a stellar-evolution code solves only for pressure, temperature, and energy flow along the radius. It has no way to represent a magnetic field, and solving the full magnetic equations alongside stellar evolution is beyond today's codes.

**What the authors do:** they add the field's effect to the pressure. In the inner 90% of the star, treated as wrapped (toroidal) field, the total pressure is gas pressure plus magnetic pressure. In the outer 10%, treated as pole-to-pole field, the magnetic pressure is subtracted. The field strength is not evolved; it is given at each point by a formula that depends only on the local density. This single equation carries the whole result:

$$
B(\rho) = B_{\rm s} + B_0\left[1 - e^{-\eta\,(\rho/\rho_0)^{\gamma}}\right]
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $B$ : the magnetic field strength at a point inside the star (Gauss)
- $\rho$ : the local density there (grams per cubic centimeter)
- $B_{\rm s}$ : the surface field, a small offset (Gauss)
- $B_0$ : the ceiling the interior field approaches, fixed at $10^{14}$ Gauss
- $\rho_0$ : the density at which the field is well on its way to that ceiling, fixed at a billion grams per cubic centimeter
- $\eta$ : how quickly the field climbs toward the ceiling (no units; 0.7 to 1.3 in the paper)
- $\gamma$ : how sharply it climbs with density (no units; 0.8 or 0.9)

**What this actually means:** it is a lookup table with a soft ceiling. At low density the field is tiny; as density rises it climbs, then saturates at a hundred trillion Gauss. Think of a fan-speed curve in firmware keyed to temperature: the fan is not modelling airflow, it simply reads a sensor and looks up a setting. Here the "sensor" is density and the "setting" is the field, and the two knobs that shape the curve are exactly the two numbers the results depend on.

**What it buys:** a working, stable one-dimensional code that can follow the field's effect through every stage of the star's life. The cost is that the field's growth is assumed, not derived, and the stronger the authors set the curve, the more support they get.

### Step 2: making ordinary magnetic white dwarfs

**The failure it prevents:** a claim that heavy magnetic white dwarfs form naturally needs a natural starting point, not a white dwarf built by hand.

**What the authors do:** they evolve main-sequence stars of three, five and eight solar masses through the giant phases, imposing strong winds to strip the envelopes, until each leaves a nearly fully degenerate white dwarf of 0.45, 0.82 or 1.02 solar masses. The field formula runs the whole time. In the young white dwarfs the central field is around a trillion Gauss and the surface field a hundred thousand to a few hundred thousand Gauss — within the observed range for magnetic white dwarfs. At this stage the field changes nothing: these stars sit on the ordinary mass–radius curve. That is what the paper means by "dormant".

**What it buys:** a continuous story from an ordinary star to an ordinary-looking magnetic white dwarf, which is the paper's main claim of novelty.

### Step 3: feeding the white dwarf and watching the limit move

**The failure it prevents:** a dormant field cannot hold up extra mass; something has to strengthen it.

**What the authors do:** they add mass to the surface of the 1.02-solar-mass carbon-oxygen white dwarf at a steady one-billionth of a solar mass per year, spread evenly over the sphere. As it gains mass, it shrinks and its center gets denser. Through the formula, the central field rises toward a hundred trillion Gauss. Without a field the star reaches about 1.38 solar masses, carbon ignites at the center, and the run stops. With a field the star stays larger at every mass and keeps accreting, up to a new maximum.

**What it buys:** the headline numbers. With the moderate profile (climb rate 0.7) the maximum is about 1.8 solar masses; with 1.1 it is about 2.2; with 1.3 it is about 2.4.

![Figure 2](figures/zuraiq-aea97e_fig2.png)

*Figure 2 (from the paper): radius (km, vertical) against mass (solar masses, horizontal) for an accreting white dwarf with three field profiles, compared with Chandrasekhar's curve.*

**What to look at:** where each curve ends. The orange Chandrasekhar curve plunges to its end at 1.4; the three magnetized curves end at about 1.8, 2.2 and 2.4. The order of the endpoints is set entirely by the climb-rate knob in the formula above — a stronger curve gives a bigger star.

### Step 4: does what the star is made of matter?

**The failure it prevents:** a white dwarf with leftover helium could burn that helium violently once compressed, which might destroy it long before any magnetic limit is reached.

**What the authors do:** they compare the carbon-oxygen white dwarf with a 0.45-solar-mass hybrid that still holds about a tenth of a solar mass of helium. The carbon-oxygen star reaches its magnetic maximum (about 2.35 with the profile used in this comparison) just as its central carbon ignites. The helium-rich star, magnetized or not, ignites helium partway up and is destabilized below its maximum. To prove helium is the culprit, they rerun with nuclear energy switched off in the structure equations; then both stars follow their curves to the top.

**What it buys:** a fair caveat built into the result. Only white dwarfs with negligible helium can climb to the new limit; for helium-rich ones the field only shifts the mass at which helium ignites. The authors also note that real accretion at these low rates would trigger nova-like surface flashes, which the code does not model.

### Step 5: puffy white dwarfs of ordinary mass

**The failure it prevents:** several observed white dwarfs around half a solar mass are measurably larger than the cold mass–radius curve predicts. Temperature alone explains some of that (a hotter star is puffier), but not always all of it.

**What the authors do:** they cool their accreted, magnetized and non-magnetized white dwarfs to fixed surface temperatures and build mass–radius curves for each temperature. A field adds a few percent to the radius on top of the temperature effect. They then compare with measured masses and radii from two observational samples. For one well-measured star, SDSS J123204.19+522548.2 at about 8,750 K, the radius predicted with field plus temperature misses the measurement by under 1%, while temperature alone misses by about 7%.

**What it buys:** an observational hook at ordinary masses, where real data exist, rather than only at super-heavy masses, where none do.

### Step 6: stability and field decay

**The failure it prevents:** a strongly magnetized star might tear itself into a non-spherical shape, which a one-dimensional code cannot represent, or its field might decay before the story finishes.

**What the authors do:** they report that the ratio of magnetic to gravitational energy stays below about one tenth for the heavy white dwarfs, within the range Braithwaite's simulations found stable. They estimate the star's distortion from a sphere (its ellipticity) at below one part in a hundred thousand, which they use to justify the one-dimensional code. And they estimate that the field takes about 5 billion years to decay, longer than the accretion phase.

**What it buys:** the case that the model is self-consistent. The Claim check below found problems with two of these three numbers.

### Claim check

Done in Python (`chk.py`, analyst script): a toy white dwarf with the textbook density profile of a fully relativistic star, scaled to the paper's maximum masses and the radii read off Figure 2, with the paper's own field formula applied.

**Central field at the maximum mass — Reproduced.** The paper says the 2.4-solar-mass star reaches a central field of about 80 trillion Gauss. The formula gives that at a central density of about 1.3 billion grams per cubic centimeter, a sensible value for a star of that radius. So the field the paper quotes follows from its own formula.

**Is the field still "dormant" at the center? — Reproduced, with a note.** At a billion grams per cubic centimeter the magnetic pressure from the formula is about 40% of the electron pressure. The field is therefore a genuine second beam in the core of the heavy stars, not a small correction — consistent with the paper's story. It also means everything rests on the formula being right in exactly the region nobody can observe.

**Energy budget: is one tenth enough? — Partly reproduced; significant tension.** For the toy star with the paper's formula, magnetic energy comes out at 4–7% of gravitational energy, matching the paper's "below one tenth". But a simple energy-balance (virial) argument for a star supported by light-speed electrons says that lifting the limit from 1.44 to 2.4 solar masses needs magnetic energy near 29% of gravitational energy (24% for 2.2). Turned around, a one-tenth budget would lift the limit only to about 1.7 solar masses. So the paper's stated stability budget and its stated maximum mass do not obviously fit together: either the field is stronger, relative to gravity, than "below one tenth" (which moves the star toward the unstable range), or the code is extracting more support per unit of field energy than physics allows. One likely route to the second is the isotropic-pressure treatment in Step 1, which counts the field's push without its inward tension. This is an order-of-magnitude argument for an idealized star, not a refutation, but it is the most important open question about the headline number.

**Ellipticity: is the star really spherical? — Not reproduced; significant finding.** The paper's ellipticity formula sets the density change from the field equal to the magnetic pressure. Pressure is an energy per volume, not a mass per volume; converting it to a density requires dividing by the speed of light squared, which makes it about a hundred billion billion times smaller. Doing exactly that with the paper's formula gives a distortion of a few parts in a hundred thousand — the same order as the paper's quoted value, which suggests that this is how the number was computed. But a magnetic field deforms a star through force, not through its mass-equivalent, and the standard estimate scales with the magnetic-to-gravitational energy ratio itself, which the paper puts at up to a tenth. The physical distortion is therefore probably a few percent to about ten percent — roughly a thousand times larger than claimed. This does not by itself sink a one-dimensional approach (a few percent can still be treated approximately), but it removes the paper's main quantitative justification for it, and it bears directly on the energy-budget tension above.

**Accretion time — minor inconsistency.** Growing from 1.02 to 2.4 solar masses at a billionth of a solar mass per year takes about 1.4 billion years, but the paper says accretion lasts "at most about 1 billion years" when arguing that field decay (about 5 billion years) can be ignored. The margin shrinks from about five to about three and a half; the conclusion survives.

**Sensitivity: what controls the new limit?** The maximum mass is set by the two shape knobs in the field formula. Moving the climb rate from 0.7 to 1.3 moves the limit from 1.8 to 2.4, and the authors chose values "such that we obtain complete evolution", because steeper profiles crash the run. So the new limit is not a prediction of a single number; it is a family of limits indexed by an assumed, unobservable interior profile. The conclusion "the limit can exceed 1.4" survives for any profile reaching the ceiling of a hundred trillion Gauss at core densities; it fails if real interior fields stay near the trillion Gauss the young white dwarfs start with.

**Robustness (simulation): comparison with an independent method.** No second code or analytic limit is used. Self-consistent two-dimensional calculations by other groups, which solve for the field's geometry instead of prescribing it, have argued that deformation and electron capture at high density limit how much extra mass a field can hold; those were among the critiques the authors say were mistaken. The present paper does not re-run any of those cases, so the one-dimensional result has not been checked against the methods that disagree with it.

### Assumption audit

**Watch:** A reader likely assumes the code shows the field *growing* as the star is compressed. The paper actually assigns the field from density by formula at every step; the growth is an input, motivated by flux freezing, not a computed outcome.

**Watch:** A reader likely assumes "new mass limits" means a new fundamental number replacing 1.4. The paper actually gives a set of maxima (about 1.8 to 2.4) that track a chosen field profile; the authors' own phrase is that the limit's "value greatly depends on the underlying physics, here the magnetic field".

**Watch:** A reader likely assumes the measured surface fields constrain the interior fields used. The paper says only surface fields are observable and that its interior fields are about a hundred thousand times stronger; the surface values are consistent with observations, but the interior values that do the work are unconstrained by any measurement.

**Watch:** A reader likely assumes the better fit to SDSS J1232 is evidence for interior magnetism. The paper adds a field (with tunable profile) to a temperature-only model and gets a closer radius for one star; more parameters fitting one data point better is expected, and the paper defers a systematic study with error bars and degeneracies to future work.

## 5. What's genuinely new or clever

The real novelty, new to the field and not just to the reader, is embedding a magnetic pressure term in a full, decades-tested stellar-evolution code and running it continuously from a hydrogen-burning star, through the giant phases and envelope loss, to an accreting white dwarf. Previous B-WD papers built a single static star with a field and asked whether it balances. This one asks whether a star can *get there* through a normal life history, and it answers within its assumptions: yes, as long as the white dwarf has little helium and accretes slowly. The "dormant field" idea is the clever hinge — a field too weak to matter during the whole ordinary life of the star, so the early evolution looks completely normal, which becomes important only once accretion pushes the core density high enough.

A second, smaller contribution is applying the same machinery to ordinary-mass white dwarfs and showing that, if interior fields exist, they would add to the temperature-driven puffiness of observed white dwarfs. That gives the idea a foothold in the mass range where real masses and radii are measured.

The composition comparison is also useful and honest: by switching nuclear energy off and on, the authors show that helium burning, not magnetism, ends the climb for helium-rich stars, which sets an intrinsic limit on which white dwarfs could ever become B-WDs.

### Predictive content check

**Falsifiable handle:** the cleanest prediction is that white dwarfs with measured masses well above 1.4 solar masses should exist and should be large for their mass — around 2,000 to 3,000 km for a 2-solar-mass star, against near-zero radius on the standard curve. None has been seen; the paper points to candidates (slow radio-pulsing objects and the ultramassive magnetic white dwarf ZTF J1901+1458) but none is claimed as a B-WD. A second, nearer handle is the ordinary-mass prediction: magnetized white dwarfs at a given temperature should be a few percent larger than non-magnetic ones, which a large, well-measured sample with known surface fields could test. As delivered here, though, the paper fits one object and does not state a population-level number, so this handle is currently more retrodictive than predictive.

**Formalism load:** this half fires, because the paper's headline claim (new mass limits) rests entirely on a prescribed function rather than a derivation. The pressure formula is load-bearing in the narrow sense that it generates every number, but it is generating them from an assumed interior field profile, so the maximum masses are better read as "what the code returns for this input" than as predictions. The stellar-evolution machinery (nuclear network, winds, cooling) is genuinely doing work, especially in the helium-burning result.

## 6. Limitations and open questions

1. **The interior field is prescribed, not computed, and unobservable.** **(A) Consensus** — the paper states that it replaces Maxwell's equations with a density-dependent formula and that only surface fields can be measured; the field community broadly agrees this is the key unknown. **(paper App. A, App. C)**

2. **Energy-budget tension: one-tenth magnetic energy looks too small to lift the limit to 2.4.** **(C) Speculative** — this comes from my recomputation using the paper's field formula on an idealized star and a textbook energy-balance argument; a real non-ideal star could shift the numbers, but probably not by a factor of three. **(analyst inference)**

3. **Ellipticity probably understated by about a thousand times.** **(C) Speculative** — the quoted value is reproduced only if magnetic pressure is converted to a mass density through the speed of light, which is not how fields deform stars; the physical estimate from the paper's own energy ratio gives a few percent to ten percent. A unit choice I could not see in the paper could change this. **(analyst inference)**

4. **The field is treated as pressure that pushes equally in all directions, without inward hoop tension.** **(B) Contested** — the authors include a sign change for the outer pole-to-pole region and say the approach follows earlier work, but critics of B-WDs have argued that the full direction-dependent field stresses reduce the support; whether the one-dimensional treatment is adequate is disputed. **(paper App. A + broader literature)**

5. **Strong-field stability is assumed from an energy ratio, not tested.** **(B) Contested** — the paper relies on Braithwaite's stability range for mixed fields; whether stars with interior fields near a hundred trillion Gauss remain stable, and whether electron capture at high density lowers the maximum, has been argued both ways in the literature. **(paper §2, §5 + broader literature)**

6. **The maximum depends on a tunable profile chosen to avoid crashes.** **(A) Consensus** — the paper states that its two shape parameters were chosen so that evolution completes and that steeper profiles destabilize the star. **(paper App. B)**

7. **Accretion is spherical, slow, and free of novae.** **(A) Consensus** — the authors say the code has no cataclysmic-variable or nova physics, that real fields above a million Gauss would funnel gas onto the poles, and that realistic rates may differ. **(paper §3)**

8. **Over-bright supernovae have other explanations.** **(B) Contested** — the paper treats SN 2003fg-type events as pointing to heavy progenitors, while much of the supernova community favors asymmetric explosions, interaction with surrounding gas, or mergers; the B-WD route is one candidate among several. **(broader literature)**

9. **Single-object evidence at ordinary masses.** **(A) Consensus** — the paper shows one well-fit object and says a systematic study with errors and degeneracies is left to future work. **(paper §4)**

10. **No rotation and no back-reaction on convection.** **(A) Consensus** — the authors list both as approximations and argue rotation would not change the qualitative result. **(paper §5)**

## 7. Detailed summary and explanation

The paper asks whether a white dwarf heavier than the Chandrasekhar limit could form through an ordinary stellar life, if a strong magnetic field sits inside it. The authors take the Cambridge STARS stellar-evolution code and add a magnetic pressure term: inside most of the star the field adds pressure; near the surface it subtracts some. They do not solve for the field. Instead, at every point and every moment, the field strength is set by the local density through a formula with a ceiling of a hundred trillion Gauss.

They evolve stars of three, five and eight solar masses from hydrogen burning through the giant phases to white dwarfs of 0.45 to 1.02 solar masses. In these young white dwarfs the field is weak enough to change nothing, so they look like ordinary magnetic white dwarfs with surface fields in the observed range. Then they let the heaviest one accrete gas slowly. Its core grows denser, the formula strengthens the field, and the star, which would have stopped at about 1.38 solar masses and ignited carbon without a field, keeps growing to between 1.8 and 2.4 solar masses depending on the profile. Helium-rich white dwarfs do not make it: helium ignites first. For ordinary-mass white dwarfs, the field adds a few percent to the radius on top of temperature, which the authors use to explain one unusually large observed star.

The framing of this summary is deliberate. The paper's language ("confirm the possibility", "new mass limits") reads as a discovery, but what it demonstrates is a conditional: *if* white-dwarf interiors carry fields that rise to about a hundred trillion Gauss as they are compressed, and *if* the field can be treated as extra one-dimensional pressure, then a full stellar life history can lead to super-Chandrasekhar white dwarfs. The first condition is unobservable and is set by choice; the second is the subject of an unresolved methodological dispute. My own checks reproduced the paper's central field from its formula and confirmed that the field genuinely dominates part of the core pressure. They also turned up two things a reader should hold onto: the stated magnetic energy budget looks too small to support 2.4 solar masses on a simple energy-balance argument, and the stated near-perfect sphericity seems to come from treating magnetic energy as mass, with a physically sensible estimate about a thousand times larger. Neither kills the idea, but both weaken the paper's case that its one-dimensional treatment is self-consistent.

The takeaway for a reader is that the Chandrasekhar limit is a limit on *electron* support. Any additional support, magnetic or rotational, raises it; the question has never been whether that is possible in principle but whether nature supplies enough of it. This paper moves the argument from static models to evolutionary ones, which is real progress for the B-WD program, but it does not yet move the field's consensus, because the decisive input — the interior field — is still chosen rather than measured or derived.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the two significant findings in the Claim check. The energy-budget tension rests on a virial argument for an idealized star supported entirely by light-speed electrons, while the paper's heavy, large-radius stars have partly slower electrons and a non-ideal structure; I believe the factor-of-three gap is too large to close that way, but I have not run the paper's code. The ellipticity finding assumes the paper divided magnetic pressure by the speed of light squared, inferred from the match in order of magnitude, not stated in the paper.

## 8. Three crystallized takeaways

1. **The Chandrasekhar limit is a limit on electrons, not on white dwarfs.** Give a white dwarf a second source of support, such as a strong internal magnetic field, and the maximum mass moves — this paper's code moves it from 1.4 to as much as 2.4 solar masses.

2. **The answer is only as good as the field you put in.** The code never computes the field; it reads it from a formula, and the new maximum tracks that formula's settings. Interior fields a hundred thousand times stronger than any measured surface field are assumed, not observed.

3. **The paper's own consistency checks need rechecking.** On simple estimates, its stated magnetic energy looks too small for the mass it supports, and its "almost perfectly spherical" figure appears to treat field energy as mass — so the result is a promising evolutionary path for a contested idea, not a confirmation of super-heavy white dwarfs.

## 9. Shorter summary

White dwarfs, the Earth-sized remnants of Sun-like stars, are held up by electrons crammed so tightly that quantum rules force them to move fast. In 1935 Chandrasekhar showed that this support runs out near 1.4 solar masses: as electrons approach light speed, their push stops gaining on gravity. That ceiling underpins Type Ia supernovae, the standard candles that revealed cosmic acceleration. A few unusually bright Type Ia supernovae, though, seem to need heavier stars.

This paper, from the group that has long proposed strongly magnetized "B-WDs", adds a magnetic pressure term to the Cambridge STARS stellar-evolution code and follows stars from hydrogen burning to white dwarfs. The field is not computed; it is set at each point by a formula that strengthens it with density up to a hundred trillion Gauss. Young white dwarfs look normal. When one accretes gas slowly, its core gets denser, the formula raises the field, and the star grows past 1.4 to between 1.8 and 2.4 solar masses, depending on the chosen field profile. White dwarfs with leftover helium ignite it first and never get there. At ordinary masses, the field makes white dwarfs a few percent larger, which the authors use to explain one unusually large observed star.

The approach is a real step: an evolutionary path instead of a static model. But the interior field that does the work is assumed, unobservable, and about a hundred thousand times stronger than any measured surface field. Simple checks also raise two flags. The stated magnetic energy, about a tenth of gravity's, looks too small to support 2.4 solar masses. And the claim that the star stays almost perfectly round appears to treat magnetic energy as if it were mass; a standard estimate suggests a distortion of several percent.

The paper shows what a stellar code returns if strong interior fields exist. It does not show that they do. The test remains finding a white dwarf well above 1.4 solar masses with an oversized radius.
