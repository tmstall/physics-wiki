## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**"Two Windows on One Machine": Recovering 90%-Missing Time Series by Exploiting the Fact That They're All Views of the Same Hidden Clockwork**

The trick isn't a bigger model — it's refusing to treat the missing-data problem as curve-fitting at all, and treating it instead as a geometry problem with a guaranteed exact answer.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Complex systems — climate, ecosystems, brains, markets, road networks — are usually studied through whatever time series happen to be observable, because the actual governing equations are unknown or intractable. That data is never clean: sensors fail, satellites get clouded out, financial series have gaps, EEG channels drop. The standard fix is imputation — filling gaps by fitting some kind of relationship, statistical or learned, between the variables you do have and the ones you're missing.

The landscape before this paper split into two camps. One camp (mean/median filling, k-NN, random forests, support vector machines, ordinary neural nets) treats imputation as a regression problem: learn a statistical association between a complete series and an incomplete one, then predict the gaps. This works when missingness is mild and there's plenty of fully-observed context to learn from, but it degrades badly under two compounding pressures the authors name explicitly: heavy *intra-series* missingness (a single variable losing 80–90% of its own values) and heavy *inter-variable* missingness (most of the variables in a high-dimensional system being incomplete simultaneously, leaving very little fully-observed signal to regress against in the first place). The second camp — S-map and its multi-view extension MVCM, out of the empirical-dynamic-modeling (EDM) tradition associated with George Sugihara's group — takes a different approach: it reconstructs the system's attractor (its state-space "shape") from a single time series using Takens' delay-embedding theorem, then imputes by local neighbor-fitting on that reconstructed geometry. This dynamics-aware approach has a good track record in ecology, but was built and validated for mild missingness in comparatively low-dimensional systems, not the extreme, widespread-missingness, high-dimensional regime this paper targets.

FPRM's move is to keep the EDM camp's core idea (attractor reconstruction from delay embedding) but replace S-map/MVCM's *local, neighbor-based* fitting with a *global, one-to-one manifold-to-manifold mapping*, learned once via Gaussian process regression, between the full reconstruction (from a fully-observed series) and the partial reconstruction (from an incomplete one). The paper is explicit that this is architecturally a direct descendant of the authors' own 2024 *Nature Communications* paper on predicting multiple observations via low-dimensional embeddings (ref. 19) — FPRM extends that lineage from "prediction" to "extreme-missingness imputation," and pushes the validation from small systems up to 120-dimensional coupled chaotic systems and cross-domain real-world data (transportation, EEG, exchange rates, climate).

**Paper Type & Stakes.** This is a methods/framework paper: a new algorithm plus an extensive validation suite (four model systems, four real-world domains, ten baseline comparisons), not a single empirical discovery. What's at stake is practical rather than paradigm-shifting — whether data-driven modeling of high-dimensional complex systems can survive the kind of catastrophic, widespread missingness that satellite, sensor-network, and multi-channel biomedical data actually exhibit in the field, rather than the mild missingness most benchmark imputation methods are tuned for.

**Prior Belief Check.** Nothing here contradicts mainstream dynamical-systems theory — Takens' embedding theorem (1981) is textbook material, and the idea that delay-embedded attractors from different observables of the same system are diffeomorphic to one another is a direct, well-known consequence of it, not a new theoretical claim. What would surprise a specialist is less "is this true" and more "does it actually hold up numerically at 90% missingness in 120 dimensions" — that's an engineering result, not a theoretical one, and a reasonable EDM specialist would consider it a plausible but nontrivial extension worth checking empirically, exactly as the paper does. This reads as a solid, expected-direction engineering advance on a known theoretical foundation, not a surprising or field-reorienting result.

**Replication & Convergence Note.** This is a single-group result, published essentially yesterday (accepted 9 September 2026, shared as an Article-in-Press on 19 September 2026) — there has been no time for independent replication, and none exists yet. On the positive side for future verifiability: code (MATLAB R2024b) and all data are deposited in a public Figshare repository (DOI 10.6084/m9.figshare.30446765), which is good practice and makes independent replication straightforward in principle. What independent confirmation would look like: another group re-running FPRM (or re-implementing it from the equations, which are fully specified) on the same public real-world datasets (Caltrans PeMS traffic data, the cited EEG dataset, the exchange-rate series) and reproducing comparable ρ/RMSE numbers to those in Table 1.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**A dynamical system's hidden geometry.** Any system that evolves over time according to some (possibly unknown) rule traces out a trajectory in an abstract "state space" — imagine a point moving through a multi-dimensional room, one axis per interacting variable. Over a long run, that trajectory settles onto a lower-dimensional shape called an *attractor*: for the classic Lorenz system this is the famous two-lobed "butterfly." You almost never get to watch the point move through the full room directly — you only get one or a few scalar time series (individual coordinate readouts), because that's all your instruments give you.

**Takens' embedding theorem: reconstructing the room from one window.** This is the load-bearing piece of background, and it's a genuinely CS-friendly idea despite its topology-flavored name.

**Concept:** A dynamical system with *n* interacting components traces a trajectory on an attractor living in (at most) *n*-dimensional state space. Takens' theorem (1981) says that if you take *any single* one of the system's output time series and turn it into a sequence of sliding windows — each window being *E* consecutive (lagged) samples of that one series — the resulting cloud of window-vectors forms a reconstructed shape that is *diffeomorphic* to the true attractor: smoothly, invertibly deformed, but with the same topological structure, for a generic large-enough choice of window length *E*. This holds even though you only ever measured one variable directly. It's not an approximation that gets better with more data in the usual statistical sense — it's a structural guarantee from the geometry of the dynamics itself, given genuinely deterministic, sufficiently smooth dynamics.

**Vehicle:** A single hardware performance counter — say, L2-cache-miss rate — sampled every cycle. On its own, one number per cycle looks like it tells you almost nothing about the full state of a many-core, many-resource machine. But if a component is coupled tightly enough into everything else happening on that core (memory contention, branch mispredictions, prefetcher behavior), then a short sliding window of *consecutive* lagged readings of just that one counter — cache-miss rate now, one cycle ago, two cycles ago — starts to implicitly encode much more about the machine's overall state than the single instantaneous reading did. You've turned one thin stream into a fatter, more informative "state vector" purely by stacking it against its own recent past.

**Analogy:** the system's true state ↔ the full machine state (every counter, every queue, every register). A single time-series variable ↔ one hardware counter's readout stream. The embedding dimension *E* and delay *τ* ↔ how many lagged copies you stack, and how far apart in time. The reconstructed attractor *M(x)* ↔ the cloud of sliding-window "state vectors" built from that one counter. Diffeomorphism to the true attractor ↔ the (guaranteed, not merely hoped-for) claim that this sliding-window cloud is a faithful, invertible encoding of the real machine state — not just "correlated with it," but structurally equivalent to it.

**Breaks when:** real hardware counters are actually correlated with the rest of the machine state through noisy, imperfect physical coupling — cache-miss rate and branch-mispredict rate track each other approximately, with residual slack the vehicle has no way to make disappear. Takens' theorem's claim is stronger and more absolute: for a genuine low-dimensional deterministic dynamical system observed without noise, the reconstructed attractor is *exactly* diffeomorphic to the true one, full stop, not merely well-correlated with it. The vehicle has no category for "provably, not just empirically, equivalent" — that gap is exactly what the theorem supplies and no hardware-counter intuition can substitute for.

**Central analogy for this paper:** Two hardware counters, one exact translation table.

**Gaussian process regression, briefly.** GPR is the specific tool FPRM uses to *learn* the mapping between two reconstructed attractors once the theory guarantees such a mapping exists. Think of it as a smoothed, uncertainty-aware lookup table: given a handful of known (input, output) pairs, GPR doesn't fit a fixed equation — it keeps all the training examples around and, for a new input, produces an output that's a weighted blend of nearby training outputs (nearby inputs get more weight, via a kernel/similarity function), plus an honest confidence band that widens in regions with little training data. It's a natural fit here specifically because the "training set" for any single mapping can be tiny — when 90% of a variable is missing, only 10% of its paired data points are available to learn from — and GPR is well known for staying well-behaved with small samples, unlike neural networks, which tend to want a lot of data.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**The setup.** Suppose a system has *n* interacting variables. One series, x_i(t), is fully observed. Another, x_j(t), has gaps. FPRM's job: fill those gaps using only x_i(t) and whatever parts of x_j(t) survive.

**Step 1 — full reconstruction.** Delay-embed the complete series x_i(t) into an *E*-dimensional attractor, M(x_i). Concretely, here's exactly what that construction (the paper's Eq. 6) looks like on a toy 8-point series, with E = 3 and lag τ = 1 — small enough to walk through by hand:

Toy series: x = [10, 12, 15, 11, 9, 14, 16, 13] (t = 1…8).

Each reconstructed "state vector" is just three consecutive readings, stacked:

X(1) = (10, 12, 15)
X(2) = (12, 15, 11)
X(3) = (15, 11, 9)
X(4) = (11, 9, 14)
X(5) = (9, 14, 16)
X(6) = (14, 16, 13)

That's the whole trick, mechanically: a sliding window sweeping across the series, one step at a time — identical in spirit to building a shift register or a fixed-size ring buffer, except here the buffer's contents *are* the reconstructed state. Stack of six such rows is M(x_i).

**Symbol definitions:**
E : embedding dimension — the window length (chosen from the system's own dynamics, not from how many variables you happen to observe; see Supplementary Fig. S1 in the paper for how the authors pick it)
τ : time delay/lag between consecutive entries within a window
M(x_i) : the reconstructed attractor — the full stack of window-vectors X(t)

**What this actually means:** every row of that matrix is one "snapshot" of the system's inferred state, built from nothing but one variable's recent history — the sliding-window/shift-register picture above, made concrete.

**Step 2 — partial reconstruction.** Do exactly the same sliding-window construction on the incomplete series x_j(t). Wherever x_j(t) had a missing value, every window row that includes that time point inherits a gap. The result, M(x_j), is the same *kind* of object as M(x_i), just with some entries blanked out.

**Step 3 — the mapping.** Because both M(x_i) and M(x_j) are diffeomorphic to the same true underlying attractor (Takens' theorem, applied twice — once per series), there exists an exact one-to-one mapping F between them:

F : M(x_i) → M(x_j)

**What this actually means:** F isn't fitted the way a regression line is fitted to scattered data — it's guaranteed to *exist* by the geometry, in the idealized noise-free case. GPR's job is only to *approximate* that pre-existing, theoretically-guaranteed mapping from the data actually available — it's a surrogate for something the theory says is already there, not something GPR is inventing from scratch.

**Step 4 — training and imputation.** At every time point where both the full and partial reconstructions have valid entries, you get a matched training pair (input from M(x_i), output from M(x_j)). GPR trains on those matched pairs. Then, at any time point where M(x_j) is missing but M(x_i) is intact, you feed M(x_i)'s value through the trained mapping and read off the predicted (imputed) value.

**The scoring.** Performance is judged with two metrics per imputed variable: Pearson correlation ρ between imputed and true values, and a normalized RMSE. A tiny worked check, since the paper's Table 1 reports these in pairs like "(0.89, 0.07)": suppose true values are [2, 4, 6, 8] and a (deliberately imperfect) imputation gives [3, 4, 5, 9]. Working through the paper's own formulas (its Eq. 19) by hand: ρ ≈ 0.93 (strong positive correlation — the imputation tracks the true series' ups and downs well) and normalized RMSE ≈ 0.38 (moderate absolute error relative to the imputed series' own spread). That's roughly the same ballpark the paper reports for its harder 90%-missingness cases (Table 1: ρ around 0.8–0.95, RMSE around 0.4–0.7 for most datasets) — so those headline numbers correspond to genuinely useful, not merely statistically-significant-but-practically-weak, imputations.

**Assumption Audit**

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes FPRM reconstructs the system's *actual* hidden variables — temperature, pressure, whatever the real physical quantities are. The paper actually reconstructs a *topologically equivalent proxy* space (a diffeomorphic image of the true attractor), built entirely from time-lagged copies of one observed number. It's an honest stand-in for the true state space, not a recovery of the literal physical variables themselves — which matters because the E-dimensional "state vector" the method operates on has no direct physical interpretation coordinate-by-coordinate.

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes any one fully-observed variable works about as well as any other as the "anchor" for imputing the rest. The paper's own overall-performance analysis (Fig. 6g–i) shows real variation: in the tested real-world systems, some anchor variables can reliably impute nearly every other incomplete variable, while others can only successfully impute a specific subset. The paper attributes this to noise and system complexity but doesn't offer a principled way to predict, in advance, which observed variable will make the best anchor for a given deployment — that's discovered empirically after the fact.

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes FPRM handles the case where *no* variable is fully observed just as readily as the "one clean anchor" case that dominates the paper's headline results. The core method as derived (Eqs. 1–18) structurally requires at least one fully-observed series to serve as the full reconstruction. The paper does sketch an extension ("lag FPRM") for the all-missing case using staggered valid/incomplete segment pairs instead of a clean anchor, but describes it as still limited for long, synchronous missing stretches across all variables — this is flagged by the authors themselves as an open direction, not a solved case.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

The single cleverest move is architectural, not statistical: instead of asking "what's the best regressor to fit between these two series," FPRM asks "what geometric object do these two series jointly determine, and is there a structural — not merely statistical — relationship between those two objects." Because the answer (via Takens' theorem) is yes, the imputation problem gets reframed from "learn a correlation, hope it generalizes to the missing region" into "approximate an already-guaranteed-to-exist mapping." This is why the paper's own ablation (swapping GPR for SVM or a neural net, "SVM*"/"NN*" in Table 1) still works reasonably well — the *architecture* (map between reconstructed attractors) is doing the real work; the specific regressor is comparatively interchangeable machinery for approximating a relationship the theory already promises exists. That's genuinely new relative to the direct-regression camp of imputation methods, though it is an extension — not a first invocation — of the dynamics-aware EDM lineage (S-map, MVCM) the authors are explicit about building on.

The second notable move is scaling that idea to a regime (120 real-world-adjacent dimensions, up to 90% missingness in nearly every non-anchor variable) that the EDM lineage's prior methods (designed for ecology data with comparatively mild missingness) hadn't been shown to survive.

**Predictive Content Check.**

**Falsifiable handle:** The paper makes concrete, quotable, checkable predictions — e.g., for the 120D coupled Lorenz system with 90% missingness in every non-anchor variable, average ρ ≈ 0.84–0.87 and normalized RMSE ≈ 0.48–0.54 (Fig. 4f). Because the paper fully specifies both the synthetic systems' governing equations (its Eqs. 3–5) and the code (public on Figshare), this is directly re-testable: an independent re-implementation that failed to reproduce comparable ρ/RMSE on the same synthetic systems would falsify the specific numerical claims (though not necessarily the underlying theory, which would survive even a failed re-implementation if the failure traced to an implementation detail rather than the core idea). There's also a structural, theory-level prediction worth naming explicitly: the paper's own argument implies imputation quality should degrade in a *specific, predictable direction* — toward failure — for systems that violate the "genuinely low-dimensional deterministic dynamics" assumption (truly stochastic systems, or systems with abrupt regime-switching tipping points, which the paper itself flags as an unresolved challenge). That's a real, falsifiable directional prediction rather than a post-hoc rationalization, because the paper commits to *why* such systems should break the method (violated embedding-theorem preconditions) rather than just noting empirically that they might.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

The FPRM framework requires at least one fully-observed "anchor" series for each incomplete variable it imputes; when every variable in a system suffers substantial missingness simultaneously, the core method doesn't directly apply, and the paper's own proposed extension for that harder case ("lag FPRM") is explicitly described as still struggling with long, synchronous missing segments across all variables. **(A) Consensus** — this is stated plainly by the authors themselves as a structural limitation, not something this analysis is inferring. **(paper §Merit and limitation)**

Not every fully-observed variable makes an equally good anchor: the paper's own network-based overall-performance analysis shows some anchors can impute nearly all other incomplete variables while others impute only a narrow subset, with no principled a priori way given to predict which will be which. **(A) Consensus** on the existence of the phenomenon (it's reported directly from the paper's own experiments, Fig. 6g–i) — but **(C) Speculative** on the causal explanation, since the paper attributes it only loosely to "noise and inherent complexity" without a worked mechanism, and this analysis has not independently verified that attribution. **(paper §Overall performance / analyst inference on the explanation)**

Gaussian process regression is well known in the broader machine-learning literature to scale poorly with training-set size (classically cubic cost in the number of training points for exact GPR), and FPRM's design requires training one separate GPR mapping *per incomplete variable* — potentially hundreds of separate models for a system like the 500-variable transportation dataset tested here. The paper reports no wall-clock timing, memory footprint, or discussion of computational scaling at all. **(C) Speculative** — this is drawn from general knowledge of GPR's known scaling behavior, not from anything the paper states; given the modest training-set sizes actually used here (hundreds to ~1,700 points per series), it's plausible this simply wasn't a practical bottleneck in these specific experiments, but the paper gives no way to know how the framework would behave at meaningfully larger scale. **(analyst inference)**

The paper tests three specific, stylized non-random missingness patterns (continuous segments, threshold-induced, periodic) but the classical statistical missing-data taxonomy (MCAR/MAR/MNAR) — in particular missingness mechanisms that are themselves correlated with unobserved confounding factors — isn't addressed in those terms, and it's not obvious the three tested patterns are representative of the harder end of that taxonomy. **(B) Contested** — reasonable people could disagree about whether the tested patterns are a fair proxy for real-world non-random missingness or whether genuinely adversarial MNAR mechanisms would behave quite differently; the paper doesn't take a position on this framing at all. **(analyst inference)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

FPRM addresses a genuine, well-motivated gap: existing time-series imputation methods either ignore system dynamics entirely (direct statistical regression, which degrades badly under extreme or widespread missingness) or exploit dynamics only weakly and locally (S-map/MVCM's neighbor-fitting, validated mainly for mild ecological missingness). FPRM's contribution is to push the dynamics-aware idea further: rather than fitting local neighbors on a single reconstructed attractor, it builds two full attractor reconstructions — one from a fully-observed series, one from an incomplete one — and learns a single global mapping between them, justified by the fact that Takens' embedding theorem guarantees such a mapping exists in principle. Gaussian process regression approximates that mapping from whatever matched, non-missing pairs are available.

The validation is broad rather than deep in any one domain: two low-dimensional model systems (a 3-species ecology model, the classic 3D Lorenz system), two 120-dimensional coupled chaotic systems (Lorenz and Rössler), and four real-world datasets spanning transportation (500 road-occupancy series), neuroscience (64-channel EEG), finance (52 exchange-rate series), and climate (in the Supplementary material, not examined in this pass). Across all of these, the headline stress-test is: with only one variable fully observed and every other variable missing 90% of its values, can the framework still impute reliably? The answer, consistently, is a qualified yes — ρ typically lands in the 0.8–0.95 range and normalized RMSE in the 0.4–0.7 range across the tested systems at 90% missingness, comfortably outperforming ten baseline methods spanning classical statistics, standard machine learning, and the prior dynamics-aware EDM methods (Table 1). The framework is also shown to degrade gracefully, not catastrophically, under added noise and under variation in coupling strength between subsystems.

The interpretive choice this analysis makes in framing the write-up is to foreground the *architectural* reframing (statistical-fitting problem → geometric-mapping-approximation problem) over the *numerical* results, because the numbers, while solid, are exactly what the underlying theory would predict if the architecture is doing what it claims — the real interest here is in why the method should work at all under such extreme missingness, not merely that it does.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the practical computational-scaling question (one GPR model per incomplete variable, potentially hundreds of models for a large system) is entirely my own inference from general knowledge of GPR's cost characteristics — the paper is silent on runtime and memory, so I cannot say with confidence whether this is a real practical bottleneck for anyone trying to deploy FPRM at genuinely large scale (thousands of variables) rather than the hundreds tested here. I'm also not fully confident in my read of why some anchor variables generalize much better than others (Fig. 6g–i) — the paper's own explanation is thin, and without seeing the actual figure (I have only the caption and surrounding text, not the rendered image, per the Access Status note above), I can't independently assess whether the pattern looks structured or closer to noise.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. Missing data in a real dynamical system isn't just "numbers you don't have" — because the whole point of a dynamical system is that its state is constrained by its own geometry, the *missing* values are pinned down by that same geometry too, at least in principle. FPRM is really just software for cashing in that theoretical guarantee.

2. The clever part isn't the machine-learning model (Gaussian process regression) — it's recognizing that two different sensors watching the same underlying system are secretly *exactly* translatable into each other, not just correlated, as long as you first re-express each one as a little sliding-window "state vector" instead of a raw number stream.

3. The method has a real Achilles' heel: it needs at least one variable you can always trust to be complete, to serve as the anchor everything else gets translated from. When *nothing* in the system is reliably observed, the theory's guarantee doesn't disappear, but the practical recipe this paper hands you mostly does.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Real-world time series — climate, ecology, brain, market, traffic data — are riddled with gaps: broken sensors, cloud cover, dropped channels. Most imputation methods fill those gaps by fitting a statistical relationship between the variables you have and the ones you're missing, which falls apart when most variables are missing most of the time.

This paper proposes a different strategy, called Full-Partial Reconstruction Mapping (FPRM), built on a classic piece of dynamical-systems math called Takens' embedding theorem. That theorem says something genuinely useful: if you take a single observed time series and turn it into a sliding window of its own recent values, that window secretly encodes almost as much about the whole system's hidden state as if you'd measured everything directly — and, crucially, two different variables' sliding-window encodings of the *same* system are guaranteed to be exactly, smoothly translatable into one another, not just correlated. FPRM builds one such encoding from a fully-observed variable and another from an incomplete one, then uses Gaussian process regression — essentially a smart, uncertainty-aware lookup table — to learn the translation between them from whatever matched data survives, and uses that translation to fill the gaps.

Tested on chaotic model systems up to 120 dimensions and on real transportation, brain, and financial data, the method holds up even when 90% of every non-anchor variable's values are missing, consistently beating ten standard alternatives including classical statistics, mainstream machine learning, and prior dynamics-aware methods from the same research lineage. It degrades gracefully under noise, and an ablation shows the core idea survives even when the specific learning algorithm is swapped out — evidence that the architecture, not the regressor, is what's doing the real work.

The catch: the method needs at least one variable you can count on being complete, to anchor the translation. When nothing in the system is reliably observed, only a preliminary, still-limited variant of the method applies. And because this is a single-group result published within the last day, with no independent replication yet, its real-world robustness — beyond the authors' own extensive but self-conducted test suite — remains to be seen, even though the code and data are openly available for anyone to check.
