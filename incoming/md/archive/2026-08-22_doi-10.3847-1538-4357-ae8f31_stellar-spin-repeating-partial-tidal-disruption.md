# The Star That Was Already Spinning: Solving the Case of the Ever-Dimmer Tidal Flares

**Source:** Bandopadhyay, Amend, Coughlin, Nixon, Pasham & Wevers, "The Role of Stellar Spin in Repeating Partial Tidal Disruption Events," *The Astrophysical Journal*, 1007:181 (2026), DOI 10.3847/1538-4357/ae8f31. Full 7pp article + Appendix uploaded directly and read in full — no retrieval needed, no Access Status block per framework rule for a clean full-text upload. First tidal-disruption-event / accretion-physics paper in this pipeline.

**One-sentence hook:** A star doesn't have to be spun up by a black hole to explain why its repeat flares keep getting dimmer — it just has to already be spinning fast when the black hole first gets its hands on it, and that turns out to be exactly what you'd expect if the star was born from a violently disrupted binary.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

When a star wanders too close to a supermassive black hole (SMBH), the black hole's gravity pulls harder on the near side of the star than the far side. Past a certain distance — the tidal radius — that differential pull overwhelms the star's own self-gravity and the star gets torn apart. Sometimes this is total destruction (a "full" TDE); sometimes only the outer layers get stripped while a dense core survives and sails back out on its orbit (a "partial" TDE). If that surviving core is on a tight, repeating orbit, it can come back for another pass — and another — producing a repeating partial tidal disruption event (rpTDE): a handful of known transients (ASASSN-14ko, AT2018fyk, eRASSt-J045650, AT2022dbl, AT2020vdq, AT2021aeuk, AT2023uqm) that rebrighten months to years after their first flare.

TDEs matter because they're one of the few direct probes we have of otherwise-quiescent SMBHs — the flare's shape and timing encode the black hole's mass, and in principle its spin, even though the black hole itself emits no light of its own between meals. rpTDEs are a particularly information-rich subclass, because watching the *same* star get stripped multiple times, and comparing how bright each successive flare is, is a stress test that a single-flare TDE can never provide.

That stress test has been failing. Existing hydrodynamical simulations from this same research group's own prior work (and independently from other groups) have had real trouble reproducing sequences of progressively *dimmer* flares for more than about two encounters. Low-mass stars tend to get progressively brighter as they're stripped (matching AT2020vdq and AT2023uqm). High-mass stars can dim for exactly one transition — first flare bright, second dimmer — but only by nearly destroying the star outright on the second pass, which can't explain sources like eRASSt-J045650 that have already produced at least five outbursts, or AT2018fyk, whose second flare shows evidence the star survived. This paper's whole project is closing that gap: identifying the missing physical ingredient that lets a hydrodynamical simulation produce three, four, or more successively dimmer flares from a star that keeps surviving.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** theoretical/computational paper — hydrodynamical simulations proposing and testing a specific physical mechanism (pre-existing rapid stellar spin, explained by a specific formation channel) to resolve a known, named tension between rpTDE models and rpTDE observations.

**Prior Belief Check:** doesn't overturn any consensus — a direct, incremental extension of the same group's own established modeling program. Not surprising in the sense of contradicting expectations; notable because it closes a real, previously unresolved puzzle several groups had already tried and failed to fully crack. Calibrate accordingly: "solves a known open problem within an existing framework," not "the field was wrong about something."

**Replication & Convergence Note:** single-group, single-lineage result (Bandopadhyay, Coughlin, Nixon and collaborators, 2024/2025/2026 self-citation chain supplies the code, setup, and the density-increase result the mechanism leans on). ## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Tidal disruption, in one picture.** Self-gravity is the star's "structural integrity budget"; tidal force from the black hole is an external load trying to pull it apart. Cross the threshold distance (tidal radius, `rt`) and the load exceeds the margin, and material sheds. A partial disruption strips the outer casing but leaves the core intact.

**Worked equation box — tidal radius and the penetration factor:**
rt = R⋆ (M•/M⋆)^(1/3)
Symbol definitions: rt = tidal radius; R⋆ = stellar radius; M• = black hole mass; M⋆ = stellar mass.
What this means: a more massive black hole (relative to the star) disrupts from farther away. The paper also uses penetration factor β = rt/rp (rp = actual pericenter distance) — β=1 means grazing the threshold, β>1 means plunging well inside it.

**Fallback rate.** Stripped debris spreads onto a range of orbits and rains back onto the black hole over time; the rate of return, Ṁ, traces the observed light curve — its peak roughly sets peak observed luminosity.

Analogy: like a server replaying a backlog of queued write operations after a burst event knocked some loose — the "backlog drain rate" over time is Ṁ(t), and its peak height is what an observer watching the black hole "light up" actually sees. Breaks when pushed on *why* the backlog has its particular timing shape — that's set by orbital mechanics, not scheduling policy.

**Stellar spin, normalized two ways:**
Ω⋆ = sqrt(GM⋆/R⋆³) — "breakup spin," the fastest the star could rotate before flying apart.
Ωp = sqrt[(1+e)GM•/rp³] — the orbital angular frequency at pericenter.
The paper's central move is comparing a star's own spin Ω against the orbit-derived clock Ωp — not against its own breakup limit Ω⋆ (spin normalized as λ = Ω/Ω⋆, range −1 to 1).

**The Hills mechanism.** A tight, tidally-locked binary (each star's spin synced to the binary's orbital period, like the Moon showing Earth one face) wanders near an SMBH and gets torn apart. One star is flung away at extreme speed; the other is captured onto a tight, near-radial orbit around the black hole — matching the short-period, high-eccentricity orbits inferred for rpTDE sources. Crucially, the captured star arrives already spinning fast, at a rate set by the old binary's tight orbital period.

**Central analogy for this paper: stellar spin as a flywheel already near its target speed.** Each pericenter passage is a fixed-strength kick from tidal torque. A flywheel at rest gets spun up substantially by the kick (big fractional change). A flywheel already spinning near the kick's "natural rate" barely notices it (small fractional change). A star born already spinning near Ωp (via Hills capture) is the flywheel already at speed.

Breaks when pushed on retrograde spin (rotating opposite the orbit) — there the encounter geometry actually reverses the sign of imparted torque (converting retrograde toward prograde), which the simple "distance from target speed" picture can't capture. This regime wasn't simulated here — flagged explicitly as future work.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

Simulations use PHANTOM (SPH code, ~10⁶ particles per star) with initial stellar structure from MESA at three evolutionary stages: ZAMS (zero-age main sequence), MAMS (middle-age), TAMS (terminal-age, core hydrogen nearly exhausted). Each star is set spinning uniformly at Ω = λΩ⋆ before its first encounter, on a highly eccentric orbit around a 10⁶ M☉ black hole. Multiple encounters are simulated by "teleporting" the surviving core back to its starting position once it settles (~2 days post-pericenter) — a shortcut previously verified not to bias results.

**Two competing effects:**
1. **Density rises.** Each time mass is stripped off a sufficiently massive star (M⋆ ≳ 1 M☉), the remaining bound material settles denser than before (established in companion paper Bandopadhyay et al. 2025) — denser stars resist further disruption, driving a monotonic decline in stripped mass and peak luminosity per pass.
2. **Spin evolves.** Tidal torque during each pass changes stellar spin. In prior simulations starting from zero spin, imparted spin kept growing pass after pass, and because faster prograde spin makes a star more fragile, this counteracted and eventually overwhelmed the density effect — producing brightening instead of the observed dimming. This is the mismatch the paper fixes.

**Worked equation box — spin-dependent tidal radius:**
rt(λ) = R⋆ (M•/M⋆)^(1/3) (1−λ²)^(−1/3)  [prograde spin]
Worked numbers: λ=0 (nonspinning) → bracket term = 1 (baseline). λ=0.7 → (1−0.49)^(−1/3) = (0.51)^(−1/3) ≈ 1.25 — a star spinning at 70% of breakup gets disrupted from ~25% farther out than an identical nonspinning star. Concrete mechanism behind "more prograde spin → more fragile."

**The flywheel logic, put together.** If a star starts its first encounter already spinning near Ωp, tidal torque has little further work to do — λ barely changes encounter to encounter, fragility (via the equation above) stays roughly fixed, and the density effect alone drives clean, monotonic dimming. Four simulated cases confirm this:
- 1 M☉ ZAMS, λ=0.7, β=0.6 (spin above Ωp initially): torqued down to ≈0.95 Ωp on pass 1, then flat. Peak fallback rate declines ~1.5× pass 1→2, smaller thereafter — absent when the same star starts nonspinning.
- 3 M☉ TAMS, λ=0.8, β=1.0 (spin below Ωp): spun up slightly each pass but capped at Ωp (can't be spun up past it, per prior work), so spin change shrinks with more encounters. Dimming holds.
- 3 M☉ TAMS, λ=0.8, β=0.6 (spin above Ωp): torqued down each pass; both effects reinforce dimming.
- 3 M☉ MAMS, λ=0.8, β=1.0: modest spin-up occurs but density effect dominates regardless.

**Assumption Audit**
- Watch: you might assume spin is directly observed/measured. Paper actually says: spin is entirely inferred by matching simulated light-curve shape to observed light curves — "strong indirect evidence," not measurement.
- Watch: you might assume the M⋆ ≳ 1 M☉ "high-mass" threshold is derived/justified in this paper. Paper actually says: the density-increase-on-mass-loss behavior the whole mechanism depends on is imported from companion paper Bandopadhyay et al. 2025, not re-derived here.
- Watch: you might assume Hills capture guarantees the favorable prograde, near-Ωp alignment the story needs. Paper actually says: it requires "favorable alignments" — Hills capture naturally supplies spin *magnitude* (tidal locking) but not guaranteed *direction*/alignment. Misaligned/retrograde outcomes are plausible and largely unexplored.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Not "add spin as a free parameter" (prior work already varied spin) but connecting the star's spin history to its formation channel: tying pre-encounter spin specifically to Hills capture from a tidally-locked binary, which predicts both that the captured star should already spin fast and roughly what fraction of breakup that spin should be. Turns a fitting parameter into something with independent physical origin — new to the field.

Second: identifying the decoupling condition — when spin sits near Ωp, torque efficiency per pass drops enough that spin evolution stops fighting the density trend, explaining why prior all-spin-from-zero simulations kept failing past two dimming steps.

**Predictive Content Check — falsifiable handle:** sources dimming by a factor of ≲2 per outburst (AT2022dbl, eRASSt-J045650, AT2021aeuk) should be explainable by moderate prograde spins (λ≈0.7–0.8) near Ωp — testable against future outbursts from these systems. The paper also predicts its own failure point: AT2018fyk's ~order-of-magnitude drop between flares 1 and 2 would require Ω ≫ Ω⋆ under this simple picture, implausible — the authors invoke additional relativistic/three-body effects instead (AT2018fyk's black hole ~10^7.7 M☉). Stating the model's own breaking point this explicitly is a genuine falsifiable commitment.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

- Retrograde-spin regime (qualitatively different, not a sign-flip) discussed only qualitatively, never simulated. **(A) Consensus** — standard compute-cost-driven gap, and the authors say so themselves. (paper §3, "we intend to explore this scenario in future work")
- Model can't reproduce AT2018fyk's ~10x dimming without invoking relativistic/three-body effects not included in these (Newtonian) simulations. **(A) Consensus** — explicitly stated. (paper §3)
- Only four discrete simulation points run (two masses, three evolutionary stages, a handful of λ/β combinations) — a demonstration the mechanism *can* work, not a systematic sensitivity scan across the full range of real rpTDE candidates. **(B) Contested** — the group's prior work argues some results scale simply with black hole mass/stellar structure, but that scaling isn't re-verified here for the spin-dependent case. - The mechanism's foundation (massive stars get denser upon repeated mass loss) is imported from a single companion paper by the same authors, not independently re-derived or stress-tested here — single-lineage dependency. **(C) Speculative** — a structural observation, not necessarily one the field would flag as a limitation. ## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

Some rpTDE candidates show flares that get dimmer with each repeat outburst — a trend hydrodynamical simulations struggled to reproduce past two encounters, because prior models either destroyed the star after the second pass or, when it survived, generated growing spin that made later flares brighter. This paper's fix: stop treating pre-first-encounter spin as zero. Motivated by the Hills mechanism, the authors initialize simulations with stars already spinning at 70–80% of breakup, prograde-aligned with the orbit. Across four simulated combinations, the result holds: when spin starts comparable to Ωp, tidal torque has little room to change it further, spin stays roughly fixed across encounters, and the previously-established tendency of massive stripped stars to get denser drives a clean, monotonic decline in peak fallback rate over (at least) four passages.

Interpretive choice worth flagging: the paper frames this as "strong indirect evidence for the Hills mechanism" — reasoning backward from observation (real light curves show the predicted dimming pattern) to formation channel (this pattern requires the spin state Hills capture most naturally supplies). "Consistent with" is doing the heavy lifting rather than "uniquely explained by" — other formation channels leaving a star spinning near Ωp prograde would produce the identical signature; the paper argues Hills capture is the most natural way to get there, not the only conceivable way.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the paper demonstrates torque-inefficiency-near-Ωp empirically (via simulation outputs, the flat spin-vs-time curves in Figures 1–4's bottom panels), not through a closed-form analytic derivation of *why* efficiency drops near Ωp in this text. My "flywheel" framing is a physically-motivated simplification layered on top of the simulation results to make the behavior intuitive — a reasonable reading, but interpretive scaffolding rather than something the paper states in those terms.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. A repeating tidal disruption event can only keep getting dimmer over many outbursts if the star was already spinning fast *before* the black hole ever touched it — if the black hole has to spin the star up as it goes, the flares tend to get brighter instead.
2. That kind of pre-existing fast spin is exactly what you'd expect from the Hills mechanism: a tight, tidally-locked binary gets torn apart by the black hole, and the captured star arrives already spinning near the rate its old companion had synced it to.
3. This is indirect, simulation-matched evidence, not a direct spin measurement — but it's currently the best physical explanation on the table for why systems like eRASSt-J045650 and AT2022dbl keep dimming instead of brightening, with an honest admission of where it doesn't work (AT2018fyk needs extra physics this paper doesn't include).

## 9. Shorter Summary

Some black holes don't destroy a star in one bite — they take repeated partial bites over months or years, producing repeat flares called repeating partial tidal disruption events. A puzzle: several real examples get dimmer with each flare, but simulations kept failing to reproduce that pattern past the second flare. Either the star got destroyed too soon, or — when it survived — the black hole's own tugging spun it up more each time, making it more fragile and producing brighter, not dimmer, later flares.

This paper's fix: don't assume the star starts non-spinning. If it arrives already spinning fast — comparable to how fast it's orbiting at closest approach — the black hole's tugging barely changes its spin further. With spin roughly locked in place, a separate, already-known effect takes over: stars that lose mass repeatedly tend to get denser each time, and denser stars resist further stripping. That density effect alone produces a steady dimming trend across four simulated repeat encounters, matching what several real systems show.

Where does an already-fast-spinning star come from? The authors point to Hills capture: a tightly orbiting pair of stars, spinning in sync with each other, wanders near the black hole and gets torn apart. One star is flung away at extreme speed; the other is captured onto a tight orbit around the black hole, still carrying the fast spin it had as part of the old pair — naturally supplying exactly the needed ingredient.

The result isn't a direct measurement of any real star's spin — it's an inference from matching simulated light curves to observed ones. It doesn't explain everything: one particularly extreme dimming system, AT2018fyk, would need an implausibly fast spin under this simple picture, and the authors suggest extra relativistic effects (from an especially heavy black hole) are needed there instead. But for several other repeating systems, this gives the clearest physical story yet for why the flares keep fading.
