# 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Tuning the Crystal to the Dark: Plasmons as Dark-Matter Antennas**

By computing, from first principles, three real crystals whose electrons naturally "ring" at the same absurdly low energies a sub-GeV dark matter particle could ever deposit, this paper argues that purpose-tuned materials could leapfrog every existing dark-matter detector proposal by two to three orders of magnitude — and that the same crystals' lopsided (anisotropic) response doubles as a built-in lie detector against fake signals.

# 2. Big-Picture Context

The dark matter (DM) direct-detection field has spent decades chasing WIMPs — DM roughly as heavy as an atomic nucleus — with detectors tuned to feel a nucleus recoil. That programme has come up empty at the electroweak scale, and the field has increasingly turned toward *light* DM: candidates from the keV to sub-GeV range, where the tiny kinetic energy a DM particle carries (or the tiny rest-mass energy it could deposit via absorption) is far below what a conventional nuclear-recoil detector can even register. To catch something that faint, you need a target material whose electrons can be nudged by an energy deposit of tens to hundreds of milli-electron-volts (meV) — roughly a thousand times less energy than visible light — and you need that response to be *strong*, not just technically nonzero.

The dominant strategy for finding such targets, developed largely by this same research group over the last several years (their own refs. [28–31] are the theoretical backbone), is to compute a material's **loss function** — the same quantity electron-microscopists use to characterize how a material absorbs energy from a fast electron beam (electron energy-loss spectroscopy, EELS) — and reinterpret it as "how hard would this material shake if a DM particle grazed it." Superconducting aluminum (refs. [5,6,9]) has been the leading proposed target for absorption/light-mediator scattering; a 2026 high-throughput screen of the Materials Project database (ref. [49], again largely the same group) searched thousands of candidate materials computationally for better options.

This Letter takes a different tack from brute-force screening: instead of searching broadly, it targets three specific classes of "unconventional" condensed-matter systems, each already known — from the authors' own prior condensed-matter physics interests — to host an exotic, very-low-energy collective electronic excitation for a distinct microscopic reason: a charge-density-wave (CDW) transition-metal dichalcogenide (TiSe₂), a multiband "strange metal" perovskite hosting a theoretically predicted but only recently observed "demon" mode (Sr₂RuO₄), and heavily hole-doped diamond (HDD), whose plasmon frequency is continuously tunable by doping. All three are real, already-synthesized materials, not hypothetical ones — and the CDW physics of TiSe₂ and the demon-mode physics of Sr₂RuO₄ are the same materials-science territory covered in this project's earlier deep dive on attosecond core-level spectroscopy of the TiSe₂ CDW transition (2026-08-16 entry) and the Pines'-demon discovery paper cited here as ref. [26] — so the underlying electronic-structure physics should feel familiar going in.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** this is a theoretical/computational target-proposal Letter — DFT calculations plus projected detector-reach curves plus a small proof-of-concept crystal synthesis — not a claim of any dark matter detection; the stakes are guiding where the next generation of light-DM experimental effort and funding should be aimed, not settling any question about DM's existence.

**Prior Belief Check:** Nothing here overturns consensus physics — the dielectric-response formalism connecting loss functions to DM interaction rates is the same group's own established machinery, and CDW plasmons, demon modes, and doping-tunable diamond plasmonics are all previously known condensed-matter phenomena. What is genuinely surprising, even to specialists, is the *degree* of improvement claimed: 2–3 orders of magnitude beyond both superconducting aluminum and the best material found by an exhaustive automated database screen — a large jump from picking three targets by physical intuition rather than brute-force search. This reads as the next well-executed step in an active, single-lineage research programme rather than a field-consensus-shifting result.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is effectively a single-group result — nearly every methodological reference (the dielectric-formalism papers, the Materials Project screen being beaten, the fitting-procedure paper) shares overlapping authorship with this Letter. No independent group has verified these specific loss-function calculations. TiSe₂ has a *partial* independent check: existing zero-momentum optical/EELS data (ref. [23]) to compare against, revealing real if modest disagreement (discussed below). Sr₂RuO₄ and HDD have no such experimental point of comparison at all — the paper says outright that their finite-momentum loss functions "have not been discussed in the literature thus far." Independent confirmation would look like an outside group's EELS or optical measurement of Sr₂RuO₄ or HDD at nonzero momentum transfer, or ultimately a small prototype detector built from one of these materials returning a background rate consistent with the projected sensitivity.

# 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Concept 1 — Why "loss function," and why it's the same tool used in electron microscopy.**

A material's dielectric function ε(ω,q) describes how it screens an external electric field oscillating at frequency ω and wavelength set by momentum q. The quantity **W(ω,q) = Im[−1/ε_L(ω,q)]**, the loss function, tells you how much energy and momentum the material is actually *willing to absorb* at that (ω,q) — it's the standard quantity extracted from EELS experiments, where you fire fast electrons through a thin sample and see what energies get soaked up.

**Analogy:** Think of the loss function as an existing profiling API that was built to characterize a system's response to one kind of workload (a fast electron beam) but that any new workload can query, because it just describes the system's own intrinsic response — not anything specific to electrons. This paper doesn't invent new physics; it re-runs the exact same "profiler" and asks what it says about an entirely different, far weaker particle (dark matter) grazing the same material.

**Breaks when:** you push it to imply the loss function alone is the whole DM-rate calculation. It isn't — the full rate (Eqs. 1–2 below) also needs the DM's own velocity distribution and its coupling to electrons, folded in on top. The loss function is the material-specific ingredient; the DM physics is a separate, mediator-dependent multiplier.

**Concept 2 — Why a charge-density-wave transition creates a very-low-frequency plasmon (TiSe₂).**

An ordinary metal's plasmon (a collective sloshing of all its conduction electrons) sits at a frequency ω_p that scales as the square root of the free carrier density n available to slosh: ω_p ∝ √n. TiSe₂ undergoes a CDW transition below ~200 K: the lattice spontaneously distorts, opening an energy gap over much of the Fermi surface and removing most carriers from the "available to respond" pool.

**Analogy:** Picture a distributed system's heartbeat/consensus rate, which depends on how many active nodes are participating. Take most of the nodes offline (freeze them into a fixed, non-participating state, as the CDW gap does to most conduction electrons) and the collective heartbeat rate of whatever remains slows dramatically — not because the system got "weaker," but because far fewer participants are free to oscillate together.

*Worked micro-example (illustrative order-of-magnitude check, not the paper's own number):* a typical simple metal's plasmon sits around ω_p ≈ 5 eV. If only about 1 in 5,000 of those original carriers remain mobile after the CDW gap opens — a hypothetical fraction, not the paper's measured value — the new frequency scales as √(1/5000) × 5000 meV ≈ 71 meV. That lands within shouting distance of both the DFT value this paper computes for TiSe₂'s in-plane plasmon (~66 meV) and the somewhat lower value seen in existing optical data (~47 meV) — a useful sanity check that "most carriers get frozen out" is roughly the right order-of-magnitude story, even though the real calculation is far more detailed than a bare carrier-counting argument.

**Breaks when:** pushed further, since the CDW gap doesn't just remove carriers uniformly — it restructures which electronic states exist at all (new folded bands, new gaps at specific momenta). "Same nodes, fewer active" glosses over a genuine reorganization of the electronic structure, not merely a headcount reduction.

**Concept 3 — Why a multiband metal can host a mode invisible to ordinary charge probes (Sr₂RuO₄'s "demon").**

When a metal has electrons in several distinct bands, the electric field only easily excites the mode where *all* bands' charge sloshes in phase — that's the ordinary plasmon, and it's gapped at finite frequency because in-phase motion creates a net charge pileup, which costs long-range Coulomb energy to sustain. David Pines predicted in 1956 that a second mode should also exist: bands sloshing *out of phase*, so the total charge density stays neutral everywhere. Because it creates no net field, this mode evades the usual Coulomb penalty and can exist at arbitrarily low frequency (an "acoustic" mode, ω → 0 as q → 0) — and because it produces no net charge signature, it's essentially invisible to any probe that only senses aggregate charge, hence Pines' nickname, "demon."

**Analogy:** Picture two load-balanced worker pools that trade load back and forth — one gains exactly as the other loses, so the *total* system load stays flat and invisible to a monitor watching only the aggregate. Real internal activity is happening (each pool's load genuinely oscillates), but you'd only see it by monitoring the pools individually, not the sum.

**Breaks when:** asked *why* this out-of-phase mode is gapless rather than merely hard to see. The load-balancer picture has no equivalent of a long-range Coulomb interaction that specifically penalizes in-phase shifts — it mirrors the *observability* distinction, not the physical mechanism that makes one mode gapped and the other not.

**Central analogy for this paper:** *A sensor tuned to the signal's exact scale.*

# 4. Core Technical Explanation

**Step 1 — The master relations.** DM-electron scattering and absorption rates are both computed from the same loss function, via:

Rate ∝ (DM number density ÷ DM mass) × ⟨velocity-weighted coupling-squared⟩ × loss function, evaluated at the energy ω and momentum q the DM particle could actually deliver given its mass and speed.

**Symbol definitions:**
ρ_χ : local dark matter mass density in the galaxy (they use 0.3 GeV/cm³)
m_χ, m_φ, m_e : dark matter, mediator, and electron masses
μ_eχ : the DM-electron reduced mass
q : magnitude of momentum transferred in the interaction
𝒲(ω,q) : the loss function — the material's own intrinsic response
σ̄_e : a reference cross section, fixed by convention at a reference momentum q₀ = αm_e (α ≈ 1/137, the fine-structure constant)
|ℱ(q)|² : a form factor that interpolates between (q₀/q)⁴ for a very light mediator and 1 for a very heavy one

**What this actually means:** the loss function tells you how "loud" the material's electrons ring at the DM's delivered energy and momentum; everything else in the formula is bookkeeping for how strongly the DM actually couples and how fast/numerous the DM particles are (using the Standard Halo Model for their galactic velocity distribution — the same convention used in essentially every direct-detection paper, including the LZ and POLONAISE analyses already covered in this project).

*Worked micro-example — why panels (a) and (b) of Fig. 2 look so different in absolute scale:* q₀ = αm_e ≈ 3,729 eV. At a representative low momentum transfer of q = 10 eV (near the low end of the materials' interesting response), the light-mediator form factor gives (q₀/q)⁴ = (372.9)⁴ ≈ 1.9 × 10¹⁰ — a huge enhancement relative to the heavy-mediator value of exactly 1. That ten-order-of-magnitude gap in the form factor is exactly why Fig. 2(a)'s light-mediator cross sections (σ̄_e ~ 10⁻³⁸–10⁻⁴³ cm²) and Fig. 2(b)'s heavy-mediator cross sections (σ̄_e ~ 10⁻²⁷–10⁻⁴² cm²) sit on such different absolute scales — it's a convention baked into how the reference cross section is normalized, not a sign that one scenario is ten orders of magnitude "easier" to probe than the other.

**Step 2 — Choosing materials by physical mechanism, not brute-force search.** Each material is picked for a mechanistically distinct reason: TiSe₂'s CDW gap suppresses carrier density (Concept 2 above); Sr₂RuO₄'s multiple Ru 4d-derived bands crossing the Fermi level support the demon mode (Concept 3); HDD's plasmon frequency is set directly by the density of valence-band holes, which is a knob the experimentalist controls via boron doping during growth. Comparing the low-doping (n_h = 4.52×10¹⁹ cm⁻³) and high-doping (n_h = 4.52×10²¹ cm⁻³, a factor of 100 more) HDD samples, the computed optical plasmon shifts from roughly 0.35 eV to roughly 1.6 eV — qualitatively tracking the expected "more carriers, higher frequency" trend, though not quite the naive free-electron √n scaling would predict (a factor of 10, not the observed ~4.6), a real and unsurprising signature of diamond's genuinely nonparabolic valence bands at these doping levels rather than a problem with the paper's calculation.

**Step 3 — The actual computation.** All three materials' loss functions are computed fully from first principles (density functional theory at the random-phase-approximation, RPA, level), across the *entire* Brillouin zone — not just the easy zero-momentum ("optical") limit most prior work reports — and along multiple crystallographic directions, since the response is anisotropic. Figure 1 shows this directly: panels (a)–(c) are 2D color maps of the loss function versus energy ω and momentum q for TiSe₂, Sr₂RuO₄, and low-doped HDD respectively, and panels (d)–(f) show line-cut comparisons along two different crystal axes at several fixed momenta. TiSe₂ shows a sharp ~66 meV in-plane plasmon plus a broader ~700 meV interband feature from the CDW gap; Sr₂RuO₄ shows the demon mode as a broad low-energy (~80–800 meV) feature strongly suppressed along one crystal axis relative to the other, plus a sharp ~1.6 eV conventional plasmon; HDD shows both an optical plasmon (~200–1600 meV depending on doping) and a separate acoustic-plasmon feature near ~200 meV.

**Step 4 — From loss function to a projected reach curve.** Combining the computed 𝒲(ω,q) with the DM kinematics formula from Step 1, assuming a kilogram-year exposure, zero background, Poisson statistics, 95% confidence level, and sensitivity to energy deposits between 10 meV and 10 eV, the paper produces the projected cross-section-versus-mass reach curves in Fig. 2(a)–(b) and the absorption reach in Fig. 2(d).

**Step 5 — Anisotropy as a feature, not a nuisance.** Because the loss function genuinely differs along different crystal directions, and the direction of the incoming "DM wind" (set by the Solar System's motion through the galactic DM halo) is fixed in the sky while a lab-fixed crystal rotates with the Earth once a day, the predicted scattering rate should modulate on a 24-hour cycle. Figure 2(c) shows this daily-modulation prediction for a 100 keV DM mass: Sr₂RuO₄ shows the largest swing (its in-plane and out-of-plane responses are the most disparate of the three materials), TiSe₂ a smaller but still substantial one, and HDD's modulation is explicitly noted to fall below the 1% level (not shown) because doped diamond's cubic symmetry makes its response nearly isotropic.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** Reader might assume Fig. 2's reach curves represent an actual detected signal or measured sensitivity. The paper actually presents pure theoretical projections for a hypothetical future kg-year, background-free experiment — no detector was built or operated.

> **Watch:** Reader might assume the "isotropic reach" solid curves in Fig. 2(a)–(b) represent an average over random crystal orientation, as you'd expect from a polycrystalline sample. The paper explicitly reports the reach along whichever single crystallographic axis performs best ("a single orientation ẑ(x̂) provides the best reach across the entire presented DM mass range") — a best-case single-crystal-orientation projection, not a bulk-material average.

> **Watch:** Reader might assume the ~10-order-of-magnitude gap between Fig. 2(a)'s and Fig. 2(b)'s absolute cross-section scales reflects a real difference in how hard light- versus heavy-mediator DM is to detect. As the worked example above shows, it's largely an artifact of how the reference cross section σ̄_e is normalized at q₀ = αm_e for each mediator convention.

# 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Two things stand out. First, the **computational contribution is genuinely new to the field**, not just to this reader: the paper states outright that finite-momentum DFT loss functions for Sr₂RuO₄ and HDD had never before been computed, and that TiSe₂'s response across the *whole* Brillouin zone (not just the zero-momentum optical limit) had likewise been missing from the literature. This is real, previously-unavailable materials data.

Second, the **selection logic is clever rather than the underlying physics**: instead of screening thousands of candidate materials automatically (as the same group's own earlier Materials Project survey did), this Letter reasons backward from three *distinct microscopic mechanisms* that are each independently known to produce a strong, tunable, sub-eV collective mode — a CDW gap, a multiband demon mode, and doping-controlled plasmonics — and shows all three beat the automated screen's own best finds by 2–3 orders of magnitude. That's "new to the field" in degree, even though none of the three underlying condensed-matter phenomena is new to physics.

**Predictive Content Check**

*Falsifiable handle (always):* This is fundamentally a projection paper, not a discovery paper — it predicts what a future kg-year experiment *could* see, conditional on the theory being right, rather than reporting an observed rate. But it does make two genuinely checkable claims now: the computed loss-function line shapes and peak energies themselves (e.g., TiSe₂'s ~66 meV in-plane plasmon, Sr₂RuO₄'s ~1.6 eV bulk plasmon, HDD's doping-dependent plasmon frequencies) are directly testable against future EELS or optical measurements — and for TiSe₂, this comparison has *already* partly been done, revealing real quantitative disagreement (discussed in Section 6). So: relabels known condensed-matter phenomena as DM targets, but predicts new, checkable numbers for two of the three materials' finite-momentum response.

*Formalism load (conditional):* Fires, and is judged load-bearing. The DFT-RPA machinery is not decorative — it *is* the paper's entire computational contribution; strip it out and there is no result left to report.

# 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> DFT-RPA calculations for TiSe₂ show real, quantified disagreement with existing zero-momentum optical data: the measured plasmon frequency sits somewhat lower than the DFT prediction, and additional phonon features appear in the data that are entirely absent from the DFT loss function. **(A) Consensus** — explicitly acknowledged and discussed by the authors themselves, who attribute it to the known difficulty of accurately capturing the CDW gap and screening in DFT. **(paper §"In Fig. 1(d)...")**

> Sr₂RuO₄ and HDD's finite-momentum loss functions have zero experimental point of comparison anywhere in the current literature — these are genuinely unverified theoretical predictions. **(A) Consensus** — the paper says so itself. **(paper, "the theoretical DFT-based loss functions of Sr₂RuO₄ and HDD have not been discussed in the literature thus far")**

> All reach projections assume an idealized, background-free detector with perfect Poisson statistics over a kg-year exposure — real detectors will face thermal/infrared photon backgrounds, radiogenic and surface backgrounds, cosmogenic secondaries, and will need dedicated low-threshold sensor engineering not yet demonstrated for any of these three materials. **(A) Consensus** — explicitly flagged by the authors in their own End Matter. **(paper, End Matter "Experimental considerations")**

> Only TiSe₂ received an actual experimental synthesis proof-of-concept in this Letter (milligram-scale crystals grown via chemical vapor transport); feasibility for Sr₂RuO₄ and HDD rests entirely on citations to prior literature rather than anything demonstrated in this paper. **(B) Contested** — reasonable physicists could argue citing well-established growth literature is sufficient, since both materials are already routinely synthesized in condensed-matter labs (Sr₂RuO₄ in particular has a decades-long single-crystal-growth tradition, albeit historically optimized for superconducting-quality purity rather than DM-detector-scale mass production). **(analyst inference)**

> The RPA level of theory used throughout does not include excitonic (electron-hole binding) effects, which the authors' own cited literature (ref. [24], on exciton condensation in TiSe₂) shows can matter in exactly this material. Whether this materially shifts the DM-relevant part of the loss function is not addressed either way in this Letter. **(B) Contested** — genuinely disputed how much this matters for the specific (ω,q) region relevant to DM detection versus the broader spectral region excitonic physics is usually invoked for. **(broader literature)**

> The 2–3 order-of-magnitude improvement claim over the Materials Project database benchmark could partly reflect that both surveys share the same DFT-RPA-level systematic approximations, rather than this work having found materials that would still win under a higher level of theory. **(C) Speculative** — this is the analyst's own inference, not raised by the paper; a specialist may have a stronger view on how much RPA-level systematics would shift a *relative* comparison between two RPA-level calculations. **(analyst inference)**

# 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

Light dark matter — anywhere from keV to sub-GeV mass — can only deposit tiny amounts of energy (tens to hundreds of milli-electron-volts) when it interacts with ordinary matter, whether by nudging an electron (scattering) or being absorbed outright. Finding it therefore requires target materials whose electrons respond strongly at exactly those tiny energies. This Letter computes, entirely from first-principles calculations, how three real crystals — the charge-density-wave material titanium diselenide (TiSe₂), the layered perovskite strontium ruthenate (Sr₂RuO₄), and heavily boron-doped diamond (HDD) — would respond to such a nudge, using the same "loss function" mathematics developed for interpreting electron-microscopy experiments.

Each material earns its spot for a different physical reason. TiSe₂'s charge-density-wave transition below 200 K removes most of its free electrons from circulation, which — the same way slowing a distributed system's active node count slows its overall response rate — drags its natural collective-oscillation frequency down from a typical metal's electron-volt scale to a mere ~66 milli-electron-volts. Sr₂RuO₄ hosts multiple electron bands that can slosh charge in opposite directions, producing zero net charge signature but a real, low-frequency collective mode — a 1956 theoretical prediction (the "demon") only recently confirmed experimentally. Heavily doped diamond simply lets you dial the plasmon frequency up or down by controlling how many holes you dope in during growth.

The headline finding is that detectors built from any of these three materials could outperform the leading existing proposal (superconducting aluminum) and the best candidate found in an exhaustive automated database search by two to three orders of magnitude, across both dark-matter-electron scattering and dark-photon absorption channels. A second, distinct payoff is directional sensitivity: because these materials' response genuinely differs along different crystal axes, and the Earth's daily rotation continuously changes which crystal axis faces the incoming dark-matter "wind," a real dark-matter signal should show a measurable 24-hour modulation pattern that ordinary (isotropic) backgrounds would not — a powerful, largely free way to discriminate signal from noise. Sr₂RuO₄ shows the strongest such modulation of the three, TiSe₂ a smaller but still real one, and cubic-symmetry HDD essentially none.

The interpretive choice worth flagging is that this paper is explicitly a *target-identification* Letter, not a detector paper: it doesn't build anything beyond a small proof-of-concept crystal sample, and every reach curve is a best-case theoretical projection layered on top of DFT calculations that — by the authors' own account — already show real, unresolved quantitative disagreement with experiment for the one material (TiSe₂) where a comparison is even possible.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the technical machinery behind how the paper converts a handful of DFT-computed directions into a full 3D anisotropic response (the "symmetry-aware angular interpolation" using a von Mises–Fisher kernel, described only briefly in the End Matter and deferred to the Supplemental Material) is compressed here into "they interpolate using crystal symmetry" — I did not work through that interpolation scheme's actual mathematics, and the plain-language version above may understate real subtlety in how faithfully it reproduces the true anisotropic response between the sampled directions.

# 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. Dark matter detectors don't have to use generic materials — this paper shows that picking crystals whose electrons already "ring" naturally at the exact tiny energy scale a light dark-matter particle could deliver (via a charge-density-wave gap, a multiband "demon" mode, or tunable doping) can beat both the leading proposed material and an exhaustive computer search by 100- to 1000-fold.

2. The very anisotropy that makes these crystals good detectors also makes them good lie detectors: because their response is different along different crystal axes, and the Earth's rotation constantly changes which axis faces incoming dark matter, a real signal should pulse on a 24-hour cycle in a way ordinary backgrounds shouldn't.

3. This is a target-selection roadmap, not a discovery — every number is a theoretical projection for an idealized future experiment, and even the one material with an experimental comparison point (TiSe₂) already shows real, acknowledged disagreement between the theory and existing measurements.

# 9. Shorter Summary

Dark matter lighter than a proton can only deposit tiny amounts of energy — far too little for conventional detectors, which are tuned to feel a much bigger nuclear kick. This paper proposes three real, already-manufacturable crystals as better targets, each chosen because its electrons naturally oscillate at exactly the low energy scale such faint dark matter could deliver.

Titanium diselenide undergoes a phase transition that freezes out most of its free electrons, dragging its natural oscillation frequency down from a typical metal's scale to a tiny fraction of that. Strontium ruthenate hosts multiple electron populations that can slosh in opposite directions, creating a nearly-invisible-to-ordinary-probes but very-low-frequency collective mode predicted theoretically back in 1956 and only recently confirmed. Heavily boron-doped diamond simply lets researchers dial its natural oscillation frequency up or down by controlling how much boron goes in during growth.

Using detailed first-principles quantum calculations — reusing the same mathematics developed for electron-microscopy experiments — the authors show that detectors built from any of these three materials could out-detect the current best proposed material, and even the best candidate found by an exhaustive computer search of a huge materials database, by one hundred to a thousand times. As a bonus, because these crystals respond differently depending on which way you point them, and the direction of Earth's motion through the galaxy's dark matter effectively rotates once a day relative to a lab-fixed crystal, a genuine dark matter signal should visibly pulse over a 24-hour cycle — a built-in way to distinguish real signal from ordinary background noise, which wouldn't pulse that way.

It's important to be clear about what this paper is and isn't. It's not a dark matter detection — no detector was actually built or run. It's a theoretical proposal, backed by real physics calculations and a small proof-of-concept crystal-growing experiment, arguing that these three material families deserve serious experimental investment. And the calculations themselves aren't perfect: for the one material where an independent experimental check exists, there's already a real, acknowledged gap between theory and measurement — a reminder that "the math says it should work this well" and "an experiment confirms it works this well" remain two different, sequential steps.

## Appendix — Deep-Dive Discussion Log (2026-09-14)

**Prompt:** Unpacking, from first principles, the Section 3 passage on Sr₂RuO₄'s "demon" mode — specifically "David Pines predicted in 1956 that a second mode should also exist: bands sloshing out of phase, so the total charge density stays neutral everywhere."

**Step 0 — why an ordinary plasmon is gapped at all.** In a single-band metal, displacing electrons creates a local charge imbalance; by Poisson's equation this generates a real electric field, which pulls the electrons back and (given their inertia) produces an oscillation. The restoring mechanism is the *long-range* Coulomb interaction: in 3D, Coulomb's 1/r potential has a Fourier transform ~1/q², which diverges as q→0, so even a perfectly uniform (long-wavelength) charge ripple still generates a real restoring field. That's why the plasmon has a finite frequency even at q=0 (a "gap"), unlike an ordinary sound wave. The textbook result: ω_p² = n e²/(ε₀m), where n is carrier density and m is the carrier's effective mass — no q-dependence at leading order, the fingerprint of a long-range-Coulomb-driven mode.

**Step 1 — two bands, one shared field.** With two independent carrier populations (two bands, each with its own density n_i and mass m_i, hence its own "as if alone" plasma frequency ω_pi² = n_ie²/(ε₀m_i)), both bands feel the *same* electric field, sourced by the *total* charge fluctuation δn_total = δn_1+δn_2 (Coulomb's law doesn't care which band a charge came from). Working through continuity + Newton's law for each band, driven by that shared field, gives δn_i = (ω_pi²/ω²)·δn_total for each band. Self-consistency (summing over both bands must reproduce δn_total) forces ω² = ω_p1² + ω_p2² — the **ordinary plasmon**: both bands slosh *in phase* (same sign), producing nonzero total charge, still driven by the same long-range-Coulomb mechanism as Step 0, just gapped at a frequency set by the combined charge of both bands.

**Step 2 — the mode that derivation can't find, and why.** The derivation assumed E is sourced by δn_total and found only one self-consistent solution. But consider δn_1 = −δn_2 exactly, everywhere: δn_total ≡ 0, so by Poisson's equation E = 0 — the long-range Coulomb mechanism simply isn't switched on for this combination. Charge genuinely sloshes back and forth between the two bands, but anything sensing only *total* charge sees nothing. In the bare long-range-Coulomb-only picture this mode has *no restoring force at all* — a "zero mode" at every q. What turns it into a real, finite-frequency acoustic mode (ω∝q, vanishing only at q=0) is residual physics the crude derivation omits: each band's own Fermi/degeneracy pressure and short-range (non-Coulomb) interband coupling. That residual coupling is far weaker than the bare long-range Coulomb interaction, which is why this mode sits at meV scale (Sr₂RuO₄'s demon spans roughly 80–800 meV per this paper's DFT) rather than eV scale. *Worked toy example (illustrative, not the paper's numbers):* ω_p1=1 eV, ω_p2=2 eV gives an ordinary plasmon at ω=√(1²+2²)=√5≈2.24 eV, with δn_1=(1/5)δn_total=0.2·δn_total and δn_2=(4/5)δn_total=0.8·δn_total — both positive multiples of δn_total, confirming "in phase." The demon's exact δn_1/δn_2 split isn't pinned down by this simplified long-range-Coulomb-only derivation — it depends on the residual short-range coupling the toy model omits, flagged honestly as the derivation's limit rather than papered over with an invented number.

**Step 3 — correcting the naive phonon analogy.** In a diatomic lattice, the *acoustic* branch is the sublattices moving *together* (gapless — rigid translation costs zero energy), and the *optical* branch is them moving *oppositely* (gapped — a real short-range spring resists relative displacement at any wavelength). The electronic case is the mirror image: the *in-phase* combination is gapped (ordinary plasmon, resisted by the long-range Coulomb field), and the *out-of-phase* combination is gapless (demon, since no long-range field is generated to resist it). Same "in-phase vs. out-of-phase" language, opposite mechanism, opposite mode gets the gap — carrying phonon intuition over uncritically gets this backwards, refining the "Breaks when" caveat originally attached to the load-balancer analogy in Section 3.

**Step 4 — the "demon" name and how it was ever observed.** Pines' 1956 prediction drew on the same plasma-physics toolkit that gives ion-acoustic waves in an ordinary electron-ion plasma (electrons nearly perfectly screen the ions' long-range field, leaving only a weak residual restoring mechanism — structurally the same idea as Step 2, with two electron bands standing in for electrons-and-ions). Because the mode produces no net charge, it creates no oscillating dipole moment and is essentially invisible to light (optical spectroscopy, which probes q≈0) or to any charge-aggregate-sensing probe — hence "demon." It took until 2023 (Husain et al., *Nature* 621, 66 — this paper's own ref. [26]) to observe it directly, via momentum-resolved EELS (M-EELS) in Sr₂RuO₄: at finite q the two bands' differing velocities mean the cancellation isn't quite exact, leaving a small but real signature — the same finite-q feature this paper's own DFT calculation reproduces in Fig. 1(b)/(e).

**Tie-back:** Sr₂RuO₄ needs multiple *distinct* Fermi-surface sheets (from different Ru 4d orbitals) for this mechanism to have two bands to play off each other — that multiband structure is the entire reason this "strange metal" hosts a demon mode at all, and why this dark-matter-detection paper singled it out over an ordinary single-band metal.
