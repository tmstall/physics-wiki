## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Listening to a Black Hole's Center: How the Ringing Gives Away Where It Ends**

A black hole's fastest-fading "ring tones" secretly carry a number describing how space behaves right at its center. In one exactly solvable quantum-corrected example, that number visibly changes as you listen to higher and higher tones, hinting at a switch from classical to quantum behavior deep inside.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

**The problem.** A black hole has a boundary called the horizon: nothing that crosses it can get back out. At the very center, Einstein's theory of gravity (general relativity) predicts a *singularity*, a spot where the curvature of space grows without limit and the equations stop making sense. Most physicists expect quantum effects to take over there and change the picture. But nobody can go look. Anyone who crossed the horizon couldn't report back.

**The indirect approach.** Black holes ring. Disturb one, and it responds like a struck bell: it vibrates at a set of characteristic tones, and each tone fades at its own rate. These are called *quasinormal modes* (QNMs). The lowest, slowest-fading tones are what gravitational-wave detectors pick up from colliding black holes. This paper cares about the opposite end: the extremely high, extremely fast-fading tones ("highly damped" modes). For about twenty years, theorists have known that these are sensitive to the geometry near the center, not just near the horizon. Motl and Neitzke showed this in 2003 by tracing where a famous number in the Schwarzschild ringing pattern comes from.

**What's hard.** Two obstacles stand in the way of turning that into a real probe:

1. To see *quantum* effects at the center, you need a black-hole solution that includes them exactly. The usual approach adds corrections one at a time, and the paper's introduction explains why that fails: near the center, each correction becomes bigger than the one before it, so cutting the series off gives the wrong answer.
2. Even with an exact solution, you need a way to turn ringing data into a statement about the center.

The authors tackle both, but in a deliberately simplified universe with two space dimensions plus time, where an exact quantum-corrected black hole is known.

**Paper Type & Stakes.** This is a short theoretical-methods paper (a Letter) with one worked example. It's a proof of principle: could ringing data *in principle* reveal the center of a black hole, including where quantum effects take over? Nothing here is observable with any current or planned instrument.

**Prior Belief Check.** Nothing is overturned. The core idea, that high overtones encode the center, is about twenty years old. Two things are new: a cleaner way of reading the information out (Section 4, Step 4), and an explicit demonstration that a classical-to-quantum switch shows up in the ringing. Experts won't be shocked by either, and the idea is clearly "in the air." The paper's own "Note added" says two other papers appeared during its final preparation that overlap with it, and it says a formula in it was uncovered independently by Hartnoll & Zhiboedov. So the fair reading is incremental but well executed and timely.

**Replication & Convergence Note.** The general readout formula has independent support: Hartnoll & Zhiboedov reached it by a different method. The headline example, the classical-to-quantum switch in the exact quantum black hole, comes from one group and its own numerics. Independent confirmation would mean another group computing the ringing of that same black hole with a different numerical method and finding the same drift in the measured exponent. It would also confirm the path assumption described in Section 3d.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

### 3a. One function describes the black hole, and its blow-up is the "singularity"

In these models, one function f(r) of the distance r from the center encodes how time and distance are stretched. Outside the horizon f is positive. Inside it's negative. Near the center, f usually blows up as a power of 1/r.

*Worked example (define → check → generalize).* For an ordinary uncharged black hole in 4D, f = 1 − 2M/r. Take units where 2M = 1 and look at r = 0.001: f = 1 − 1000 = −999. The "1" is irrelevant; only the 1/r piece matters. So near the center f behaves like −(constant)/r, which is a power law with **exponent s = 1**. A larger s means a more violent blow-up. That exponent s is the number the paper wants to read from the ringing.

### 3b. Ringing, tones and overtones

**Analogy:** think of a struck bell. It rings at a set of allowed tones. Each tone has a pitch and a fade rate, and the *overtone number n* (0, 1, 2, …) labels them from the lowest up. For a black hole, each tone is one number with two parts: the real part is the pitch and the imaginary part is the fade rate. If you know linear algebra, these are like eigenvalues: the special values at which the wave equation has an allowed solution.

**Breaks when:** you treat the black hole as an object you could touch. A bell's tones come from the metal. A black hole's tones come from the empty curved space around it, with a one-way exit (the horizon), and that exit is why the tones fade.

For very high n, the tones form a regular *ladder*: each rung sits a fixed step above the last, plus a small shift. The paper reads information from that shift and from tiny corrections to it.

### 3c. Why the highest overtones look at the center

**Concept:** the higher the overtone number n, the more finely wiggly the wave. A very wiggly wave ignores smooth, gentle features of the terrain it travels through. It responds only where the terrain is extremely sharp. In a black hole, the sharpest place is the center. So as n grows, the ringing pattern is controlled more and more by exactly how the geometry blows up near r = 0, that is, by s. If the center has two layers (say a mild outer layer and a harsh inner core), moderate n sees the outer layer and very large n sees the core.

**Vehicle:** the memory-mountain benchmark. You sweep the working-set size of a loop and record average access latency. Small working sets sit on the L1 plateau, then latency steps up through L2, L3 and DRAM. Each plateau's height identifies a level of the hierarchy, and each step marks where a level runs out. You never open the chip. You learn its layers from how one measured number responds to one swept parameter.

**Analogy:**

| Black hole | Memory mountain |
|---|---|
| Overtone number n | Working-set size |
| The measured exponent (defined in Section 4) | Measured latency |
| A flat stretch of that exponent | A plateau |
| Outer classical layer / inner quantum core | Two levels of the hierarchy |
| n where the exponent changes | The capacity cliff between levels |

**Breaks when:** you ask how the probe reaches its target. The benchmark's loads physically travel to the level they measure. The ringing never travels to r = 0. The dependence comes from mathematical continuation through a *complex plane* (next subsection), along a path that has to be assumed. The cache picture has no slot for "the answer depends on an assumed route."

**Central analogy for this paper:** Memory-mountain sweep: overtone number as working-set size.

### 3d. Complex numbers and "Stokes lines" (just enough to follow the logic)

To find the allowed tones, the math follows the wave from the outer edge of the universe to the horizon and demands the right behavior at each end. The technique lets the distance r take *complex* values, numbers with two parts such as 3 + 2i, which you can picture as points on a flat plane instead of a line.

Along most routes through that plane, the two basic wave solutions differ in size by an astronomical factor, and the smaller one gets swamped. *Worked numbers:* if the fading part of the exponent is 50, the solutions differ by e^100, about 3 × 10^43. The fix is to follow special routes where both solutions stay the same size, called **Stokes lines**. Which routes connect the boundary, the center and the horizon depends on the global layout of the plane, and the paper *assumes* that layout matches a well-studied case (a "Schwarzschild-AdS-like" layout).

**Analogy:** in double-precision floating point, 10^16 + 1 gives back 10^16, because the 1 is lost below the last stored bit (I checked this in Python). Off a Stokes line, the smaller solution is the "1" that disappears.

**Breaks when:** you think more bits would fix it. Floating-point loss is a precision artifact. The Stokes phenomenon is built into the math: the smaller solution's coefficient genuinely changes as you cross certain curves. That's why the layout of the routes matters, and why it's an assumption, not a detail.

### 3e. Kasner exponents and eons: how space stretches and squeezes at the center

Approaching the singularity, imagine a clock τ that reads 0 at the singularity. Distances along each direction scale like τ raised to a power p (its *Kasner exponent*): one exponent per direction. A positive p means that direction shrinks to nothing, a negative p means it stretches without bound.

*Worked example.* Take the exponents (−1/3, 2/3, 2/3) at τ = 0.001. The first direction scales by 0.001^(−1/3) = 10, so it's stretched 10×. The other two scale by 0.001^(2/3) = 0.01, so they're crushed to 1%. In ordinary 4D gravity in a vacuum, two rules constrain the exponents: they add up to 1, and their squares add up to 1. Check: −1/3 + 2/3 + 2/3 = 1, and 1/9 + 4/9 + 4/9 = 1. ✓

A stretch of time with fixed exponents is an *epoch*. Physicists have proposed longer regimes called *eons*, periods dominated by one type of physics: classical gravity, or quantum corrections. Crossing from one eon to another changes the rules that set the exponents.

**Analogy:** SimPoint-style phase analysis of a long program run: an epoch is a stable phase, and an eon is a change in what limits performance (say compute-bound to memory-bound).

**Breaks when:** you look for a boundary. Program phases usually change at an identifiable point in the code. An eon change is a smooth crossover inside one continuous function, roughly where two competing terms become equal.

### 3f. The "quantum BTZ" black hole

**BTZ** is a black hole in a simplified universe with two space dimensions plus time. That universe is *anti-de Sitter* (AdS) space, which you can picture as a universe with a built-in reflecting wall: waves reach the boundary and bounce back, which makes the math well behaved. The classical BTZ black hole has a *mild* center: f is a constant there, and the curvature never blows up.

**Quantum BTZ (qBTZ)** adds the effect of a strongly interacting quantum field pushing back on the geometry ("backreaction"). That adds a term that grows like 1/r, and now the center is a true singularity. A knob called ℓ sets how strong the quantum effect is: small ℓ means weak. The solution is *exact* for any ℓ, because it can be built as a slice through a bigger classical solution in one more dimension (a "braneworld" construction).

**Analogy:** profiler overhead. Instrumenting a program changes its timing. Usually you ignore that, but "backreaction" means including the effect exactly.

**Breaks when:** you treat the quantum effect as a measurement artifact. It's a real physical stress in spacetime, not an observer effect.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### Step 1 — Turn ringing into a wave crossing a landscape

Separating out the swirling motion around the black hole leaves a one-dimensional problem: a wave moving through a "landscape" (technically an effective potential). The landscape has three special places: the outer boundary of AdS, the horizon, and the center. At the high frequencies of high overtones, only places where the landscape blows up matter. That means the center. The other two ends are simple.

### Step 2 — Near the center, the problem is solved by standard wave functions and one number

Close to the center, the equation matches a standard one whose solutions are *Bessel functions*. Think of them as the sine and cosine of radial geometry. Everything about the near-center solution is fixed by one number:

$$
\nu = \frac{s}{2(s+1)}
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\nu$ : a dimensionless number controlling how waves behave near the center
- $s$ : the singularity exponent from Section 3a (how violently $f$ blows up)

**What this actually means:** the paper compresses the center's whole character into one number, ν. *Worked numbers:* s = 0 (classical BTZ, where f is constant) gives ν = 0. s = 1 (the quantum black hole, and also 4D Schwarzschild) gives ν = 1/4. s = 2 gives ν = 1/3. As s grows large, ν creeps toward 1/2. So ν is a smooth, slowly climbing function of s, and reading ν tells you s.

### Step 3 — The ladder of tones, and why its shift isn't enough

Matching solutions along Stokes routes gives the ladder from Section 3b: the n-th tone sits at n times π (in the paper's units), plus a fixed shift set only by s. For s = 1, the shift is (ln 2)/4, about 0.173. *Check:* cos(π/4) = 0.7071, doubled is √2 = 1.4142, its natural log is 0.3466, and half of that is 0.1733. ✓ That's exactly the leading term in the paper's quantum-black-hole result.

The catch: this shift depends on the *assumed layout* of Stokes routes. Choose a different layout and you'd get a different shift. So the shift alone can't be trusted as a readout of s.

### Step 4 — The trick that makes the readout robust

The authors look at tiny corrections on top of the ladder. One correction depends on the *angular momentum number m*: how many times the wave winds around the black hole (m = 0 means no swirl). The paper finds that this correction is proportional to m² and shrinks with overtone number like n^(−2ν). Now take the ringing at two swirl numbers, m₁ and m₂, and subtract. Everything that doesn't depend on m cancels, including the assumption-sensitive ladder. What's left is proportional to (m₁² − m₂²) × n^(−2ν). The unknown proportionality constant still depends on the assumed layout, but it's just a number multiplying the curve. *The exponent of n does not depend on it*.

*Worked example.* If ν = 1/4, quadrupling n shrinks the leftover by 4^(1/2) = 2. If ν = 0, it doesn't shrink at all. If ν = 1/3, it shrinks by 4^(2/3) ≈ 2.52. To turn that into a measurement, the paper defines a local estimate:

$$
\nu_{\text{eff}} = -\frac{\ln\left(|\Delta_{n+1}|/|\Delta_n|\right)}{2\ln\left((n+1)/n\right)}
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\nu_{\text{eff}}$ : the exponent measured locally, at overtone $n$
- $\Delta_n$ : the leftover after subtracting the two swirl numbers, at overtone $n$
- $\ln$ : natural logarithm

**What this actually means:** it asks "how much did the leftover shrink when n grew a little, and what exponent explains that?" *Worked numbers:* suppose the leftover is 0.100 at n = 100 and 0.050 at n = 400. It halved while n quadrupled: −ln(0.5) / (2 ln 4) = 0.693 / 2.773 = **0.25** ✓. If the geometry is layered, ν_eff changes with n, and that change is the signal.

**Analogy:** the "subtract the empty loop" trick in microbenchmarking. You can't trust an absolute timing because it contains fixed overhead you can't model. So you time two versions that differ only in the thing you care about and look at how the *difference* scales.

**Breaks when:** you assume the overhead cancels for free. In a benchmark, identical overhead is guaranteed by construction. Here, the claim that the unknown pieces don't depend on m is argued analytically and checked numerically for only one black hole.

### Step 5 — Translating the exponent into stretch-and-squeeze exponents

The measured number is a combination of the Kasner exponents from Section 3e (the paper's eq. 38):

$$
q = \frac{2\,(1-p_\phi)}{1-p_t}
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $q$ : the power of $n$ in the leftover (equal to $2\nu$)
- $p_\phi$ : Kasner exponent of the direction that goes around the black hole
- $p_t$ : Kasner exponent of the direction along the time coordinate, which is spatial inside the horizon

**What this actually means:** the ringing hands you *one* number, a particular mix of the two exponents. It can't separate them. In the simplest case, with one blackening factor, the geometry itself ties them together and you get both.

*Worked check.* Plugging s = 1 into the paper's formulas (eq. 37) gives (p_t, p_φ) = (−1/3, 2/3) for the quantum black hole. Then q = 2 × (1/3) / (4/3) = 1/2, which equals 2ν = 2 × 1/4. ✓ For classical BTZ, (p_t, p_φ) = (0, 1), giving q = 0, which equals 2ν = 0. ✓ The formula is consistent at both ends.

### Step 6 — The layered center of the quantum black hole

The exact quantum black hole has f(r) = r²/ℓ₃² − 8G₃M − ℓF(M)/r. In plain words: a term that grows with r², a constant "classical BTZ" term, and a quantum term proportional to 1/r.

*Worked numbers* (my own toy values: ℓ₃ = 1, 8G₃M = 1, ℓF = 0.01):

| r | f(r) | Which term dominates |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | −0.77 | classical constant (−1) |
| 0.1 | −1.09 | mostly classical |
| 0.01 | about −2.00 | classical and quantum equal |
| 0.001 | −11.00 | quantum term (−10) |

So this black hole has a classical outer layer and a quantum-dominated core, two eons in one function. Higher overtones probe closer to the center, so at moderate n the measured exponent should look classical (near 0) and at very large n it should look quantum (near 1/4).

That's what the numerics show. **Figure 1** compares numerically computed tones with the analytic formula at ℓ = 10, for m = 1 and m = 2. Agreement improves as n climbs from 10 to 1,000. **Figure 2** is the headline: the measured exponent against overtone number, for ℓ from 10^-4 to 10^3. For small ℓ the exponent starts near 0 and climbs toward 1/4. Larger ℓ moves the switch to lower n. For the largest ℓ, the classical layer is gone and the exponent sits near 1/4 from the start. The caption says that for the smallest ℓ, convergence would take hundreds of thousands of overtones.

```
near-center behavior (exponent s)
   → one number, ν
   → the swirl-dependent leftover shrinks like n^(−2ν)
   → measure the shrink rate → recover ν → recover s
   → translate into stretch/squeeze exponents
   → a change in the measured ν with n = a change of eon
```

*Analyst-authored flow chart of the paper's logic. The assumed Stokes layout affects the ladder, but the subtraction step is designed to remove that from the exponent.*

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "looking inside" means something an outsider could measure. **The paper actually says** the information is available only in principle. The tones involved (n from about 100 to 100,000) fade far too fast for any real detector. The paper points toward a mathematical dual description in which such tones are observable, but doesn't carry that out.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the subtraction trick makes the method independent of the assumed Stokes layout. **The paper actually says** only that the *exponent of n* is fixed by the local structure at the center. The constants in front are computed for the assumed layout, and the paper argues, but doesn't prove, that the exponent survives for other layouts. It checks that numerically for only one black hole.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the method returns *the* Kasner exponents. **The paper actually says** it returns one combination. Only when the geometry has a single blackening factor do both exponents follow.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the "quantum eon" means quantized geometry. **The paper actually says** qBTZ is *semiclassical*: an ordinary classical geometry sourced by a large quantum field. My own check (analyst inference): the exponents (−1/3, 2/3) match the time and angle directions of the 4D Schwarzschild black hole's interior, (−1/3, 2/3, 2/3). That would explain why they break the vacuum rules for a 3D universe (they add up to 1/3, not 1). It's a slice of a 4D vacuum result, so the "quantum" core is, in the underlying 4D picture, a classical singularity.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**1. The subtraction readout (new in this form, developed in parallel by others).** The leading ladder shift has been known to depend on the singularity exponent since the mid-2000s, but it hangs on the assumed layout. Reading the exponent from *how the difference between two swirl numbers shrinks* removes that dependence from the exponent. The paper's own Note added says another paper reached a version of this in parallel. The idea is clean experimental design applied to asymptotic math.

**2. An exact worked example of an eon switch showing up in ringing (new to the field).** Exact solutions with a controllable classical-to-quantum crossover are rare. Here one knob (ℓ) moves the crossover, and the switch appears as the measured exponent moving between two predicted plateaus, 0 and 1/4.

*New to you vs. new to the field:* that high overtones probe the center is old. The subtraction readout and the explicit eon switch are the new parts.

**Adversarial-posture verdict:** the paper earns a real core. The derivation is explicit, the key integrals have closed forms, and the predictions are checked against independent numerics. But the core is narrower than the title: *reading one scaling exponent of a simplified, semiclassical center, under an assumed path layout.*

### Predictive Content Check

1. **Falsifiable handle:** the paper makes sharp predictions *inside* its own theory. For the quantum black hole at large n, the swirl-difference must shrink like n^(−1/2), which means quadrupling n halves it. The measured exponent must level off at 1/4, and the switch from 0 to 1/4 must move to larger n as ℓ shrinks. Any of these could have failed against the numerics. They passed. There's **no observational handle**: nothing here can be measured in real ringdown, and the alternative description in which it could be measured isn't computed.
2. **Formalism load** (checked because this is a preprint with heavy math): the machinery is doing real work, not decoration. The special integrals produce the n^(−1/2) scaling and the prefactor that the numerics confirm. The weak joint is a substantive *assumption* the math takes as given rather than derives: the Stokes layout.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**The Stokes layout is assumed, and the claim that the readout survives other layouts is argued, not proven.** The exponent's independence from the layout is checked numerically only for the one quantum black hole. **(B) Contested** — the paper itself flags the layout as an assumption and argues it can be relaxed, while a third-party review summary (a secondary source I couldn't verify) says contour-independence isn't shown. **(paper, p. 2 and Supplement; broader literature)**

**Only one combination of Kasner exponents can be read out.** With two blackening factors, the ringing can't separate them. **(A) Consensus** — the paper says so directly. **(paper, near eq. 38)**

**Convergence is very slow for weak quantum effects.** For small ℓ, the exponent reaches its quantum value only after hundreds of thousands of overtones. That's hardest exactly where quantum effects are subtle. **(A) Consensus** — stated in the Fig. 2 caption. **(paper, Fig. 2)**

**No route to observation.** These tones are far too damped to measure in real ringdown, and the mathematically dual picture in which they could be measured isn't worked out. **(A) Consensus** — the paper presents this as in-principle and future work. **(paper, Discussion)**

**Older related results aren't cited.** Das & Shankaranarayanan (hep-th/0410209) and Ghosh, Shankaranarayanan & Das (hep-th/0510186) computed high-overtone ringing for black holes with generic power-law centers, including AdS, and I found no citation to either in this Letter's references. **(C) Speculative** — I compared only abstracts, and couldn't check whether the companion paper cites them or how closely they match the Letter's eq. 14. **(analyst inference)**

**The "quantum" core is a classical 4D singularity seen from a slice.** Whether the switch reflects quantum-gravitational physics, and not only the large quantum field's effect in this construction, is outside what the paper shows. **(C) Speculative** — my own identification, from the exponent match in Section 4. **(analyst inference)**

**One simple probe, one simple black hole.** The probe is a basic wave, the black hole is static, uncharged and 2+1-dimensional, and it's a single exactly solvable example. Whether the readout survives in more realistic settings is open. **(A) Consensus** — the paper lists charge, other asymptotics, higher dimensions and matter as future work. **(paper, Discussion)**

**Next 12–24 months:** an independent numerical check of Fig. 2; a direct computation of the Stokes layout for the quantum black hole; the observable-side version of the readout (a related paper, arXiv:2609.23776, is already moving that way); and consistency with new bounds on Kasner exponents (arXiv:2609.16980).

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

**Summary.** The authors ask whether the very high, fast-fading overtones of a black hole's ringing can reveal the shape of its center, and whether they'd show a switch from classical to quantum behavior there. They work in a simplified universe with two space dimensions plus time, where an exact quantum-corrected black hole exists.

Near the center, the geometry behaves like a power law with exponent s, and the wave equation there is solved by Bessel functions with index ν = s/(2(s+1)). Following the wave through the complex plane along Stokes lines gives a regular ladder of tones with a fixed shift that depends on s. But that shift also depends on an assumed layout of the path, so the authors turn to a tiny correction that depends on how much the wave swirls around the hole. That correction shrinks with overtone number as n^(−2ν). Subtracting the ringing at two swirl numbers cancels everything else. Fitting how the difference shrinks gives ν, hence s, hence one combination of the center's stretch-and-squeeze exponents.

Applied to the exact quantum black hole, the center has two layers: a mild classical layer with exponents (0, 1) and a quantum-dominated core with exponents (−1/3, 2/3). The measured exponent drifts from 0 to 1/4 as the overtone number rises, and the numerics agree with the analytic prediction. That drift is the spectral signature of a switch between eons. It shows up at lower overtones for stronger quantum effects and needs hundreds of thousands of overtones for very weak ones.

**Why it's framed this way.** Under the adversarial reading, the main contribution is the *subtraction trick*, so I built the analysis around it rather than around the older leading-order result. I also flagged the slice-of-4D interpretation, because the word "quantum" in "quantum eon" can mislead: it describes the brane-side view, while the deeper picture is a classical singularity. The takeaway is that the method is sound and the example is clean, its generality is argued rather than proven, and several groups reached similar ideas at the same time.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **A black hole's highest overtones are a profiler for its center.** How fast the swirl-dependent part of the ringing shrinks with overtone number encodes an exponent describing how violently the geometry blows up at the center.

2. **Subtracting two measurements beats trusting one.** The ladder shift depends on an assumed path through the complex plane. The *difference* between two swirl numbers removes that dependence from the exponent, like subtracting an empty loop in a microbenchmark.

3. **In an exact quantum-corrected black hole, the ringing shows the center changing rules.** The measured exponent climbs from 0 to 1/4 as you go to higher tones. In the underlying 4D picture, that "quantum" core is a classical singularity seen through a slice.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Black holes hide their centers behind a horizon, and the center is where quantum effects are expected to matter most. Nobody can look, so this paper explores an indirect route: listen to how a black hole rings.

A disturbed black hole vibrates like a struck bell, at a set of tones that each fade at their own rate. Physicists have known for about twenty years that the very highest, fastest-fading tones are sensitive to the geometry at the center. What this paper adds is a way to read a number from those tones: an exponent describing how violently the geometry blows up there.

The trick is to compare waves that swirl around the black hole at two different rates and subtract. The overall pattern depends on an unproven assumption about how the math connects, and it cancels out. What's left shrinks at a rate that depends only on the center's exponent. Measure the shrink rate and you've measured the exponent.

The authors test this on an exactly solvable, simplified black hole in a universe with two space dimensions, one that includes the back-reaction of a quantum field. Its center has two layers: a mild classical outer layer and a harsher quantum-dominated core. At higher and higher tones, the measured exponent climbs from zero to one quarter, a fingerprint of the switch between layers, which the authors call a transition between "Kasner eons."

Caveats: the method's generality rests on an assumption the authors argue for but don't prove. The tones involved are far too faint to observe in nature. Weak quantum effects would need hundreds of thousands of tones to show. And in the underlying four-dimensional picture, the "quantum" core is an ordinary classical singularity. It's a clean proof of principle, not a practical probe.

## Appendix — Deep-Dive Discussion Log (2026-09-29)

### Q1. "More details" on the complex-distance / Stokes-line passage (§3d)

**The passage probed** (from §3d): the math lets the distance take complex values; along most routes one of the two basic solutions is astronomically bigger than the other and the small one is lost; the fix is to follow special routes (Stokes lines) where both stay comparable; which routes connect the boundary, horizon and center depends on the layout of the whole map, and the paper assumes a "Schwarzschild-AdS-like" layout.

**1. Why a complex distance is allowed.** The wave equation is an ordinary differential equation in the radial distance r. Its coefficients are smooth functions of r, so it still makes sense for complex r. Physical answers come from the real ends: the wave must vanish at the AdS boundary and must be purely ingoing at the horizon. The complex plane is only a route between those ends. Paper eq. (5) works in the tortoise coordinate, ξ(r) = ∫ dr / f(r), which turns the wave equation into d²R/dz² + (1 − U)R = 0 with z = ωξ.

**2. The two basic solutions and the size problem.** At large |ω|, U is small away from the singular points, so the two solutions are e^{+iωξ} and e^{−iωξ}. For complex ωξ their sizes are |e^{+iωξ}| = e^{−Im(ωξ)} and |e^{−iωξ}| = e^{+Im(ωξ)}, a ratio of e^{2·Im(ωξ)}. *Analyst toy numbers (checked in Python):* |ω| = 100 and Im ξ = 0.3 give Im(ωξ) = 30 and a ratio e^60 ≈ 1.1 × 10²⁶. Double precision keeps about 2⁵³ ≈ 9 × 10¹⁵ (ln 2⁵³ = 36.7), so it loses the small term once Im(ωξ) passes about 18.4; 10¹⁶ + 1 == 10¹⁶ is True. The boundary conditions are statements that one exponential's coefficient is exactly zero (for example, "no outgoing wave at the horizon"). You cannot enforce that on a route where that exponential is 10²⁶ times smaller than its partner.

**3. The fix: routes where Im(ωξ) = 0.** Along such a route both exponentials have size 1, so both coefficients stay visible and can be matched term by term. This is the paper's Stokes-line matching. *Naming note (analyst, as far as I know):* in the pure-math convention these curves are anti-Stokes lines, and "Stokes lines" are where the imbalance is largest. The QNM literature and this paper use "Stokes" for the both-comparable case. The physics does not depend on the name.

**4. The local map at each of the three places (paper's Supplement, eqs. 51–52). (A)**

- *Boundary:* exactly one route reaches it, and along it Arg(ω) = −Arg(ξ₀). A real ω would need a real ξ₀.
- *Horizon:* ξ ∝ log(r − r_h), so the routes spiral into the horizon.
- *Center:* near r = 0, ξ ≈ −r^{s+1} / (a(s+1)), so ωξ is real when (s+1)·Arg(r) equals a constant plus kπ. Rays leave the center every π/(s+1). Hand check: s = 0 gives 2 rays, 180° apart; s = 1 gives 4 rays, 90° apart; s = 2 gives 6 rays, 60° apart, which is 2(s+1) in every case (the paper's count for integer s).

**5. What "the layout" means, and why it is an assumption. (A) for the assumption, (B) for the claim that the exponent survives other layouts.** The local pictures are derived. What the math does not hand you is which lane at the center links to which route to the boundary and which to the horizon, because that depends on the whole f(r). The paper does not compute it for the quantum black hole (paper p. 2 and Supplement). It assumes the Schwarzschild-AdS pattern: come in from the boundary, turn one lane counterclockwise (π/(s+1) in r), and go out to the horizon.

Since z ∝ r^{s+1}, a one-lane turn in r is a half-turn in z, i.e. z → z·e^{iπ} (paper eq. 55). That gives the Bessel functions the phases e^{i2α±}, with 2α₊ = π/2 + πν. For s = 1 (ν = 1/4) that is 3π/4 (checked). Those phases feed the offset in the ladder, ωξ₀ = nπ + (i/2) ln[2cos(πs/(2(1+s)))]. A different layout (a turn by two lanes, or the other way round) would change the phases. The paper works out only the one-lane case, so what the alternative offsets would be is not derived here. That is why the offset alone cannot be trusted as a readout of s, and why the paper uses the m-difference trick, which cancels the layout-dependent constant and leaves the exponent. That the exponent is layout-independent is argued, not proven.

**6. Analyst check: why ξ₀ is complex, using classical BTZ. (analyst inference, spacing only)** With ξ(r) = ∫₀^r dr / f, the integral to infinity passes a simple pole at the horizon, which gives ξ₀ an imaginary part of magnitude π / f′(r_h). For f = r² − 1 (r_h = 1, f′(1) = 2): the principal-value part is 0 (confirmed numerically), so |ξ₀| = π/2, and the ladder spacing is π/|ξ₀| = 2, which equals 4πT with T = f′(r_h)/(4π) = 1/(2π). That matches the known BTZ tone spacing and puts ω on the imaginary axis, consistent with Arg(ω) = −Arg(ξ₀). This tests the spacing only, not the offset. The sign convention (which side of the pole the path goes) was not chased.

**7. Where the analogies fail.**

- *Floating-point absorption:* it is a precision artifact, so more bits would fix it. The subdominant coefficient in this problem genuinely changes as you cross certain curves (the Stokes phenomenon proper).
- *Unstable recurrences (forward recurrence for J_n with n > x, where the dominant solution contaminates the minimal one):* the Bessel functions near the center are exact solutions, so the rotation z → ze^{iπ} uses an exact identity, not asymptotic bookkeeping.
