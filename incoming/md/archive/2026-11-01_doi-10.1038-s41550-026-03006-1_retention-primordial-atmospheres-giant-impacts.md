## 1. A heavier planet keeps its air

**Hook:** A planet-sized collision should blast away a young world's hydrogen, but this paper argues the opposite: the mass the impactor adds tightens gravity's grip on the gas far more than the impact's heat loosens it, so collisions may be how sub-Neptunes are made rather than how they are destroyed.

---

## 2. Big-Picture Context

The Kepler telescope found that small planets come in two sizes. Planets between Earth and Neptune pile up near 1.3 and 2.4 times Earth's radius, with a clear shortage near 1.75 times Earth's radius. This gap is called the radius valley. The standard reading is that both groups have rocky interiors of similar make-up, but the larger group still wears a thin hydrogen-rich envelope left over from birth, while the smaller group has lost it. The smaller planets are called super-Earths; the larger ones, sub-Neptunes. Why some planets lost their envelopes and others did not is one of the central puzzles in exoplanet science.

Two explanations dominate. In the first, called photoevaporation, X-rays and ultraviolet light from the young star heat the top of the atmosphere and drive it off. In the second, called core-powered mass loss, heat leaking out of the planet's own hot interior does the same job over a billion years or more. Core-powered mass loss needs a hot interior, and giant impacts are a natural source of heat. Several population studies therefore treated late giant impacts, the collisions that happen after the gas disk around the star has cleared, as envelope-removal events that help turn sub-Neptunes into super-Earths.

This paper challenges that picture with an accounting argument. A collision does two things at once: it heats the planet, and it makes the planet heavier. The authors argue that earlier work handled the heating carefully but did not fold the added mass into the atmosphere's gravitational binding at the same step of the calculation. Once both are counted together, along with the parts of the impact energy that never reach the surface (the impactor's iron sinking straight to the center, and rock-melting soaking up heat), the authors find that a late giant impact leaves a planet *more* able to hold its hydrogen than it was before the collision. They then run a synthetic population of 100 million planets and reproduce the observed two-peak radius pattern.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is a theoretical population-synthesis paper that combines a simplified planet-interior model with an atmospheric-escape model to argue that late giant impacts favor envelope retention; at stake is which physical process sorts small planets into super-Earths and sub-Neptunes.

**Prior Belief Check:** The headline result contradicts a strand of the literature that treats impacts as envelope strippers (for example, studies that pair impact heating with core-powered mass loss). It does not overturn the radius valley as a mass-threshold phenomenon; most leading models, photoevaporation included, already predict that heavier rocky cores keep their envelopes. Experts will find the *reversal* of the impact's sign interesting, but much of its force rests on a modeling choice the authors defended in their own earlier paper: that the temperature at the place where gas actually escapes is set by starlight, not by how hot the surface is. That choice is contested by the core-powered mass-loss community, though one independent group (Tang, Fortney and Murray-Clay, 2024) has also concluded that long-lived core-powered loss is weak. Treat this as a significant reframing within an ongoing debate, not a settled result.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is a single-group result that builds directly on the same authors' earlier series of escape papers; no independent group has yet reproduced the reversal with a different interior and escape model. A paper by Zlimen, Schlichting and Rogers posted the same week (arXiv:2610.38852, not read here) addresses giant impacts and the radius valley through hydrogen dissolving into magma, and comparing the two would be a natural first test. Independent confirmation would mean another code, with its own treatment of where escaping gas gets its temperature, finding that impacted planets retain their envelopes more often than unimpacted ones.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

### Why does a sliver of hydrogen double a planet's size?

A rocky planet five times Earth's mass is only about one and a half times Earth's radius, because rock and iron are dense and compress under their own weight. Wrap that same rock in hydrogen amounting to well under one percent of the planet's mass and the radius can grow to more than twice Earth's. Hydrogen is so light that a small mass of it, held up by heat, puffs out into a tall layer. That is why the radius valley can separate two families whose interiors are nearly identical: the dividing line is not how much rock a planet has, but whether a thin hydrogen layer survived.

**Analogy:** Think of a server chassis with and without a fan shroud. The electronics inside are the same, but the shroud, which weighs almost nothing, changes the box's outer dimensions dramatically. Measuring outer size alone splits the population into "shrouded" and "bare" boxes.

**Breaks when:** you ask what holds the shroud in place. A shroud is bolted on; the hydrogen layer is held only by gravity against its own heat, so it can leak away continuously. The rest of this paper is about that leak.

### How does a planet leak its atmosphere?

Near the planet, gravity holds hydrogen tightly. Far from the planet, gravity weakens, and gas that is warm enough flows outward in a steady wind. Somewhere between, the outward flow speeds up past the local speed of sound. That location is the **sonic point**. Below it, the gas is held in a slow, nearly static atmosphere; above it, the gas is on its way out and cannot be called back. The rate of loss is set almost entirely by how dense the gas is at the sonic point, and that density depends exponentially on how deep the gas must climb out of the gravity well compared with its thermal energy.

For a chemist, this is an Arrhenius law. The "activation energy" is the gravitational binding of one molecule, which grows with the planet's mass. The "temperature" is the gas temperature at the sonic point. Raise the barrier or lower the temperature, and the escape rate drops exponentially.

The paper uses a fitted form of this law. The sonic-point distance is

$$R_s = \frac{G M_n \bar{\mu}}{2 k_B T_s}$$

and the loss rate is

$$\frac{dM_{\rm atm}}{dt} = 1.26\times 10^{15}\left(\frac{R_s}{R_\oplus}\right)^2 e^{-\frac{19}{27}\frac{R_s}{R_\oplus}}\ \ {\rm kg\,s^{-1}}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $R_s$ : distance from the planet's center to the sonic point (m)
- $G$ : the gravitational constant
- $M_n$ : mass of the rocky planet (the paper approximates it by the total mass) (kg)
- $\bar{\mu}$ : average mass of one gas molecule (about 2.3 atomic mass units for hydrogen with helium)
- $k_B$ : Boltzmann's constant
- $T_s$ : gas temperature at the sonic point (K)
- $R_\oplus$ : Earth's radius
- $dM_{\rm atm}/dt$ : rate at which atmosphere mass is lost (kg per second)

**What this actually means:** The first equation says the sonic point sits farther out when the planet is heavier or the gas is cooler. The second says loss falls off exponentially with that distance. Because heavier means farther out means exponentially slower loss, the escape timescale is a cliff, not a slope. This is the same structure as subthreshold leakage in a transistor: each step up in threshold voltage cuts the leakage current by a fixed factor, so a modest shift moves a device from "leaky" to "tight."

*Analyst-built illustration (not from the paper):* the escape cliff for a planet with no impact, computed from the two equations above at the population's average stellar heating (sonic-point temperature about 1,100 K).

```
 rocky mass        sonic point        time to lose the envelope
 (Earth masses)    (Earth radii)
 ------------------------------------------------------------
   4                  ~37             ~60 million years   LOST
   5                  ~44             ~11 billion years   edge
   6                  ~51             ~2,400 billion yrs  KEPT
 ------------------------------------------------------------
 planet age in the model: 5 billion years
 one extra Earth mass  ->  escape time x ~200
```

*What to look at:* the right-hand column jumps by a factor of a few hundred for each added Earth mass, so a planet near the threshold flips from bare to wrapped with a small mass gain. That is the lever the whole paper pulls.

> **Concept:** Escape is controlled at the sonic point, which lies tens of planet radii out. Its loss rate depends exponentially on the ratio of gravitational binding (set by planet mass) to gas temperature there. The paper's key physical claim is that the sonic-point temperature is normally set by starlight, not by the surface temperature, because the hot gas near the surface stops convecting and radiates its heat away well below the sonic point. The one exception is a surface so hot that convection reaches all the way up to the sonic point.

> **Vehicle:** A transistor's off-state leakage current depends exponentially on its threshold voltage divided by the channel temperature. The channel sits under a heat spreader that holds it near the coolant temperature. Heat dumped deep into the bulk silicon below raises the bulk temperature, but the spreader carries it away before it reaches the channel. Raising the threshold voltage, by contrast, cuts leakage everywhere at once. Only if the bulk gets so hot that the spreader saturates does the channel temperature follow it upward.

> **Analogy:** Threshold voltage ↔ gravitational binding per molecule, which rises with planet mass. Channel temperature ↔ sonic-point temperature. Coolant temperature ↔ the planet's equilibrium temperature set by its star. Heat dumped into the bulk ↔ impact heat deposited in the mantle. Spreader carrying heat away ↔ the radiative layer that decouples the surface from the sonic point. Spreader saturation ↔ the convective layer reaching the sonic point.

> **Breaks when:** you ask about the reservoir. A transistor leaks from an unlimited supply; a planet leaks from a finite envelope of a few tenths of a percent of its mass, so "leak rate" turns into "how long until empty," and the outcome is a race between that time and the planet's age. The vehicle has no slot for a supply that runs out, which is why the paper's real decision variable is a timescale, not a rate.

### Where does the energy of a giant impact go?

A giant impact is a collision between bodies of planetary size, with the smaller one carrying at least a tenth of the larger one's mass. Collisions happen near the speed at which the two bodies would just escape each other, so the energy released is roughly the gravitational binding energy gained when the two merge. The paper argues that this energy does not all heat the surface. First, the impactor's iron core plunges through the molten rock to the center and stays hot down there, contributing little to heating the rock above. Second, much of the energy that does go into the rock is spent melting it rather than raising its temperature. For a planet with an Earth-like iron fraction, the authors estimate that the rocky mantle receives about 84 percent of the impact energy, and a large share of that goes into melting.

Melting as a heat sink needs no rebuilding for a chemist: it is the enthalpy of fusion, and the paper uses values that rise from a fraction of a megajoule per kilogram at low pressure to about nine megajoules per kilogram at the pressure of Earth's core-mantle boundary.

**Analogy:** Think of a power surge hitting a server. Some of the energy goes straight to ground through the chassis (the iron sinking to the center), and some goes into a phase-change thermal buffer that absorbs heat at constant temperature (latent heat of melting). Only what is left raises the temperature of the components you can measure.

**Breaks when:** you treat the ground path as a loss. The iron's energy is not gone; it stays stored deep in the planet as heat. The paper simply argues that it is stored where it cannot reach the atmosphere quickly.

### What is a magma ocean, and when does it stop pushing heat outward?

After a giant impact, much of the mantle is molten: a global magma ocean. A magma ocean behaves like a liquid and convects vigorously, carrying heat to the surface quickly. Once cooling brings the melt fraction down to about 40 percent, crystals lock together and the mixture flows like a solid. The temperature at which this happens near the surface, about 1,610 K, is called the **rheological transition**. Below it, heat from deep inside reaches the surface only slowly, so the surface can no longer drive escape. The paper uses this transition twice: as the end of the "hot phase" after an impact, and as the assumed surface temperature just before the impact.

**Analogy:** A liquid-cooled loop that suddenly gels. While the coolant flows, it moves heat out fast; once it gels, the hot component is effectively insulated even if it is still very hot inside.

**Breaks when:** you expect the gel point to be sharp. Rock melts over a range of hundreds of degrees, because it is a mixture of minerals; the 40 percent point is a practical boundary, not a phase transition.

### Population synthesis in one paragraph

The paper does not model one planet; it rolls dice for 100 million planets. Each draw sets a rocky mass from an assumed mass distribution, an iron fraction between 10 and 40 percent, a stellar heating level from the observed Kepler sample, a hydrogen envelope fraction that grows with mass, and whether and how hard it was hit late. Each planet is then evolved and its final radius recorded. The histogram of final radii is compared with the observed one. This is a Monte Carlo over input distributions; its outputs are only as constraining as those input distributions are well known.

### Origins & Big Picture: how super-Earths and sub-Neptunes come to be

Close-in planets of a few Earth masses are the most common kind of planet known in the galaxy, yet our solar system has none. Their story starts in the gas-and-dust disk that surrounds a young star for its first few million years. Solid material clumps into bodies the size of the Moon or Mars, often called planetary embryos, which grow by sweeping up pebbles and smaller bodies. Once an embryo reaches about an Earth mass while the disk gas is still around, its gravity pulls in a thin hydrogen and helium envelope. How much gas it gathers depends steeply on its mass: heavier embryos capture gas faster, which is why the paper scales the envelope fraction with the square of the mass.

While the disk is present, gas also pushes the growing planets inward toward the star, and gravitational nudges between neighbors tend to lock them into orderly orbital patterns called resonant chains, where orbital periods form simple ratios. These chains are stable only while the disk gas damps their motions. When the star's radiation clears the disk, after a few million years, many chains go unstable. Orbits cross, and planets collide: the late giant-impact phase. N-body simulations of this phase, which the paper relies on, suggest that something like half to three-quarters of planets suffer at least one such collision after the gas is gone. The Moon-forming impact on the early Earth is our local example of the same kind of event, though Earth formed farther out and with far less gas.

The timing matters. An impact while the disk is still present can strip gas, but the planet can recapture gas from the disk afterward. An impact after the disk is gone is final: whatever envelope the planet loses then is never replaced. That is why the paper focuses on the single largest post-disk impact.

After the impacts, the long phase of atmospheric escape begins. Close to the star, a planet's equilibrium temperature, set by starlight, can be several hundred to two thousand kelvin. Over hundreds of millions to billions of years, escape winds drain the envelopes of planets below some critical mass, leaving bare rocky super-Earths; heavier planets keep enough hydrogen to remain sub-Neptunes. Photoevaporation (driven by the star's early X-rays and ultraviolet light) and core-powered mass loss (driven by the planet's own heat) are the two leading candidates for this draining, and both successfully reproduce the radius valley in population models. Other pictures exist too: some argue that many sub-Neptunes are water-rich rather than hydrogen-wrapped, or that some planets formed after the gas was mostly gone and never had much hydrogen.

This paper inserts the giant impact between formation and escape and asks which way it pushes. Its answer is that the impact mainly acts as a mass boost delivered at a critical moment: planets near the mass threshold that get hit are pushed above it and keep their envelopes, while planets that are not hit stay below it and lose theirs to steady starlight-driven escape. In this picture, the sub-Neptune population is enriched in planets that grew by collision, and its rocky interiors should, on average, be heavier than those of super-Earths. That is the population-level link between what the paper models (one impact, one planet, one escape calculation) and what telescopes see (two peaks in a radius histogram).

**Central analogy for this paper:** raising the threshold beats heating the bulk.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

*Analyst-built concept diagram (not from the paper):*

```
 draw planet: rocky mass, iron fraction, starlight, H envelope
                         |
              late giant impact?  (~52-74% yes)
               /                         \
             yes                          no

  add impactor mass (all of it)      cold escape at starlight
  impact energy -> ~84% mantle       temperature, 5 billion yrs
  minus melting -> surface temp            |
  (about 5,000 - 30,000 K)                 |
              |                            |
  hot phase: escape timer vs               |
  magma-ocean timer (to 1,610 K)           |
              |                            |
  then cold escape for the rest            |
              \                           /
               final radius: rock only or rock + H
                         |

```

Read it top to bottom: every planet goes through the same cold-escape test at the end; an impact changes the planet's mass first and adds a short hot phase before that test.

### Step 1: Count the mass gain in the same step as the heat

**The failure it prevents:** treating an impact as pure heating, so the post-impact planet is evaluated with a hot surface but without full credit for being heavier.

**What the authors do:** they assume the impactor merges completely, so the planet's mass jumps by the impactor's mass, and they evaluate the atmosphere's escape using the new, larger mass from the moment of impact onward. The impact size is described by one number: the impactor's mass as a fraction of the final planet mass, which ranges up to one half (two equal bodies).

**What it buys:** because escape is exponential in mass, even a 20 percent mass gain moves the sonic point outward by about 20 percent and can change the escape time by orders of magnitude for a planet near the threshold.

### Step 2: Route the impact energy before it reaches the surface

**The failure it prevents:** assuming all impact energy becomes surface heat, which overstates how hot the planet gets.

**What the authors do:** they compute the energy released as the change in the mantle's gravitational binding energy when the impactor is added, using a simple two-layer planet (uniform-density iron core under a uniform-density rocky mantle). They leave out the iron core's share, since it sinks to the center and stays there. They neglect direct heating of the atmosphere by the impactor's passage, arguing from the very high Reynolds number of the flow that viscous drag is negligible. Their estimate of the mantle's share, about 84 percent for an iron fraction of 0.3, falls inside the 50 to 90 percent range found in independent impact simulations.

**What it buys:** a physically motivated, smaller heat input. The energy budget grows steeply with mass, as mass to the power seven-fourths.

### Step 3: Turn the remaining energy into a surface temperature

**The failure it prevents:** treating the planet as a single blob at one temperature, which ignores melting and the fact that pressure changes the melting point with depth.

**What the authors do:** they build a mantle melting model fitted to laboratory and theoretical data (solidus, the 40 percent melt point, and liquidus as functions of pressure), compute temperature profiles down through the mantle including the heat soaked up by melting, and measure how much energy it takes to raise a mantle from a surface temperature of 1,610 K to some higher value. Matching that to the deposited energy gives a closed formula for the post-impact surface temperature. It rises with planet mass and with impactor size: roughly 4,000 K for an Earth-mass planet hit by an impactor one-tenth its final mass, and about 30,000 K for a 10-Earth-mass planet formed by two equal halves.

**What it buys:** a fast formula that can be evaluated for 100 million planets. Notice the scaling: surface heating grows as mass to the power of about 0.58, while gravitational binding per molecule at the surface grows as mass to the power of about 0.75. Heating grows more slowly than binding, which is the quantitative core of "binding beats heating."

### Step 4: Decide the temperature where gas actually escapes

**The failure it prevents:** using the surface temperature as the escape temperature, which would make every hot post-impact planet lose its envelope almost instantly.

**What the authors do:** they compute two candidate sonic-point temperatures and take the larger. One is the temperature an upward-convecting atmosphere would have if convection reached the sonic point from the hot surface. The other is the starlight-set value, slightly below the planet's equilibrium temperature because escaping gas cools as it expands. In most cases, they say, the convective layer gives out well below the sonic point, so the starlight-set value wins. This rule comes from the authors' 2026 *Icarus* paper, which also argued that escape is not limited by the planet's interior energy flow ("energy-limited" escape) but by the sonic-point density. They also exclude X-ray and ultraviolet photoevaporation, arguing it needs a thin, transparent upper layer that does not exist above a freshly impacted, very hot planet.

**What it buys:** this is the decisive step. Once the sonic-point temperature is pinned to starlight, the surface heat drops out of the escape rate, and only the mass gain remains.

### Step 5: Race three timers

**The failure it prevents:** asking "does it lose atmosphere?" without asking "fast enough to matter?"

**What the authors do:** for an impacted planet, they compare the time to empty the envelope at the hot-phase loss rate with the magma-ocean lifetime (scaled from about 10,000 years for Earth's magma ocean, growing with mass). If the envelope survives the hot phase, they switch to the cold, starlight-set loss rate for the remainder of a fixed 5-billion-year planet age. Planets that are not hit get only the cold test. Each test either empties the envelope (final radius = rocky radius) or trims it (final radius from an envelope model that also accounts for outgassed heavier gases such as water and carbon dioxide).

**What it buys:** a cheap two-stage stand-in for a full time-dependent evolution, which the authors say would be too expensive for a population study.

### Step 6: Build the population and compare it with Kepler

**The failure it prevents:** showing that one planet keeps its atmosphere without showing that the population matches what we see.

**What the authors do:** they draw 100 million planets from a broken power-law mass distribution centered on a few Earth masses, give each a hydrogen envelope whose fraction grows with the square of its mass (with the normalization chosen so that 7 to 10 Earth-mass planets land at the observed 2.4-Earth-radius peak), assign a post-disk impact with probability between 52 and 74 percent, and draw impact sizes from three different falling distributions plus a uniform control. They then compare the final-radius histogram with the Kepler (California-Kepler Survey) occurrence data.

**What it buys:** a direct comparison with the observed valley, and a way to test how much the result depends on impact assumptions.

### Claim Check

Checks were run in Python (`claim_check.py`, analyst-written) from the paper's own equations and stated parameters; the authors' Zenodo code was not run.

**Mantle share of impact energy — Reproduced.** Evaluating the paper's two-layer energy expressions gives a mantle share of 0.85 at an iron fraction of 0.3, falling from 0.97 to 0.76 across the 10 to 40 percent range, matching the paper's 0.84. This is the first link in "heat goes elsewhere," and it holds as stated.

**Post-impact surface temperature formula — Reproduced.** The formula's leading constant follows from Earth's mass, radius, and the fitted energy constant (8,489 versus the quoted 8,500). It gives about 12,700 K for a 5-Earth-mass planet hit by an impactor one-fifth its final mass, and about 31,800 K at the 10-Earth-mass, equal-halves extreme, consistent with the temperatures the paper reports.

**No-impact mass threshold — Partly reproduced.** Rebuilding the cold escape test (the two displayed equations, the envelope and molecular-weight rules, the observed spread of stellar heating, a 5-billion-year age) gives a 50 percent loss point near 5 Earth masses, against roughly 4 to 4.5 in the paper's retention map; the shape (a steep cliff) matches. The paper does not state the heat-capacity ratio of the gas, so I assumed 1.4, which plausibly accounts for the offset. The threshold is real and mass-driven as claimed.

**Sensitivity check — the coupled regime.** The conclusion rests on step 4: the surface heat must not convect up to the sonic point. A crude test (mine, not the paper's) treats the hot atmosphere as an ideal gas cooling along an adiabat from the post-impact surface temperature. It puts the coupling edge at impactors of about 35 to 45 percent of the final mass for 3 to 10 Earth-mass planets; past that edge, the toy envelope would be lost in years, well inside a magma-ocean lifetime of hundreds of thousands of years. Such large impacts are 5 to 14 percent of impacts under two of the paper's size laws. The paper's own regime test evidently keeps these planets decoupled (its retention map shows near-total retention there), but that test is defined in the earlier *Icarus* paper and code, which I did not read. The conclusion survives unless real post-impact atmospheres stay convective to the sonic point for the biggest collisions; that would weaken, not reverse, the population result, since those impacts are rare.

**Robustness test — alternative explanation for the fit.** The model's own controls answer whether impact physics is needed for the valley. With no impacts, a valley still forms near 1.75 Earth radii; impacts mainly adjust the relative heights of the two peaks. With the hot phase stretched to 5 billion years, the result barely moves, which shows that impact heating has almost no leverage in this model. Taken together, a late impact here behaves as a mass boost. The same population fit could likely be obtained with no impacts by shifting the starting mass distribution upward by the average mass the impacts add (my inference, not tested). The fit is shown visually only; no goodness-of-fit statistic is reported, so the 52 to 74 percent cases cannot be ranked against each other or against the no-impact control.

**Internal consistency — one significant finding.** The escape-rate fit is described in the Methods as built for hot conditions, with heat crossing the lower atmosphere by conduction, and the paper states that cooler atmospheres would need a revised fit. Yet the same fit is used for the cold, starlight-set phase that lasts billions of years, and that cold phase is what sets the mass threshold in the check above. The threshold that produces the valley therefore comes from a fit applied outside the regime its authors validated it for. This does not change the direction of the impact effect (mass still raises the barrier exponentially), but it does make the *location* of the threshold, and so the population match, less secure than the figures suggest. Minor: one exponent in the 40-percent-melt formula repeats its scale constant (32.089), likely a transcription slip.

### Assumption Audit

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes "impacts help planets retain their atmospheres" means an impacted planet keeps its gas better than an unimpacted planet of the same final mass. The paper actually compares each impacted planet with *its own lighter, pre-impact self*. At equal final mass, the impacted planet is hotter and so slightly worse off; the benefit comes entirely from the added mass.

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes the model needs giant impacts to reproduce the radius valley. The paper's own no-impact control still makes a valley in the right place; impacts tune the ratio between the two peaks, while the valley itself comes from the exponential mass threshold plus an envelope fraction that rises with mass and is normalized to the observed upper peak.

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes the paper demonstrates that impact heat cannot reach the escaping gas. The paper actually inherits that from its earlier work: the sonic-point temperature is pinned to starlight unless convection reaches the sonic point, and this paper's tests (including the 5-billion-year magma ocean) are consistent with that rule but do not test it independently.

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes the Reynolds-number argument shows the impactor barely heats the atmosphere. The argument actually shows only that viscous drag is negligible; a body moving several times faster than sound through gas heats it mainly through shock waves, which do not depend on viscosity. The paper separately cites shock-driven losses of 10 to 20 percent of the envelope, and these losses are not included in the population model.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

The cleverest move is bookkeeping. Earlier impact-loss calculations asked "how much does the impact heat the planet?" and then evaluated escape. This paper writes the heat and the mass gain into the same ledger at the same moment, and then routes the heat through the planet's interior before letting any of it near the atmosphere. Each piece (iron sinking, latent heat, mass-dependent binding) exists in the literature; combining them in one self-consistent energy budget, with an explicit scaling showing heating grows more slowly with mass than binding, is new to the field.

The second insight is reframing what an impact *is* in population terms. Instead of a competing loss channel alongside photoevaporation or core-powered loss, the impact becomes a mechanism that moves planets across the mass threshold those channels create. This links the giant-impact phase (from N-body simulations of chain instability) to the ratio of super-Earths to sub-Neptunes, which is a cleaner statement than "impacts strip atmospheres."

What is new to the reader but not to the field: the sonic-point decoupling and the rejection of energy-limited escape come from the authors' 2026 *Icarus* paper, and the radius valley as a mass threshold is standard.

### Predictive Content Check

**Falsifiable handle:** The paper's stated prediction is that sub-Neptunes have heavier rocky interiors than super-Earths, testable with radial-velocity masses (PLATO and others) and atmospheric spectra (JWST, Ariel). That prediction can fail, but it is weakly discriminating: photoevaporation and core-powered mass loss also predict heavier cores for planets that keep their envelopes at a given stellar heating. The mechanism-specific handle, which the paper does not state, is that the *ratio* of sub-Neptunes to super-Earths should track how often late instabilities occurred. Systems with signs of past orbital upheaval (fewer planets, more tilted or eccentric orbits, broken resonances) should host relatively more sub-Neptunes at a given mass and irradiation than calm, resonant systems. If no such trend exists, the "impacts as a ladder" picture loses its distinctive content.

**Formalism load:** This fires, because the Methods carry a heavy apparatus (pressure-dependent melting curves, adiabats with latent heat, polytrope interiors) for a population result. The apparatus is not decorative in principle: it is what establishes that heating is too weak to matter. In practice, though, the population outcome is driven by the mass gain and the cold-phase escape fit, as the 5-billion-year magma-ocean test shows; the mantle thermodynamics could be replaced by "heat is negligible" with little change to the headline histogram. The math justifies a null on heating rather than generating a prediction.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**L1. The escape law is used outside the conditions it was fitted for.** The fit assumes hot, conduction-dominated lower atmospheres; the paper says cooler atmospheres need a revised fit, yet the multi-billion-year cold phase that sets the mass threshold uses it unchanged. **(A) Consensus** — the paper itself states the restriction, and nothing about it is in dispute; only its size is unknown. **(paper §4.1)**

**L2. Whether the largest impacts couple the surface to the sonic point.** The key step pins the escape temperature to starlight; a crude adiabat estimate suggests impactors above about a third to a half of the final mass could push convection to the sonic point, where loss would be fast. **(C) Speculative** — this comes from my recomputation with an ideal-gas adiabat that ignores radiation and hydrogen dissociation, while the paper's actual regime test (in its earlier paper and code) was not read. **(analyst inference)**

**L3. Impacts act as a mass boost, and the population fit is degenerate with tuned inputs.** The no-impact control still makes a valley, the envelope normalization is tuned to the upper peak, the mass distribution is adopted without refinement, and the comparison to data is visual with no fit statistic. **(B) Contested** — the authors present the controls as showing impacts regulate peak ratios, which is a fair reading, while a skeptic can read the same panels as showing the valley does not need impacts. **(paper §2.2, §4.3; analyst inference)**

**L4. Photoevaporation is left out entirely.** It is excluded for hot impacted planets for a stated physical reason, but also omitted for unimpacted planets, where the paper agrees it would operate. **(B) Contested** — the paper argues adding it would only strengthen its conclusion (more loss for unimpacted planets), which is plausible, but it would also shift the threshold and so the population match. **(paper §4.1; broader literature)**

**L5. Collision mechanics are idealized.** The model assumes complete merging, neglects shock-driven envelope loss (which the paper itself puts at 10 to 20 percent) and direct shock heating of the gas, and considers only the single largest late impact. **(B) Contested** — impact simulation groups disagree on how much atmosphere a giant impact removes directly, and the paper argues these effects are secondary to binding. **(paper §1, §4.4, §4.8; broader literature)**

**L6. Hydrogen dissolving into the magma ocean is not modeled.** A hot magma ocean can absorb large amounts of hydrogen and release it later, changing both how much envelope exists and how it evolves; the paper mentions this solubility but sets it aside. **(B) Contested** — recent work (including the same-week Zlimen et al. preprint on silicate-hydrogen mixing) argues it matters for impacts and the valley, but how much is open. **(paper §4.1; broader literature)**

**L7. Simplified timeline and starting state.** Pre-impact surfaces are set at the 1,610 K transition, the escape rate is frozen at its value at impact, all planets are 5 billion years old, and the hot-to-cold switch is a single step. **(A) Consensus** — the paper describes each as an idealization chosen for tractability, and the authors call for a coupled interior-envelope model. **(paper §4.1, §4.8)**

Follow-up work for the next one to two years: a coupled interior-envelope evolution code run through a late impact, a validated cold-phase escape law, a population fit with a likelihood rather than visual agreement, and an observational test of whether sub-Neptune fractions track signs of past dynamical instability.

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

The paper asks a simple question: when two young planets collide after the gas disk has cleared, does the merged planet lose its hydrogen envelope or keep it? The established intuition says lose it, because a collision makes the planet's interior extremely hot, and a hot interior can power an escaping wind. The authors argue that intuition misses half of the ledger. A collision also makes the planet heavier, and the rate at which gas escapes depends exponentially on how deep the planet's gravity well is relative to the gas temperature at the sonic point, the place where the outgoing wind becomes supersonic.

They then argue that the heat does less than it seems. The impactor's iron core sinks to the planet's center and keeps its energy there. About 85 percent of the energy reaches the rocky mantle, and much of that is spent melting rock rather than heating it. What remains still makes the surface tremendously hot, from several thousand to about thirty thousand kelvin. But in their escape model, the temperature that matters is at the sonic point, tens of planet radii out, and there it is set by starlight unless the surface is so hot that convection reaches all the way up. Under that rule, the post-impact heat mostly fails to reach the escaping gas, while the extra mass raises the barrier for every molecule.

Running 100 million synthetic planets through this model reproduces the observed radius valley, with peaks near 1.3 and 2.4 Earth radii and the gap near 1.75. Planets that are hit late are pushed upward in mass and tend to keep their envelopes, becoming sub-Neptunes; planets that are not hit stay lighter and, over billions of years, lose their envelopes to starlight-driven escape, becoming super-Earths. The fraction of planets hit adjusts the ratio of the two populations, and the authors predict that sub-Neptunes should have heavier rocky interiors on average.

Why frame it this way: the paper's real content is a sign flip on the role of giant impacts, and that flip comes from two ingredients working together, the mass gain and the decoupling of the escape temperature from the surface. The checks support the first ingredient fully: the energy routing and temperature formulas reproduce from the paper's own inputs, and the escape threshold is a genuine exponential cliff in mass. The second ingredient is inherited from the authors' earlier paper; this paper is consistent with it but does not test it, and a crude estimate suggests the largest impacts sit closer to the coupling edge than the text implies. The model's own controls also show that, within it, impacts behave almost purely as mass additions: removing impacts still leaves a valley, and stretching the hot phase to billions of years changes almost nothing. The fair takeaway is that late giant impacts are unlikely to be the envelope strippers some earlier models assumed, and that they plausibly help set how many sub-Neptunes exist. Whether they are needed to explain the valley is not shown, and the exact threshold rests on an escape law applied outside its stated range.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the hot-phase regime test, meaning how the authors decide whether a freshly impacted planet's convective layer reaches the sonic point. That rule lives in their 2026 *Icarus* paper and in code I did not read, and my ideal-gas toy, which suggested the largest impacts could cross into the coupled regime, ignores radiation, hydrogen dissociation, and the real structure of a hot envelope, any of which could move that edge in either direction.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. A giant collision both heats a planet and makes it heavier, and for holding onto hydrogen the extra weight wins: escape depends exponentially on gravity, while the heat mostly ends up deep inside the planet or spent melting rock.

2. In this model, a late impact works like a ladder over a mass threshold: planets that climb it by collision stay puffy sub-Neptunes, and planets that don't get hit stay light and slowly lose their hydrogen to starlight.

3. The reversal is real within the model, but it rests on the authors' earlier rule that starlight, not surface heat, sets the temperature where gas escapes; the paper's own controls show the radius valley appears even without impacts, so impacts tune the balance of the two planet families rather than create the gap.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Small exoplanets come in two sizes: rocky super-Earths about 1.3 times Earth's radius and sub-Neptunes about 2.4 times Earth's radius, with a gap between. The usual explanation is that sub-Neptunes still wear a thin hydrogen envelope from birth while super-Earths lost theirs. Some models proposed that late giant impacts, collisions after the star's gas disk clears, heat planets enough to blow those envelopes away.

Modirrousta-Galian and Korenaga argue the opposite. A collision adds mass as well as heat, and the rate at which hydrogen escapes falls exponentially as a planet gets heavier. Meanwhile, much of the impact's energy never reaches the escaping gas: the impactor's iron sinks to the center, melting rock soaks up heat, and, in their escape model, the temperature where gas actually leaves is set by starlight unless the surface is extraordinarily hot. The net effect is that an impacted planet holds its hydrogen better than it did before the impact.

Running 100 million synthetic planets reproduces the observed two-peak pattern of planet sizes. Planets hit late are pushed above a mass threshold and stay sub-Neptunes; planets not hit stay lighter and lose their envelopes over billions of years. The authors predict that sub-Neptunes should have heavier rocky interiors than super-Earths.

Checks of the paper's energy and temperature formulas reproduce its numbers. Two caveats matter. First, the model's own no-impact run still produces the size gap, so impacts tune how many planets fall on each side rather than creating the gap. Second, the escape law is applied to cool, billion-year conditions that the paper itself says it was not built for, and the key rule decoupling surface heat from escape comes from the authors' earlier work rather than being tested here. The result is a strong argument that late giant impacts are not envelope strippers, and a plausible but not yet unique account of why sub-Neptunes are so common.
