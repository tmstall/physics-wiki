# 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Young's Double Slit, Shrunk to Two Atoms**

Point a focused electron beam at two silicon atoms sitting 1.36 Å apart inside a crystal, and the crystal itself turns those two atomic columns into a working double slit — and because the two atoms don't jiggle independently but *together*, the interference fringes survive all the way up to 900 K, turning a two-century-old optics demonstration into a working probe of how stiff a single chemical bond actually is.

# 2. Big-Picture Context

Young's double-slit experiment is the archetypal demonstration that light (and, as physics learned over the following two centuries, matter) behaves as a wave: two coherent sources, one interference pattern, encoding both wave and object. Over the last hundred years, the same logic has been pushed to progressively smaller and stranger interferometers — single electrons (Tonomura et al., 1989), neutrons, whole atoms, and eventually molecules as large as buckyballs and even ~25,000-Da assemblies (Fein et al., 2019). Each step down in scale (and up in mass) has been treated as a fresh test of how far quantum superposition survives, and each has required building an increasingly exotic, purpose-built interferometer — slits machined at nanometre precision, atom chips, molecular beam sources.

This paper takes a different tack: instead of building a smaller interferometer, it recognizes that a *crystal already has one built in*. Two neighbouring atomic columns in a crystal lattice, separated by a fixed, sub-nanometre distance, can act as the two slits — if you can get a coherent electron probe to illuminate both at once and read out the resulting diffraction pattern with enough precision. The authors do exactly this inside a silicon crystal using scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) with a pixelated detector, focusing an electron probe onto the midpoint between two adjacent Si atomic columns (the "[110] dumbbell," separated by 1.36 Å) and recording the resulting diffraction pattern at each of ~360 crystallographically equivalent atom pairs. The result: a set of interference fringes exactly matching what two coherent point sources 1.36 Å apart should produce.

What makes this more than "double-slit, but smaller" is the second finding, which is really the point of the paper: real atoms at finite temperature are not stationary — they vibrate. Naively, if the two "slits" are wobbling around independently, their effective separation fluctuates from shot to shot, and averaging over many such fluctuating separations should wash out anything beyond the lowest-order fringe (this is exactly the textbook picture of how thermal motion degrades fine diffraction detail — the physics behind the Debye-Waller factor that every crystallographer already builds into diffraction analysis). The authors show that this naive picture is wrong for how these two particular atoms actually move: because they're chemically bonded to each other, their thermal vibrations are *correlated* rather than independent — when one moves, its neighbour tends to move with it — and that correlation is exactly what's needed to keep the effective slit separation nearly constant from configuration to configuration, preserving high-order interference fringes even at 900 K (three times room temperature). This isn't just a robustness result: because the specific *amount* of correlation needed to explain the observed fringe visibility is calculable, the interference pattern becomes a direct, quantitative readout of how strongly the two atoms are dynamically coupled — which turns out to be a direct probe of the local, direction-dependent stiffness of the Si–Si bond itself.

**Paper Type & Stakes.** This is a methods/instrumentation-and-demonstration Article: a genuinely new measurement capability (atomic-scale interferometry using a crystal's own lattice as the interferometer) validated on a well-understood test material (bulk silicon), with the explicit goal of extending it to materials and structures — defects, interfaces, chemically asymmetric bonds — where existing techniques (which measure phonon dynamics only as an average over macroscopic sample volumes) can't reach. The stakes are less about silicon itself and more about opening a new local, real-space window onto correlated atomic motion and low-energy phonon dynamics relevant to thermal transport.

**Prior Belief Check.** The finding is best described as *confirmatory of theory but genuinely surprising to specialists in electron microscopy* — a distinction worth drawing carefully. The idea that correlated (as opposed to independent) atomic vibrations could in principle preserve some coherent scattering signal is not new theoretical territory; frozen-phonon and phonon-correlation formalisms used in electron diffraction have existed for decades (the paper's own citations to Loane, Xu & Silcox 1991 and Van Dyck 2009 on inelastic/frozen-phonon scattering theory go back over 30 years). What's genuinely surprising to specialists is where this shows up: thermal diffuse scattering (TDS) — the diffuse, seemingly structureless background produced by inelastic electron-phonon scattering — has long been treated in electron microscopy as essentially decoherent noise, useful mainly for extracting an *averaged* Debye-Waller factor, not as a channel that could carry fine coherent structure. This paper's explicit separation of elastic and inelastic scattering (via a Quantum Excitation of Phonons, or QEP, calculation) shows that the *inelastic* thermal diffuse background itself still carries coherent double-slit fringes — a genuinely field-relevant reframing of what TDS is and does, even though it doesn't contradict the underlying quantum scattering theory.

**Replication & Convergence Note.** This is a single-laboratory result (University of Tokyo-led, with theoretical/simulation input from a University of Vienna collaborator) obtained on one specific, high-end instrument (a JEOL ARM300CF STEM with a cold field-emission gun, a DELTA aberration corrector, and a DECTRIS ARINA pixelated detector) — a combination of hardware that is not widely available, meaning independent replication by an outside group is a real practical barrier rather than a matter of re-running an easy experiment. That said, the paper builds in *strong internal convergence*: three independently constructed models — a simplified nearest-neighbour harmonic chain, a full first-principles (machine-learned interatomic potential) phonon calculation, and the experimentally extracted correlation coefficients — all agree closely with each other across three separate temperatures (300 K, 500 K, 900 K). That's meaningful cross-validation of the *method*, but it is convergence between different theoretical treatments checked against one lab's data, not independent experimental replication by a second group.

# 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Double-slit interference, briefly.** When a coherent wave (light, electrons, atoms) passes through two narrow slits, each slit acts as a new point source; the two resulting waves overlap and interfere, producing alternating bright and dark fringes on a screen wherever the two paths arrive in or out of step. The fringe spacing depends on the wavelength and the slit separation — measure the fringes, and (knowing the wavelength) you can read off the separation, or vice versa.

**Analogy:** Two speakers playing the identical audio signal, placed a fixed distance apart, produce loud and quiet spots as you walk past them — the loud spots are where sound from both speakers arrives in phase (crests aligning), quiet spots where it arrives exactly out of phase and cancels. The double slit is the same phenomenon with two coherent point emitters instead of two speakers.

**Breaks when:** you push the analogy to ask what happens if the speakers themselves are shaking randomly. Two loudspeakers bolted to a wall don't move; this experiment's "slits" are real atoms that vibrate continuously, and unlike engineered emitters, their separation is never perfectly fixed from moment to moment — the entire point of the paper is what happens to the fringes once you take that vibration seriously.

**STEM, CBED and 4D-STEM.** A scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM) focuses an electron beam to a very fine spot (here, ~1.1 Å across — smaller than the spacing between the two atoms being studied) and rasters it across a thin sample. At each probe position, rather than just measuring a single transmitted intensity, this experiment records the *entire* diffraction pattern the electrons produce after passing through the sample — a technique called convergent-beam electron diffraction (CBED). Collecting a full CBED pattern at every scan position (rather than one number per position) is what's meant by "4D-STEM": two dimensions of probe position, two more of the resulting diffraction pattern, all recorded by a fast pixelated detector.

**Analogy:** A conventional STEM image is like a single-pixel light sensor scanned across a scene, one intensity value per position. 4D-STEM is like replacing that single-pixel sensor with a full camera at every scan position — you get vastly more information (an entire diffraction "fingerprint") at the cost of dramatically more data to process.

**Breaks when:** you push this toward thinking of the diffraction pattern as a simple photograph. A CBED pattern doesn't record a real-space image at all — it's built entirely from how the electron wave's momentum redistributes after interacting with the crystal's potential, more like a full frequency-domain spectrum than a photo, and reading physical information (atomic positions, bond stiffness) back out of it requires the kind of inverse modelling this paper does at length, not visual inspection.

**Electron channelling — the crystal as its own waveguide.** As the electron beam travels through the crystal, the positively charged atomic nuclei attract the (negative) electron wave, pulling the beam's intensity preferentially toward the atomic columns it's near. This is "channelling": the crystal's own electrostatic potential acts like a built-in guiding structure that concentrates the electron wave onto atomic columns as it propagates deeper into the sample — which is precisely how a probe aimed at the midpoint between two atoms ends up delocalized over *both* columns, effectively splitting into two coherent point sources without any physical slit ever being cut.

**Analogy:** This is the same physical idea as an optical fibre guiding light along its core by continuously bending the light back toward the higher-refractive-index centre — except here there are *two* attractive cores side by side, so the beam splits its intensity between both rather than being confined to just one.

**Breaks when:** you push this to ask about stability. An optical fibre's guiding structure (the refractive-index profile) is fixed and engineered; here, the "guiding structure" is made of the very atoms whose vibration is the subject of the experiment — the waveguide and the thing perturbing the waveguide are the same physical object, which is exactly why the fringes are sensitive to how those atoms move.

**Thermal diffuse scattering, phonons, and the Debye-Waller factor.** Atoms in a crystal are never perfectly still; even at low temperature, quantum zero-point motion keeps them vibrating, and thermal energy adds more motion as temperature rises. These vibrations come in collective, wave-like patterns called phonons — a phonon isn't one atom shaking, it's a coordinated ripple of displacement moving through the whole lattice, and any given phonon mode is defined by its wavelength/direction (its wavevector, q) and its frequency (which branch — acoustic or optical — it belongs to). When an electron (or X-ray, or neutron) beam scatters off a vibrating crystal, most of it still scatters elastically (into the sharp Bragg diffraction spots, just weakened by an averaged-out "blurring" factor called the Debye-Waller factor), but a smaller fraction scatters *inelastically* off the phonons themselves, producing a smooth, low-intensity diffuse background between the sharp spots — the thermal diffuse scattering this paper is centrally concerned with.

**The frozen-phonon method.** Because directly solving the full quantum electron-phonon scattering problem is hard, a common practical trick (used throughout this paper) is to treat each phonon-induced atomic displacement as if it were momentarily frozen in place, calculate the ordinary (static-lattice) diffraction pattern for that one snapshot, and then average the results over many randomly sampled snapshots — each snapshot drawn from the correct statistical distribution of displacements. Do this with snapshots where every atom moves *independently* (the "Einstein model," used here as the naive baseline) versus snapshots where neighbouring atoms' displacements are drawn from a *correlated* distribution (informed by the real phonon modes) and you get two different predictions for what the diffraction pattern should look like — this comparison is the paper's central diagnostic tool.

**Central analogy for this paper:** Correlated bond vibrations as common-mode noise rejection.

# 4. Core Technical Explanation

**The setup.** A high-purity silicon crystal is oriented along its [110] zone axis, in which pairs of Si atoms form "dumbbells" — two atomic columns just 1.36 Å apart, projected along the beam direction. A ~1.1 Å (FWHM) focused electron probe is positioned exactly at the midpoint between one such pair, and the full CBED pattern is recorded via 4D-STEM. Because channelling delocalizes the beam over both columns (as described above), this specific geometry — probe centred *between* two atoms, not on one — creates the two-coherent-source condition needed for double-slit interference; positioning the probe directly on a single atomic column, by contrast, produces no such fringes (confirmed directly, Extended Data Fig. 1). A simplified two-circular-aperture Fraunhofer-diffraction calculation, using apertures sized to match the beam's point-spread function and separated by exactly 1.36 Å, reproduces the fringe spacing seen in the real data almost exactly — direct confirmation that the observed pattern really is geometric double-slit interference from the atom pair, not some unrelated diffraction artefact.

**The two competing models of atomic motion.** The authors then ask what happens to this interference at finite temperature, using frozen-phonon simulations under two different assumptions about how the two atoms move: (1) the Einstein model, in which each atom's thermal displacement is drawn independently, with an amplitude fixed by the experimentally known Si mean-squared displacement at each temperature; and (2) a fully correlated model, in which the displacements of both atoms are drawn jointly from the actual phonon-derived statistics of the crystal (i.e., respecting which normal modes move the two atoms together versus apart). The Einstein model predicts that all but the lowest-order fringe should vanish — thermal motion should wash out the fine structure. The correlated model instead reproduces the experimentally observed fringe pattern in detail, including higher-order maxima the Einstein model can't explain. This comparison is repeated and holds up as temperature is pushed from 300 K to 900 K: the correlated model keeps matching; the Einstein model's mismatch gets worse.

**Separating elastic from inelastic scattering.** To make sure this isn't some artefact of how the two models were compared, the authors use a Quantum Excitation of Phonons (QEP) calculation, which can split the total simulated scattering into its elastic (coherent, Bragg-like) and inelastic (thermal diffuse) pieces separately. The result is the paper's most conceptually pointed finding: under the correlated model, coherent interference fringes are visible *inside the thermal diffuse scattering itself* — the very component of the signal that's conventionally treated as decoherent background. Under the Einstein (independent-motion) model, the thermal diffuse component is smooth and featureless, exactly as textbook intuition expects.

**From fringe visibility to a number: the correlation coefficient.** To quantify how correlated the two atoms' motion actually is, the authors define, for each direction (x = along the bond/intercolumn axis, y = perpendicular to it, both measured in the plane perpendicular to the beam), a correlation coefficient ρ_α between the two atoms' displacements — the normalized covariance of their thermal motion (ρ_α = +1 means perfectly in-phase motion; 0 means fully independent; negative values would mean anti-correlated/out-of-phase motion). By systematically varying (ρ_x, ρ_y) in simulation and comparing against the experimental CBED pattern (specifically the high-angle thermal-diffuse region, deliberately masking out the central bright-field disc and low-angle Bragg spots to avoid contamination from residual lens aberrations or sample mistilt), they extract a best-fit pair of correlation coefficients at each temperature via mean-squared-error minimization. The headline numbers, at 300 K: ρ_x = 0.362 ± 0.005, ρ_y = 0.184 ± 0.005 (95% confidence interval) — closely matching the values predicted independently from a full ab initio (machine-learned-potential-based) phonon calculation, ρ_x = 0.39, ρ_y = 0.24. Both coefficients rise modestly with temperature (to 0.419 ± 0.009 and 0.288 ± 0.010, respectively, at 900 K) but stay in roughly the same regime — the *shape* of the correlation (bond-parallel stronger than transverse) is robust across the whole 300–900 K range, even though the raw vibration amplitude itself grows substantially with temperature.

**Worked equation — from correlation coefficient to bond stiffness.** To turn a measured correlation coefficient into a physically meaningful bond property, the authors build a minimal model: treat the two atoms as if they sit at the ends of a simple 1D harmonic chain, each atom coupled to its actual bonded neighbour by a spring of stiffness *k_α* (the real interatomic bond) and to the rest of the surrounding crystal lattice by an effective "on-site" spring of stiffness *K_α* (a stand-in for everything else the atom is embedded in). Solving this linear-chain model analytically under thermal equilibrium gives an exact relationship between the correlation coefficient and the ratio of these two stiffnesses, κ_α ≡ k_α/K_α:

$$\rho_\alpha=\frac{2\kappa_\alpha}{2\kappa_\alpha+1+\sqrt{4\kappa_\alpha+1}},\qquad\text{with the exact inverse}\qquad \kappa_\alpha=\frac{\rho_\alpha}{(1-\rho_\alpha)^2}.$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$\rho_\alpha$ : the measured correlation coefficient between the two atoms' displacements along direction $\alpha$ (dimensionless, $-1$ to $+1$) · $\kappa_\alpha$ : the dimensionless stiffness ratio $k_\alpha/K_\alpha$ — how stiff the bond between the two atoms is *relative to* how stiff each atom's coupling to the surrounding bulk crystal is · $k_\alpha$ : the interatomic (bond) spring constant along direction $\alpha$ · $K_\alpha$ : the effective on-site (bulk-lattice) spring constant along direction $\alpha$.

**What this actually means:** This is a closed-form dictionary translating "how correlated do two atoms move" directly into "how mechanically stiff is the bond between them, relative to how firmly each atom is held by the rest of the crystal." Plugging in the paper's own extracted 300 K values: along the bond-parallel (x) direction, $\kappa_x = 0.39/(1-0.39)^2 = 0.39/0.372 \approx 1.05$; along the perpendicular (y) direction, $\kappa_y = 0.24/(1-0.24)^2 = 0.24/0.578 \approx 0.42$. The ratio $\kappa_x/\kappa_y \approx 2.5$ is the paper's quantitative version of "the Si–Si bond resists being stretched or compressed about two and a half times more strongly than it resists being sheared sideways" — exactly the kind of anisotropic bond-stiffness statement a chemist would recognize from thinking about $\sigma$ (bond-stretching) versus $\pi$-like (bending/shearing) force constants, now extracted directly from an interference pattern rather than inferred from bulk elastic constants or vibrational spectroscopy averaged over the whole crystal.

**Which phonon modes actually matter.** Finally, the authors decompose the "mean squared relative displacement" (MSRD) — the specific quantity that governs how much fringe visibility is lost — mode by mode across the full phonon dispersion relation. Two things have to be true simultaneously for a given phonon mode to meaningfully degrade the fringes: it has to be thermally well-populated (low-frequency modes are easier to excite than high-frequency ones), *and* it has to actually move the two atoms relative to each other rather than together. Long-wavelength acoustic modes near the Brillouin-zone centre (Γ point) satisfy the first condition strongly — they're thermally very active — but fail the second: by definition, a long-wavelength acoustic wave moves large groups of neighbouring atoms almost exactly in phase, so it contributes almost no *relative* displacement between this specific atom pair. Zone-boundary transverse acoustic (TA) modes — for example the L-point TA mode at ~3.02 THz and the X-point TA mode at ~3.88 THz — do the opposite: shorter wavelength, genuinely shearing motion between neighbours, and it's these specific modes (not the overall vibrational amplitude) that dominate the fringe-visibility loss.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** the reader might picture "double-slit interferometry" here as two genuinely separate, physically distinct coherent beams recombining, the way a Mach-Zehnder or Michelson interferometer splits and recombines light. What's actually happening is a single coherent electron probe, aimed at the midpoint between two atoms, that gets passively delocalized over both atomic columns by the crystal's own electrostatic potential (channelling) — the "two slits" are an emergent consequence of dynamical electron diffraction inside the crystal, not two externally engineered apertures or beam paths. This is closer to how a single beam of light naturally spreads over two adjacent pinholes in a screen than to an interferometer with a deliberate beam-splitter.

> **Watch:** the reader might assume the extracted correlation coefficient $\rho$ captures the full three-dimensional correlation of the two atoms' vibrations. It doesn't: the analysis is explicitly restricted to the two directions transverse to the electron beam (the projected $x$ and $y$ directions); the paper states that displacements *parallel* to the beam have a negligible first-order effect on the recorded diffraction pattern and are therefore not measured at all. "Correlated vibration" here means correlated *projected, in-plane* motion, not the atoms' full 3D correlated dynamics.

> **Watch:** the reader might assume thermal diffuse scattering is, by definition, an incoherent, structureless signal — this is close to the standard textbook framing, and it's how TDS is usually *used* in electron microscopy (as an average, e.g. to extract a Debye-Waller factor). The paper's central technical move is showing that TDS is only structureless when atomic motions are independent; when they're correlated, genuine coherent interference survives inside the inelastic (phonon-scattered) signal itself, which is a real departure from how TDS is typically treated as a matter of practice, even though nothing here violates the underlying quantum scattering formalism.

# 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Two things stand out as the paper's real contributions, and they're linked but distinct.

First, **the demonstration that a crystal lattice can function as a genuine, working atomic-scale double-slit interferometer**, using nothing but a focused STEM probe and existing (if very high-end) instrumentation — no engineered slits, no beam-splitting optics, just exploiting electron channelling between two naturally occurring, fixed-separation atomic columns. This is "new to the field" in a fairly direct sense: it's the first demonstration of double-slit-style interference operating at true sub-nanometre, single-bond scale inside a solid, extending the decades-long lineage of shrinking interferometers (photons → electrons → atoms → molecules) in a genuinely different direction — down into the solid-state lattice itself rather than toward free-particle beams.

Second, and more consequential for future use, **turning the fringe-visibility loss itself into a quantitative measurement of local bond stiffness**, via the closed-form correlation-coefficient-to-stiffness-ratio relationship above. This reframes something that conventional electron microscopy treats as noise to be averaged away (thermal diffuse scattering) into a signal that directly encodes anisotropic, bond-specific mechanical information — a genuinely different kind of measurement than existing techniques (inelastic neutron or X-ray scattering) provide, since those measure phonon dispersion as an average over macroscopic sample volumes in momentum space, while this method targets one specific, chosen real-space atomic pair.

**Predictive Content Check**

1. **Falsifiable handle (always):** Yes, and it was directly tested, not just proposed. The correlated model makes a sharp, checkable prediction that diverges from the Einstein (independent-motion) model: specifically, that the third-order interference fringe should remain visible even at 900 K, while the Einstein model predicts featureless diffuse scattering at any finite temperature once vibrations are large enough. The authors tested this directly against real 300 K/500 K/900 K data and report the correlated model's prediction holding up quantitatively, while the Einstein model's mismatch worsens — a genuine confirmed prediction, not a relabelling of an already-known result. The correlation coefficients extracted purely from the diffraction pattern (ρ_x ≈ 0.36–0.42, ρ_y ≈ 0.18–0.29 across 300–900 K) were also cross-checked, independently, against values computed from a first-principles phonon calculation that used no fitting to the diffraction data at all — a second, independent falsifiable comparison that succeeded.

2. **Formalism load (conditional):** This one clearly fires and is judged load-bearing rather than decorative. The correlation-coefficient-to-stiffness-ratio equation isn't dressing — it's the entire bridge between "a measured number describing fringe visibility" and "a physically interpretable bond property," and without it the fringe-visibility measurement would have no route to a quantitative mechanical interpretation at all. The paper explicitly flags this equation's dual role: it both explains why correlation preserves coherence and provides (in the Supplementary Material, not fully reproduced here) a possible route to measuring absolute local force constants when combined with the measured vibrational amplitude — real, stated further work resting directly on this formalism.

# 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> This result depends on a specific, high-end combination of instrumentation — an aberration-corrected, cold field-emission-gun STEM with a fast pixelated detector capable of recording a full diffraction pattern at every probe position with sufficient speed and sensitivity. Few laboratories worldwide currently have this exact combination, meaning independent replication by an outside group is a genuine practical barrier rather than a routine re-run. **(A) Consensus** — this is a straightforward, uncontroversial read of the Methods section's equipment list (JEOL ARM300CF, DELTA corrector, DECTRIS ARINA detector) and the broader state of the field. **(analyst inference, paper's stated methodology)**

> The quantitative stiffness-ratio extraction relies on a simplified nearest-neighbour, 1D harmonic-chain model that lumps the entire surrounding crystal into a single effective "on-site" spring constant. It's validated by close agreement with a full first-principles phonon calculation for this specific, simple, high-symmetry material (elemental Si), but extending the same simplified picture to more complex, lower-symmetry, or chemically asymmetric bonding environments (which is exactly where the authors say this technique would be most useful — defects, interfaces, grain boundaries) is explicitly named as future work, not something demonstrated in this paper. **(B) Contested** — reasonable specialists could differ on how readily this simplified model generalizes beyond simple elemental dumbbells; the paper itself frames the extension as an open question rather than a settled capability. **(paper, Discussion and outlook section)**

> The extracted correlation coefficients describe only the two spatial directions transverse to the electron beam; motion parallel to the beam is explicitly excluded from the analysis (stated to have a negligible first-order effect on the projected signal). This means the technique currently characterizes a 2D projection of atomic correlation, not the full 3D vibrational coupling between the two atoms. **(A) Consensus** — explicitly and directly stated by the authors themselves. **(paper, Methods/Supplementary Note 11 reference)**

> The entire demonstration is performed on bulk, defect-free silicon — a simple, well-characterized, high-symmetry elemental semiconductor chosen specifically because its phonon properties and force constants are already extremely well known, which is precisely what makes it a good validation target but also means the paper doesn't yet demonstrate the technique's real payoff use case: chemically inequivalent atomic pairs (the authors explicitly name GaAs as a next target) or genuinely unknown, defect-perturbed local environments. **(A) Consensus** — explicitly named by the authors as the natural next step, not a criticism external to the paper's own framing. **(paper, Discussion and outlook)**

# 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

This paper demonstrates that a crystal lattice can serve as its own atomic-scale double-slit interferometer. Using scanning transmission electron microscopy with a fast pixelated detector (4D-STEM), the authors focus an electron probe to sub-angstrom size and aim it at the midpoint between two adjacent silicon atomic columns separated by 1.36 Å. Electron channelling — the crystal's own atomic potential acting as a waveguide — delocalizes the beam over both columns, effectively turning them into two coherent point sources without any physically engineered slit. The resulting diffraction pattern shows genuine double-slit interference fringes, confirmed by comparison with an idealized two-aperture optical model and with full dynamical-diffraction (multislice) simulations of a static, unmoving lattice.

The paper's central result concerns what happens once real atomic thermal motion is included. A naive model in which the two atoms vibrate independently of each other predicts that thermal motion should wash out all but the lowest-order interference fringe — a picture consistent with the standard textbook treatment of how thermal vibration degrades fine diffraction detail (the physics behind the Debye-Waller factor). Instead, the experimentally observed fringes — including higher-order maxima — persist essentially unchanged from 300 K up to 900 K. The authors show this is only reproduced by a model in which the two atoms' thermal displacements are correlated (moving together, at least partially, rather than independently) as dictated by the real phonon modes of silicon. Critically, using a formalism (Quantum Excitation of Phonons) that separates elastic from inelastic scattering, they show that these coherent fringes survive *inside* the thermal diffuse scattering itself — the inelastic, phonon-mediated component of the signal usually treated in electron microscopy as an incoherent, structureless background.

Because the amount of correlation needed to explain the observed fringe persistence can be extracted quantitatively (as a correlation coefficient, separately along the bond-parallel and transverse directions) and because a simple analytic model connects that correlation coefficient directly to a ratio of interatomic spring constants, the fringe pattern becomes a direct, local measurement of anisotropic bond stiffness: the Si–Si bond turns out to be roughly 2.5 times stiffer along its own axis than transverse to it, consistent with (and now directly measured via) the physical intuition that bonds resist stretching more than bending. A mode-by-mode decomposition further identifies exactly which phonon modes are responsible for the small amount of visibility loss that does occur: not the strongly-populated, long-wavelength acoustic modes (which move neighbouring atoms nearly in phase and so barely perturb the pair's relative separation), but specific shorter-wavelength, zone-boundary transverse acoustic modes that genuinely shear the two atoms apart.

The interpretive framing chosen here treats this paper primarily as an instrumentation/methods result whose main claim is capability, not a specific new fact about silicon (which is already an extremely well-characterized material, chosen deliberately as a validation target). The reader should take away that the significant claim is "this measurement is now possible and this is how to interpret it," with the more consequential applications — measuring bond stiffness at genuinely unknown or structurally complex sites like defects, interfaces, or chemically asymmetric bonds — explicitly flagged by the authors as future work rather than demonstrated here.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the precise formal route by which the Quantum Excitation of Phonons (QEP) framework rigorously separates a mixed elastic/inelastic quantum scattering density matrix into the "thermal population" and "spectral correlation" factors used in the mode-resolved MSRD analysis is derived in the paper's Supplementary Note 4, which is referenced but not included in the main text or methods pages read here — I'm taking the paper's own stated factorization (visibility loss = thermal population × (1 − spectral correlation)) at face value rather than independently re-deriving or verifying it from first principles, and a specialist in inelastic electron scattering theory could likely identify subtleties in that derivation that this analysis doesn't capture.

# 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **A crystal is a built-in double-slit interferometer, if you know how to look.** By channelling a focused electron probe between two adjacent silicon atoms, the crystal's own electrostatic potential splits the beam into two coherent sources 1.36 Å apart — no engineered slits required — producing genuine double-slit interference fringes in the diffraction pattern.

2. **Correlated jiggling, not stillness, is what keeps the fringes alive.** Contrary to the usual assumption that thermal vibration destroys fine interference detail, these two bonded atoms move together enough that their effective separation stays nearly constant — preserving high-order fringes from 300 K all the way to 900 K, and showing that coherent structure can survive inside what's normally treated as pure incoherent thermal-diffuse background.

3. **The fringe pattern is secretly a bond-stiffness meter.** Because the amount of correlation needed to explain the fringes maps directly (via an exact formula from a simple spring model) onto a ratio of interatomic spring constants, this technique turns an interference measurement into a direct, direction-resolved readout of how stiff a single chemical bond is — about 2.5 times stiffer along the bond axis than sideways to it, for silicon.

# 9. Shorter Summary

Researchers have shown that a crystal can act as its own atomic-scale version of Young's classic double-slit experiment. By focusing an electron beam precisely between two neighbouring silicon atoms just 1.36 Å apart, and using the crystal's own attractive atomic potential to naturally split the beam's intensity between both atoms (a well-known effect called electron channelling), they produced genuine double-slit interference fringes in the diffraction pattern — the same physics as light through two pinholes, shrunk down to the space between two atoms inside a solid.

The more striking part of the result concerns what happens when the atoms' inevitable thermal jiggling is taken into account. If the two atoms vibrated independently, the fine interference fringes should wash out almost completely — this is the standard expectation in electron microscopy, and it holds for atoms that aren't chemically linked. But because these two atoms are directly bonded to each other, their vibrations are correlated: when one moves, the other tends to move with it, keeping their effective spacing nearly constant. The result is that the fine fringes survive essentially unchanged from room temperature all the way up to 900 K (over 600°C) — and they survive specifically within the "thermal diffuse" part of the signal that electron microscopists have long treated as unstructured background noise.

Because the exact amount of correlation needed to explain the surviving fringes can be measured, and a simple mathematical model connects that correlation directly to the stiffness of the atomic bond, this technique turns an interference pattern into a direct measurement of how stiff a specific chemical bond is — and in which direction. For silicon, the bond turns out to be roughly two and a half times stiffer against stretching than against sideways shearing, matching what a first-principles calculation of silicon's vibrational physics predicts, with no fitting involved. This is a demonstration on a very well-understood test material; the authors' stated next step is applying the same idea to materials and structures — chemically different atom pairs, defects, interfaces — where the local atomic bonding is genuinely unknown and existing techniques can't measure it with this kind of spatial precision.
