Left alone with nothing but a list of what their peers currently believe, groups of AI agents spontaneously converge on a shared opinion by following the majority — and this behavior obeys the same single-parameter mathematical law that describes how iron atoms align into a magnet, letting the authors predict, from that one parameter, exactly how large a group of a given AI model can get before it splinters.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Human civilization runs on group coordination without a central conductor — language, norms, and informal social structure let groups of a few hundred people reach shared decisions without anyone dictating the outcome. As LLM-powered "agents" get wired together into multiagent systems (AutoGPT, AutoGen, OpenAI's SWARM, Google's Agent2Agent, and similar frameworks), the same question arises for machines: can groups of AI agents spontaneously coordinate, the way human or animal groups do, or does every useful group behavior have to be hand-engineered in?

Most existing LLM research studies one model in isolation — code generation, medical diagnosis, reasoning benchmarks. Where LLMs have been studied in groups, the focus has mostly been on using them to simulate human social behavior (social-network modeling, opinion-spread simulation) rather than treating the AI agent collective itself as the object of study. This paper does the latter directly: it asks whether real AI agent societies — no simulated humans involved — display spontaneous coordination, using the simplest possible coordination mechanism as a probe: **majority-following**, where an agent adopts whatever opinion the majority of its peers currently holds, with no other information to go on.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** this is a controlled experimental/computational paper — binary opinion-dynamics experiments run across ten different LLM architectures and group sizes from 10 to 1000+ agents — paired with an analytical model (borrowed intact from equilibrium statistical mechanics) that fits the experimental data with a single free parameter per model. The stakes are practical rather than foundational: understanding whether, and how predictably, large AI agent deployments will self-organize matters directly for designing multiagent systems and anticipating both useful emergent behavior and safety failure modes (norm lock-in, converging on bad-but-popular solutions, and so on).

**Prior Belief Check:** part of this result is unsurprising to experts — individual LLMs are already known to show social-conformity-like behaviors (sycophancy, agreement bias) in single-model studies, so *some* majority-following tendency in a group setting is a fairly natural extrapolation, not a shock. What genuinely is more surprising, even to specialists in complex systems and multiagent AI, is how cleanly the behavior collapses onto one universal mathematical form — the same tanh-shaped adoption-probability curve, differing only by a single number, across ten architecturally unrelated models from three different labs (GPT, Claude, Llama). Complex real-world social or biological systems rarely fit an exactly-solvable mean-field physics model this well; getting a clean fit across such a heterogeneous set of "agents" is the part domain experts would flag as a genuinely notable finding, not an expected one.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** this is a single-group result — one paper, one research team (De Marzo, Castellano, Garcia), no independent replication yet. The authors do build in real internal robustness checks (multiple realizations per condition, prompt-variation tests, and a bias-correction procedure for opinion labels — more below), which strengthens confidence that the result isn't an artifact of this exact prompt, but that's not the same as an outside lab reproducing it. Independent confirmation would look like a different group running the same opinion-dynamics protocol — ideally on newer model generations — and confirming both the tanh functional form and the β-versus-group-size scaling hold up. That matters especially here because two of the ten models tested (GPT-4 family, Claude family) are closed, proprietary, and can be silently updated by their vendors at any time — a live external-validity risk this paper doesn't address, since a result measured today on "GPT-4 Turbo" isn't guaranteed to still describe that API endpoint's behavior next year.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Concept 1 — Majority-following as a gossip protocol.** At each timestep, one agent, chosen at random, is shown the full list of every other agent's current opinion (just two options, called "A" and "B" here) and has to pick one for itself — with no memory of past rounds and no reward for being "right," since there's no objectively correct answer. Repeat this one-at-a-time, asynchronous update across the whole group, and you get an opinion-dynamics process nearly identical to the classic "voter model" from social physics.

**Analogy:** this is structurally a distributed gossip / eventual-consistency protocol — one node at a time reads a snapshot of everyone else's state and updates its own local value accordingly, the way a cache-coherence or gossip-based key-value store might have nodes periodically poll peers and adopt whatever value most of them currently hold.

**Breaks when:** you push on the *reason* nodes update. A real gossip protocol enforces convergence by design — that's the whole point of the algorithm. Here, nothing in the prompt tells the agent to prefer the majority; the paper explicitly gives the agent no guidance beyond "here's what everyone else thinks, pick one." Any majority-preference that shows up is emergent, not engineered — which is exactly the open question the paper is chasing, not a designed protocol feature.

**Concept 2 — Fully-connected coupling and the "majority force."** Every agent sees every other agent's opinion directly, with no network structure limiting who talks to whom — this is a **fully-connected** (mean-field) system, not a local-neighbor one. The paper measures, for a group with average opinion $m$ (ranging from $-1$, everyone on option B, to $+1$, everyone on option A), the probability $P(m)$ that the next updating agent adopts option A:

$$P(m) = \frac{1}{2}\left[\tanh(\beta m) + 1\right]$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$P(m)$ : probability the updating agent picks option A, given the current average opinion $m$
$m$ : average group opinion, $(N_+ - N_-)/N$ — the fraction preferring A minus the fraction preferring B
$\beta$ : the **majority force** — how strongly the agent's choice is pulled toward whatever the majority currently favors
$\tanh$ : hyperbolic tangent — an S-shaped curve that saturates at $\pm 1$

**What this actually means:** think of $\beta$ as a load balancer's "aggressiveness" dial. A load balancer routing requests based on which backend currently has more capacity could route purely at random ($\beta = 0$: 50/50 regardless of the imbalance) or could route almost every request to whichever backend is even slightly ahead ($\beta$ large: a small lead snowballs into near-total routing). Here, $\beta$ plays exactly that role for an agent's opinion choice — a small majority lead in $m$ gets amplified into near-certain adoption once $\beta$ is large enough, and barely matters at all when $\beta$ is small.

**Worked numerical micro-example:** take $\beta = 6$ (roughly Claude 3 Haiku's fitted low-$\beta$ regime) and a modest majority, $m = 0.2$ (a 60/40 split toward A). Then $\beta m = 1.2$, $\tanh(1.2) \approx 0.834$, so $P(m) = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.834+1) \approx 0.917$ — even a fairly weak 60/40 majority already gets adopted over 90% of the time. Compare a weaker-force model with $\beta = 1.5$ at the same $m=0.2$: $\tanh(0.3) \approx 0.291$, giving $P(m) \approx 0.646$ — barely better than a coin flip. Same majority, wildly different pull, purely as a function of one number.

**Concept 3 — Curie-Weiss ferromagnetism and the phase transition.** This exact $P(m)$ functional form is not new — it's the transition probability for **Glauber dynamics** applied to the **Curie-Weiss (CW) model**, the simplest textbook model of a ferromagnet: a collection of $N$ atomic spins, each either "up" or "down," each interacting equally with every other spin (no lattice structure, just a fully-connected coupling), tending to align with the majority orientation with a strength controlled by an effective inverse temperature — which is exactly $\beta$. The group's average opinion $m$ here is mathematically identical to a ferromagnet's magnetization.

**Analogy:** heat a bar magnet above its Curie point and its atomic spins point every which way — no net magnetization, $m=0$. Cool it below that point and spins spontaneously align, snapping into a magnetized state with $m$ near $\pm1$. $\beta$ (inverse temperature, essentially) is the dial: below a critical value $\beta_c$, disorder wins; above it, order emerges.

**Breaks when:** pushed on *why* $\beta$ takes the value it does for a given LLM. In a real magnet, $\beta$ traces back to a known microscopic mechanism — quantum exchange interaction strength between electron spins. For an LLM agent, $\beta$ is a purely phenomenological fit to observed behavior; the paper is explicit that it doesn't know what in the model's training (data patterns, system-prompt effects, RLHF tuning toward "helpful and collaborative," or some mix) actually produces a given $\beta$. The physics analogy explains the *math*, not the *mechanism*.

**Central analogy for this paper:** a gossip protocol's consensus threshold behaving like a magnet's Curie point.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**Setup.** Start with $N$ agents (all powered by the same LLM), initial opinions split evenly ($m=0$), no memory, no stakes, no explicit coordination instruction. One randomly chosen agent updates per timestep; the group gets a fixed budget of time (on average, each agent updates about 10 times) to see if it coordinates ($C = |m| = 1$, everyone agrees) or splits into subgroups.

**The universal fit (Fig. 3A).** Fitting $P(m) = \tfrac{1}{2}[\tanh(\beta m)+1]$ to ten different LLMs' actual choice data — GPT-3.5 Turbo through GPT-4 Turbo, Claude 2.0 through 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3 70B — produces a good fit in every case but one (GPT-3.5 Turbo, which is anti-majority for small $m$: it actively tends *away* from a weak majority rather than toward it). Rescaling every model's curve by its own fitted $\beta$ collapses all of them onto the *same* underlying curve — strong evidence that one universal mechanism, differing only in strength, governs all these architecturally unrelated models.

**Three regimes, set by $\beta$ versus $N$.** Solving the self-consistency equation for the equilibrium opinion $m^*$,

$$m^* = \tanh(\beta m^*)$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$m^*$ : the group's equilibrium (long-run average) opinion
$\beta$ : the majority force, same as above

**What this actually means:** this is the same equation that determines whether a ferromagnet spontaneously magnetizes. Below a critical value $\beta_c = 1$, the only solution is $m^*=0$ — no persistent group-level opinion forms, group behavior is memoryless and directionless, like a distributed system that never converges past 50/50. Above $\beta_c$, two new stable solutions appear at $\pm m^*$, growing from zero and approaching $\pm 1$ for large $\beta$ — the group tips toward one option and stays there, self-reinforcingly, the way a distributed cache under high contention snaps to whichever value got a small early lead.

But reaching $\beta_c$ isn't the whole story once $N$ enters the picture. Statistical fluctuations around $m^*$ shrink as $N$ grows (variance $\approx \tfrac{2}{N}e^{-2\beta}$), so *actually reaching* full coordination ($|m|=1$, not just $|m^*|>0$) requires a large, increasingly rare fluctuation once $N$ is big — and the time needed for that fluctuation grows exponentially with $N$ once $\beta$ falls below a second, size-dependent threshold:

$$\beta_t(N) \approx \frac{1}{2}\log N$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$\beta_t(N)$ : the crossover majority-force value, for a given group size $N$, above which coordination is reached quickly (in a time scaling only as $\log N$) and below which it becomes exponentially unlikely
$\log N$ : natural logarithm of the group size

**What this actually means:** think of a gossip protocol's convergence time under a fixed "infection rate" (how aggressively a value spreads once a peer adopts it) as network size grows. If the infection rate scales fast enough relative to $\log(\text{network size})$, convergence stays fast; if it doesn't keep pace, convergence time explodes. $\beta_t(N)$ is exactly that pace-keeping requirement, translated into the ferromagnet's language. This defines a model-specific **critical group size** $N_c$ — solve $\beta(N_c) = \beta_t(N_c)$ — above which that model's groups can no longer coordinate through majority-following alone within a reasonable time.

**Worked numerical micro-example:** for Llama 3 70B, the paper reports $N_c \approx 30$. Check the crossover condition: $\beta_t(30) = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(30) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}(3.40) \approx 1.70$. So Llama's fitted $\beta$ has to fall to around 1.70 by group size 30 for that to be the crossover point — consistent with Fig. 3B showing Llama's $\beta$ declining from roughly 6 at $N=10$ down through the 1–2 range by $N\sim 30$–100. Compare GPT-4o, with reported $N_c \approx 80$: $\beta_t(80) = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(80) \approx 2.19$ — a higher bar, met because GPT-4o's $\beta$ stays elevated longer as $N$ grows.

**Group splitting (Figs. 1 and 5).** Rather than treating a large group as one static experiment, the paper also lets an unstable large group fragment: if it doesn't coordinate within the time budget, it splits into subgroups, which are then run again, recursively — a 150-agent Llama-powered group (Fig. 1) cascades down through several splits before settling into small, stable, fully-coordinated clusters. The binned splitting probability (Fig. 5B) shows almost-zero probability of splitting below $N\approx30$, rising sharply to near-certain splitting above $N\approx60$ — an independent, purely empirical confirmation of the same critical-size transition the CW model predicts analytically.

**The capability correlation (Fig. 4B).** Across models with available MMLU (a broad knowledge-and-reasoning benchmark) scores, higher MMLU correlates with larger $N_c$ (Pearson $r \approx 0.79$, $p=0.013$) and with larger $\beta$ itself ($r \approx 0.79$, $p=0.006$). The most capable models tested — Claude 3.5 Sonnet and GPT-4 Turbo — have $N_c$ exceeding 1000, well past the human Dunbar's-number benchmark of roughly 150–300 for stable informal human group coordination, plotted directly alongside the model data for comparison.

**Assumption Audit**

**Watch:** a reader might assume "majority-following" here means the researchers built or instructed agents to follow the majority. The paper actually gives agents zero guidance toward this behavior — the prompt just lists peers' current opinions and asks for a choice. Majority-following, where it appears, is an *emergent* behavioral pattern the paper measures, not a designed rule; the paper explicitly states it cannot yet explain *why* it emerges (training-data statistics, RLHF toward "helpful and collaborative" responses, and system-prompt effects are all named as live, unresolved candidates).

**Watch:** a reader might take a reported critical group size like "$N_c \approx 1000$ for GPT-4 Turbo and Claude 3.5 Sonnet" as a directly observed threshold. For Claude 3.5 Sonnet specifically, the paper reports this as a **lower bound only** — the analysis never actually measured $\beta$ dropping to $\beta_t$ even at the largest tested size ($N=1000$), because running larger experiments against a metered proprietary API got prohibitively expensive. The stated $N_c$ values for the two most capable models are extrapolations from a fitted trend, not measured collapse points.

**Watch:** a reader might read the Dunbar's-number comparison (Fig. 4B) as a claim that AI agent groups and human groups are cognitively comparable — that a model with $N_c=1000$ is "more socially capable" than a human community. The paper is explicit that this is a **phenomenological** parallel: the underlying mechanism in LLMs could be completely different from whatever produces human group-size limits (cognitive load, theory-of-mind demands, trust-maintenance costs), and the paper's own binary-opinion framework strips out everything — memory, stakes, incentives, richer reasoning — that makes real human and primate group coordination far more complex than what's being measured here.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Two things stand out. First, treating LLM-agent groups as a genuine object of physical/behavioral study — rather than as tools for simulating human social dynamics — and finding that an *exactly solvable* equilibrium statistical-mechanics model (mean-field Ising ferromagnetism under Glauber dynamics) fits real multiagent LLM behavior with a single free parameter, and that this single-parameter fit **generalizes across ten architecturally unrelated models** from three separate labs. That's a stronger, more surprising level of universality than would typically be expected from black-box neural systems trained by entirely different organizations on different data.

Second, the paper closes the loop from a purely behavioral measurement (the fitted majority force $\beta$) back to something independently meaningful: model capability, via a statistically significant correlation with the MMLU benchmark. That turns an abstract physics parameter into a *predictive* handle — in principle, given a model's benchmark score, you could estimate the group size at which its coordination ability collapses, without running the (expensive) full opinion-dynamics experiment.

**Predictive Content Check**

*Falsifiable handle:* the paper's tightest testable claim is the derived scaling relation $\beta_t(N) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\log N$, which predicts a specific critical group size $N_c$ for any model whose $\beta(N)$ trend is measured — concretely, $N_c\approx30$ for Llama 3 70B, $N_c\approx80$ for GPT-4o, $N_c\gtrsim1000$ for GPT-4 Turbo and Claude 3.5 Sonnet. That's directly checkable: run the same protocol on a new model (or a later Claude/GPT version) at a group size straddling its predicted $N_c$, and see whether the splitting probability actually jumps the way Fig. 5B shows for Llama. This is a genuine forward prediction, not a relabeling — though it's worth noting the relation was fit and confirmed on the same experimental runs used to derive it; the strongest version of the test is applying it to a model or group size *not* used in the original fitting, which the paper only partially does (the Claude 3.5 Sonnet case is exactly this kind of held-out extrapolation, and it holds up as a lower bound rather than being falsified).

*Formalism load:* the Curie-Weiss/Glauber-dynamics machinery is doing real work here, not decorating an otherwise qualitative observation. It's what turns "some models coordinate better than others" into a single-number ($\beta$) universal fit across ten heterogeneous models (Fig. 3A), and it's what generates the specific, independently-checked $\log N$ scaling law that matches the empirically observed splitting transition (Fig. 5) and the coordination-time growth patterns (Fig. 4A). Removing the physics mapping would leave only a much weaker, purely descriptive claim ("bigger groups coordinate less often, in some models") with none of the quantitative, cross-model, cross-size predictive content.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

The experimental scenario is deliberately minimal — binary opinions only, no memory across rounds, no objective right answer, no stakes or incentives — explicitly chosen to isolate the coordination mechanism, but real multiagent deployments (collaborative coding, negotiation, decision-making) involve much richer dynamics that this framework doesn't capture. **(A) Consensus** — the authors state this directly and frame it as a first step, not a complete model of real coordination challenges. (paper, Discussion: "we analyzed an extremely simplified scenario…We argue that adding these factors would only facilitate coordination")

The underlying cause of majority-following remains genuinely unresolved — it could come from patterns learned in training data, system-prompt effects, RLHF pressure toward "helpful and collaborative" responses, an emergent computational property of scale, or some mix of these, and the paper's only real handle on ruling anything out (checking that an open-weight model, Llama, wasn't given explicit majority-following guidance in its system prompt) doesn't extend to the closed models. **(A) Consensus** — explicitly named as an open question for future research by the authors themselves, who propose repeating the experiments with fine-tuned local models as the natural next step. (paper, Discussion)

The MMLU-versus-critical-group-size correlation, while statistically significant for the specific benchmark highlighted ($r\approx0.79$–$0.81$, $p<0.05$), rests on a fairly small number of models with published scores, and the authors note the correlation is *not* statistically significant for most of the other benchmarks they checked, attributing this mainly to limited data availability rather than a genuine absence of the relationship. **(B) Contested** — reasonable readers could take this either as early evidence of a real capability-coordination link that a larger dataset would confirm, or as a fragile correlation vulnerable to which benchmark and which handful of models happen to have public scores; the paper itself flags the caveat without resolving it. (paper, Results: "it is important to stress that the correlation with the critical group size is not significant for most of the other benchmarks")

The paper's headline "beyond human scale" framing leans on Dunbar's number (150–300) as the human benchmark, which the paper itself acknowledges "has faced recent criticism" in the anthropological literature — yet the comparison still carries substantial rhetorical weight in the abstract and title. **(B) Contested** — the authors disclose the criticism directly, but the paper's own headline framing depends on the number being roughly right; if Dunbar's number turns out to be a poor empirical anchor, the "beyond human scale" claim needs a different point of comparison, not just a footnote. (paper, Discussion, refs. 52–53; broader anthropological literature)

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

This paper asks a simple question with a surprisingly clean answer: if you drop a group of LLM-powered AI agents into a decision with two options, give them no instructions beyond seeing what their peers currently prefer, and let them update one at a time, will they spontaneously coordinate — and if so, does that coordination follow any predictable pattern? Across ten different LLM architectures (the GPT, Claude, and Llama families) and group sizes from 10 to over 1000 agents, the answer is yes to both: agents do show a measurable tendency to adopt whatever their peers' majority currently favors, and that tendency — quantified as a single number per model, the "majority force" $\beta$ — obeys the exact same mathematical law (a tanh-shaped adoption-probability curve) that describes how a simple ferromagnet's atomic spins align. This isn't a loose metaphor; fitting real experimental data from ten unrelated models to that one equation works, and rescaling by each model's own $\beta$ collapses all ten onto a single universal curve.

That physics mapping does real predictive work. It explains why some models (GPT-4 family, Claude 3 Sonnet/Opus) reliably coordinate while others (GPT-3.5 Turbo, Claude 3 Haiku) mostly don't within the same time budget — they simply have different $\beta$ values. And because $\beta$ tends to *decrease* as group size $N$ grows (a weaker pull toward consensus in bigger groups, plausibly because conforming to a majority "feels" less socially necessary when the group is huge — a minority of 100 among 900 people intuitively feels more stable than a minority of 2 among 18, even at identical proportions), the model predicts a **critical group size** for each LLM, above which coordination becomes exponentially unlikely to happen in a reasonable time. That predicted critical size correlates with independent measures of model capability (the MMLU benchmark) — more capable models sustain coordination in dramatically larger groups, with the two strongest models tested (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4 Turbo) estimated to coordinate at group sizes past 1000, well beyond the roughly 150–300-person scale at which human informal group coordination typically becomes unstable.

The key interpretive line the authors draw, and that this analysis has tried to preserve throughout: none of this demonstrates that AI agents possess social intelligence, theory of mind, or human-like reasoning about their peers. Majority-following is one of the simplest possible coordination mechanisms — the same phenomenon shows up in fish schools and bird flocks — and the paper explicitly frames its contribution as characterizing *that* one mechanism precisely, not as evidence of anything richer happening under the hood.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the derivation connecting the discrete opinion-update process to a continuous stochastic differential equation (the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck approximation behind the fluctuation-variance formula, $\text{Var}[m]\approx\tfrac{2}{N}e^{-2\beta}$, and the resulting $\beta_t(N)$ threshold) is genuinely technical stochastic-process mathematics that I can report accurately from the paper's stated results and Materials and Methods description, but haven't independently re-derived or verified step by step — I'm relying on the internal consistency between that formula and the paper's own empirical figures (which do match well) rather than working the calculus through myself. I'd also flag that the mechanistic question — *what in an LLM's architecture, training data, or fine-tuning actually produces a given $\beta$* — is where the paper's own confidence is thinnest, and where my explanation necessarily inherits that same open-endedness rather than resolving it.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. Groups of AI agents, given nothing but a list of their peers' current opinions and no instruction to conform, spontaneously drift toward whatever the majority believes — and this "majority-following" strength is captured by a single number, $\beta$, that behaves exactly like the temperature-like coupling parameter in the textbook mean-field model of a ferromagnet.
2. Because that parameter tends to weaken as the group gets bigger, every model has a predictable critical group size beyond which coordination becomes exponentially unlikely — and more capable models (measured independently via the MMLU benchmark) sustain coordination at dramatically larger scales, with the strongest models tested (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4 Turbo) estimated to hold together past 1000 agents, beyond typical human informal-group limits (~150–300, Dunbar's number).
3. None of this is evidence of genuine social reasoning in AI agents — majority-following is one of the simplest coordination mechanisms known, shared with fish schools and magnetized spins, and the paper is explicit that it does not know *why* this behavior emerges from LLM training, only that it does, and how strongly, for each model tested.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Can groups of AI chatbots spontaneously agree on something, with no one directing them? This paper tests that directly. Researchers gave groups of AI agents — powered by ten different popular models, including versions of GPT, Claude, and Llama — a simple binary choice with no objectively right answer. Each agent could see what all its peers currently preferred and had to pick an option for itself, one agent updating at a time, with no instructions to follow the crowd.

The agents did anyway, to varying degrees. More strikingly, the strength of this "follow the majority" tendency, for every model tested, fit the exact same mathematical formula used to describe how a simple magnet's atomic particles align — a textbook result from physics called the Curie-Weiss model. Each model gets characterized by a single number, called the "majority force," that determines how strongly it's pulled toward whatever the group currently favors.

That single number turns out to do real predictive work. As groups get larger, this majority force tends to weaken — agents seem to feel less pressure to conform when the group is big, mirroring intuitions about how conformity pressure works in large versus small human communities. This means every model has a breaking point: a group size beyond which it becomes essentially impossible for that model's agents to reach agreement within a reasonable time. That breaking point, in turn, correlates with how capable the underlying model is on standard AI benchmarks — smarter models can coordinate in much bigger groups. The two most capable models tested can apparently sustain coordination among more than 1000 agents, larger than the group sizes (roughly 150 to 300 people) at which human informal communities typically start to struggle.

The authors are careful to note what this doesn't prove: none of it means AI agents actually understand each other, reason about each other's intentions, or possess anything resembling true social intelligence. Majority-following is one of the simplest coordination mechanisms in nature — the same basic pattern shows up in schools of fish. What's new here is showing that AI agent groups display it too, in a way precise enough to predict, and that as AI systems get deployed in ever-larger multiagent groups, this predictable-but-simple herding instinct could be either a useful design tool or a real safety risk, since a group converging on a popular-but-wrong answer is exactly what majority-following would produce.
