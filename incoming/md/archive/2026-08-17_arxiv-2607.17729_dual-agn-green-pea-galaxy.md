## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Two Black Holes, One Weird Little Galaxy**

Astronomers just caught two separate, actively feeding supermassive black holes lighting up inside a single compact, metal-poor "Green Pea" galaxy — the first time a dual black-hole system has been confirmed in exactly the kind of small, intensely star-forming galaxy that's supposed to be a nearby stand-in for how galaxies grew in the early universe.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Two well-established but previously separate threads collide in this paper. Thread one: galaxies in the early universe are thought to have grown up compact and chaotic — small, dense, forming stars at a furious rate. We can't directly dissect those distant galaxies in detail (they're too faint, too far, too blurred by cosmic distance), so astronomers lean on "local analogs" — nearby galaxies that share the same extreme physical conditions (compact size, low metallicity, intense star formation) even though they exist today rather than 12 billion years ago. Green Pea galaxies, first identified by citizen scientists in the Galaxy Zoo project sorting through Sloan Digital Sky Survey images, are the best-known example: genuinely nearby, genuinely observable in detail, and genuinely extreme in the same ways early-universe galaxies are thought to be.

Thread two: when two galaxies merge, and each one already harbors a supermassive black hole at its center, the merger can — in principle — produce a "dual AGN": two actively accreting black holes, still spatially distinct, orbiting each other before eventually spiraling together. Confirmed dual AGN are rare and, until this paper, essentially all of them have shown up in one specific setting: massive, gas-rich galaxy mergers, the kind of collision between two large, mature galaxies. Nobody had confirmed one in a small, compact, metal-poor galaxy — exactly the kind of galaxy standing in for the early universe in thread one.

This paper reports finding both threads in the same object: SDSS J162209.41+352107.5 (called J1622+3521), a known Green Pea galaxy that turns out to be hosting not one but two independently confirmed, actively accreting supermassive black holes, 8.4 kiloparsecs apart in projection, caught mid-merger.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** this is an observational discovery paper — a single, carefully multi-instrument-confirmed detection (Chandra X-ray imaging plus Keck optical spectroscopy) establishing a genuinely new category member, not a survey, simulation, or theoretical proposal. The stake is narrower than it might sound: this doesn't prove dual AGN are *common* in compact galaxies, only that they can *happen* there — a proof of existence, not a census.

**Prior Belief Check:** the broad expectation — that galaxy mergers can trigger simultaneous growth of two black holes — is mainstream and well-established; nothing here overturns that. What's genuinely surprising to specialists is *where* it's been found: every previously confirmed dual AGN sits in a massive, gas-rich merger, and the working assumption (reasonable, given the sample) had been that you need that kind of large-scale galactic environment to trigger and sustain simultaneous dual accretion. Finding it in a galaxy this small and this metal-poor extends the known dual-AGN "habitat" into genuinely new territory experts hadn't previously confirmed access to — that's the actual news, not "black holes can pair up," which nobody doubted.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** this is a single confirmed system from a single research group, reported for the first time in this paper — there is no independent replication, because there's nothing yet to replicate; it's the discovery itself. Worth naming explicitly: several of the comparison benchmarks the paper leans on for context (the He II–X-ray scaling relation, and the one other "comparison AGN Green Pea," J0822+2241) are drawn from the same or overlapping author group's own recent prior papers (Kouroumpatzakis & Svoboda 2025; Boorman et al. 2025) rather than fully independent external work. That doesn't make the detection wrong, but it does mean the broader interpretive framework this result is being placed into is still largely one research program's own body of work, not yet cross-checked by outside groups. Independent confirmation here would look like: another team, using different instruments or a different X-ray mission, either reproducing this specific detection or finding additional dual-AGN Green Peas using comparable X-ray-plus-spectroscopy methodology.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**What a Green Pea galaxy actually is.** In 2007–2009, volunteers in the Galaxy Zoo citizen-science project, sorting through thousands of SDSS galaxy images, kept flagging small, round, uniformly green-tinted blobs — hence "Green Pea." The color turned out to be real physics: an extremely strong emission line ([O III] at 5007 Ångströms) falls squarely in the SDSS green filter when these galaxies sit at a particular redshift range. That strong line, in turn, signals intense star formation and very hot, highly ionized gas. Follow-up work found these galaxies share a specific profile: physically tiny (roughly a few hundred parsecs across, versus tens of thousands of parsecs for the Milky Way), low in heavy-element content (metal-poor, meaning fewer generations of stars have processed and enriched the gas), and forming stars at a blistering rate relative to their size. That specific combination — small, metal-poor, star-forming, highly ionized — is close to the textbook description of galaxies as they're thought to have looked during the universe's first billion or two years.

> **Analogy:** think of a Green Pea galaxy as a local, fully-instrumented staging environment that reproduces production conditions you can't directly profile — you can't attach a debugger to a galaxy 12 billion light-years away, but you can point every instrument you own at a nearby system running under comparable extreme conditions and actually watch what happens.
>
> **Breaks when:** you push it to ask whether the staging environment is *identical* to production. It isn't — a Green Pea galaxy today has had 13 billion years of cosmic chemical enrichment happening around it even if the galaxy itself stayed metal-poor, sits in a very different large-scale cosmic environment (a mostly-empty, dark-energy-dominated universe rather than a dense, rapidly assembling early universe), and was necessarily selected because it's nearby and observable — a form of selection bias baked into "local analog" science generally, not something this paper can fully correct for.

**What "dual AGN" means, and why it's hard to confirm.** An AGN (active galactic nucleus) is a galaxy's central supermassive black hole in the act of actively feeding — gas spiraling in, heating up, radiating brightly across X-ray, optical, and other wavelengths. A "dual AGN" is two of these, in one merging galaxy system, both currently feeding, still spatially separate (not yet merged into one black hole). The problem is that AGN signatures can fake each other. The most commonly used shortcut — a doubled ("double-peaked") emission-line profile, as if two separate line-emitting regions were blended together in one spectrum — turns out to be seriously ambiguous: gas outflows, rotating disks, or complicated internal gas motion inside a single galaxy can produce the exact same double-peaked signature with no second black hole involved at all. The paper is explicit about this and deliberately avoids leaning on it, instead requiring two much harder-to-fake things to line up simultaneously: spatially resolved X-ray emission (you can actually see two separate bright X-ray points, not just inferred from a blended spectral line) and, independently, two separate broad emission-line regions in the optical spectrum, each showing the high-ionization "coronal" lines that essentially only an accreting black hole can produce.

> **Analogy:** confirming a dual AGN this way is like confirming a login isn't fraudulent via multi-factor authentication rather than a single password check. A double-peaked emission line alone is like a password — real, but frequently guessable by something that isn't the thing you're trying to detect (outflows, complex kinematics). Stacking independent signals — a spatially resolved X-ray point source *and* an independently broad, high-ionization optical spectrum, for *each* of two spatially separate nuclei — is like also requiring a hardware key and a biometric check: each factor has its own false-positive channel, but genuinely independent factors rarely fail in the same way at the same time, so requiring several together multiplies down the odds of a false detection.
>
> **Breaks when:** pushed to ask whether the factors really are independent. If X-ray blending and optical spectral blending shared a common cause — e.g., both were degraded by the same close angular separation between the two nuclei — the "independence" the analogy relies on weakens. This is exactly the concern examined in the Assumption Audit and Limitations sections below: the 1.9-arcsecond separation between the two X-ray sources is close enough to Chandra's native resolving power that the "independent" X-ray detections needed real modeling work (joint fitting with explicit cross-contamination terms) to actually earn their independence, rather than being trivially separate from the start.

**Chandra X-ray imaging and why hard X-rays specifically matter.** The Chandra X-ray Observatory is a space telescope that images X-ray light with enough angular sharpness to tell two nearby sources apart, rather than just seeing one blurred blob. "Hard" X-rays (roughly 2–10 keV of photon energy, versus "soft" X-rays below ~2 keV) matter here because they penetrate through the dust and gas that tends to surround an actively accreting black hole — soft X-rays get absorbed and scattered away by that surrounding material, but the harder, more energetic photons punch through. Seeing a spatially resolved, luminous hard X-ray source is a strong hint of a genuine accreting black hole, because ordinary stellar processes (supernovae, X-ray binary stars, even a very intense burst of star formation) essentially never produce X-ray luminosities as high as the ones measured here.

**Bayesian spectral fitting and nested sampling — why the "obscuration model" matters.** The observed X-ray brightness of an obscured black hole understates its true output, because the intervening gas absorbs some of the light before it reaches the telescope. To recover the *true* (intrinsic) luminosity, the analysis fits a physical model of the surrounding obscuring gas — here, a model called BNsphere that assumes the obscurer is shaped like a sphere fully enclosing the black hole — against the observed X-ray spectrum, then asks: given this geometry, how much gas (column density) and how bright a true source would together reproduce what Chandra actually saw? Because there are many free parameters (column density, intrinsic brightness, gas composition, contamination from the neighboring source, and more — 18 in total across both sources), the fit uses a technique called nested sampling (via the BXA/MultiNest software) rather than just finding one "best" answer.

> **Analogy:** nested sampling works the way a well-designed hyperparameter search explores a large configuration space — instead of blindly guessing points, it maintains a population of candidate parameter combinations, repeatedly discards the worst-performing ones, and progressively shrinks its search toward the region of parameter space that actually explains the observed data well, eventually mapping out not just a single best answer but the full range of parameter combinations consistent with the data (a full posterior distribution, not a point estimate).
>
> **Breaks when:** pushed to ask whether "explored the whole space" means "the model itself is right." Nested sampling is honest about uncertainty *within* the model you gave it — it will faithfully report how well-constrained each parameter is, given the assumed spherical-obscurer geometry — but it cannot tell you whether a spherical obscurer is the correct shape for reality. The paper's own authors flag this directly: a fully spherical cloud of gas is, in their words, "a difficult geometry to sustain in nature." The sophistication of the sampling algorithm doesn't buy back the accuracy lost to a simplified physical assumption underneath it.

**Central analogy for this paper:** confirming a signal via independent, stacked authentication factors.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**The object and the data.** J1622+3521 sits at redshift z ≈ 0.267 (roughly 3.5 billion light-years away), previously catalogued as a Green Pea galaxy with an odd, irregular optical morphology hinting at a possible ongoing merger. The team obtained 42.7 kiloseconds (about 11.9 hours) of new Chandra X-ray imaging across three separate observations, plus new Keck/DEIMOS long-slit optical spectroscopy specifically designed to cover both suspected nuclei simultaneously in a single slit exposure, supplementing archival DECaLS and SDSS imaging and spectra.

**Finding two X-ray sources.** Standard X-ray source-detection software (`wavdetect`, which looks for statistically significant clumps of X-ray photons using wavelet analysis at multiple scales) found two distinct hard X-ray sources, 1.9 arcseconds apart on the sky — corresponding to a genuine physical separation of 8.4 kiloparsecs once the galaxy's known distance is factored in. Both sources sit right where the two optical nuclei visible in the DECaLS imaging are located.

**Extracting true luminosities from blended, obscured spectra.** Because the two X-ray sources sit close enough together that light from each leaks into the extraction region of the other, the team fit both sources' X-ray spectra *simultaneously*, with explicit "mixing" terms in the model to account for cross-contamination, rather than analyzing each in isolation. Running this joint BNsphere fit through BXA/MultiNest yields, for each source, an obscuration-corrected (intrinsic) 2–10 keV X-ray luminosity and a line-of-sight gas column density:

$$\log(N_{\mathrm{H}}/\mathrm{cm}^{-2}) = 22.8^{+0.5}_{-0.2} \ (\text{Source 1}), \quad 23.6^{+0.4}_{-0.5} \ (\text{Source 2})$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$N_{\mathrm{H}}$ : the line-of-sight hydrogen column density — a measure of how much obscuring gas sits between the black hole and Earth (units: atoms per square centimeter along the sightline)
The superscript/subscript pairs give the 90% credible interval (the range the fit is confident the true value falls within) around the central estimate.

**What this actually means:** both black holes are meaningfully obscured — buried behind enough gas that a good fraction of their light is absorbed before reaching us — with Source 2 somewhat more heavily buried than Source 1. Neither, however, reaches "Compton-thick" levels (a threshold, around $\log N_{\mathrm{H}} \sim 24.4$, at which the obscuring gas becomes so dense it starts blocking even hard X-rays almost entirely) — think of it as the difference between looking through frosted glass (still obscured, but light gets through) versus a solid wall (Compton-thick, almost nothing gets through).

Correcting for that obscuration, the intrinsic 2–10 keV luminosities come out to $\log(L_{2-10\,\mathrm{keV}}/\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}) = 43.93^{+0.04}_{-0.05}$ (Source 1) and $43.55 \pm 0.11$ (Source 2) — both roughly a hundred to several hundred times more luminous in hard X-rays than the most extreme stellar processes (supernova remnants, X-ray binary populations) can plausibly produce, which is the paper's primary quantitative argument that both sources are genuinely accreting black holes rather than star-formation impostors.

**Confirming both are real, independent nuclei with optical spectroscopy.** The Keck/DEIMOS slit spectrum, oriented to cross both suspected nuclei at once, resolves two spatially and spectrally distinct emission systems. Both independently show: broad Hα emission (FWHM ≈ 1900 km/s for Source 1, ≈1700 km/s for Source 2 — velocity widths characteristic of gas orbiting very close to a black hole, in a "broad-line region"); high-ionization lines including He II, [Ne V], and [Fe VII], which require photon energies (roughly 54–99 eV) well above anything a population of ordinary stars can supply; and, on the standard BPT diagnostic diagram (a plot of specific emission-line ratios used to distinguish star-formation-powered gas from AGN-powered gas), both sources land firmly in the AGN region rather than the star-forming or ambiguous "composite" zones.

**Black hole masses and accretion rates.** Combining the extinction-corrected broad Hα luminosity and line width through a standard "virial" mass calibration (an empirical relation linking broad-line kinematics to black hole mass, originally calibrated against reverberation-mapped AGN) gives $\log(M_{\mathrm{BH}}/M_\odot) \simeq 7.31 \pm 0.13$ and $7.29 \pm 0.16$ for Sources 1 and 2 — both around 20 million solar masses, genuinely modest by supermassive-black-hole standards. Combining the X-ray luminosities with an assumed bolometric correction (a multiplier converting X-ray output to total radiative output across all wavelengths) yields Eddington ratios — how close each black hole is accreting to its theoretical maximum feeding rate — of roughly 0.66 for Source 1 and 0.29 for Source 2: both efficiently, actively growing, with Source 1 notably close to its theoretical feeding limit.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** the reader likely assumes "confirmed" dual AGN means each black hole was nailed down by one single, decisive test. It wasn't — and the paper is explicit that this is the point. Confirmation here means several independent, individually-imperfect signals (spatially resolved X-ray luminosity, broad optical lines, BPT classification, coronal lines) all converging on the same conclusion for both nuclei simultaneously. That's a strong case, but it's a case built by triangulation, not by a single knockout piece of evidence.

> **Watch:** the reader likely assumes the "common redshift" finding is a precise dynamical measurement confirming the two black holes are gravitationally bound and orbiting each other. It's actually a consistency check with substantial uncertainty: the measured velocity difference between the two nuclei is $7 \pm 74$ km/s — the paper itself states this difference "is not significant given the uncertainties." What this genuinely establishes is that the two nuclei are at the same distance (ruling out an unrelated foreground/background pair by chance alignment); it does not establish or measure an orbital relationship between them.

> **Watch:** the reader likely assumes the quoted black hole masses (~$2\times10^7\,M_\odot$) and Eddington ratios are direct physical measurements with the precision the statistical error bars suggest. They're virial estimates built on a chain of calibrated relations, and the paper explicitly notes the underlying single-epoch virial method carries an intrinsic systematic scatter of roughly 0.4–0.5 dex (a factor of roughly 2.5–3×) that is *not* included in the quoted statistical uncertainties. The qualitative conclusion — both black holes are real and efficiently accreting — is solid; the specific numbers carry meaningfully more uncertainty than the printed error bars alone convey.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

The clever methodological move, not just the discovery itself: deliberately building the confirmation on the *harder*, more independent signal combination (resolved X-ray point sources plus independently broad, high-ionization optical spectra for each nucleus) rather than the easier, more commonly used but seriously ambiguous double-peaked-emission-line shortcut that most prior "candidate" dual-AGN claims in compact galaxies have relied on. The paper explicitly positions itself against that weaker methodology in its own introduction. That's a meaningful raise in evidentiary bar for a category of claim (dual AGN in compact galaxies) that has historically been prone to false positives.

The discovery itself is genuinely new to the field, not just to the reader: extending confirmed dual AGN, for the first time, into a host-galaxy regime (compact, low-mass, metal-poor, intensely star-forming) that had no previous confirmed members — directly relevant to ongoing debates about how efficiently supermassive black holes can grow inside the kind of small, chaotic galaxies now being found in large numbers by JWST at high redshift ("Little Red Dots" and related populations), since this local system offers a detailed, high-signal-to-noise laboratory version of a question that's otherwise only accessible through much noisier high-redshift data.

**Predictive Content Check**

**Falsifiable handle (always):** the paper makes several checkable claims that future observations could contradict. If this is genuinely a bound, pre-coalescence dual AGN system rather than one dominant AGN plus a star-forming clump mimicking AGN signatures, deeper follow-up X-ray and optical spectroscopy should continue to show two independently point-like, independently luminous, independently broad-lined nuclei — not have the fainter source (Source 2) dissolve into something diffuse or star-formation-consistent under closer scrutiny. More concretely falsifiable: the specific black hole masses (~$2\times10^7\,M_\odot$ each) and Eddington ratios (0.66 and 0.29) are testable against future reverberation-mapping or dynamical mass measurements, which carry substantially smaller systematic uncertainty than the single-epoch virial estimates used here — a future measurement landing well outside the ~0.4–0.5 dex systematic band would be a genuine problem for this paper's specific numbers, even if the qualitative dual-AGN identification survived.

**Formalism load (fired explicitly, per the extra weight this check carries under adversarial posture):** the Bayesian spectral-fitting machinery (BNsphere obscuration model + BXA/MultiNest nested sampling) is doing real, load-bearing work for the paper's central claim — without it, the raw observed X-ray counts alone would only show two obscuration-suppressed point sources of unknown true brightness, not the specific obscuration-corrected luminosities that make the "both are genuinely luminous AGN, not faint contaminated sources" argument quantitative. That said, worth flagging under adversarial scrutiny: the authors note that the recovered photon-index posteriors for both sources land right back on the input prior (1.80 ± 0.15) rather than showing the data meaningfully pulling the fit away from that assumption — meaning some of the model's apparent precision on that particular parameter reflects the prior more than an independent data-driven constraint. The core luminosity and column-density numbers are less affected by this, but it's a place where the formalism's precision slightly outruns what the data alone can independently pin down.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> This is a single confirmed system from a single research group, and several of the comparison benchmarks used to contextualize it (the He II–X-ray scaling relation, the one other "comparison Green Pea AGN") come from the same or closely overlapping authors' own recent prior work rather than fully independent external studies. **(A) Consensus** — that a sample of one, interpreted partly through one group's own prior framework, has limited generalizability is a basic and broadly uncontested point about scientific inference, not a controversial claim about this specific paper. **(analyst inference — the paper itself frames this appropriately as a discovery paper and explicitly calls for future population studies, but does not flag the self-citation pattern as a limitation in its own text)**

> The 1.9-arcsecond angular separation between the two X-ray sources is close to Chandra's native point-spread function, requiring joint spectral fitting with explicit cross-contamination ("mixing") terms rather than allowing fully independent, blend-free extraction of each source's spectrum. **(A) Consensus** — PSF-driven blending in close X-ray pairs is a well-known, well-characterized systematic in the field, and the authors explicitly identify and model it themselves rather than glossing over it. **(paper §1.4, "Blending" consideration)**

> The virial black hole mass estimates, and everything downstream of them (Eddington ratios, comparisons to black-hole–host-galaxy scaling relations), carry an intrinsic systematic uncertainty of roughly 0.4–0.5 dex that is not folded into the quoted statistical error bars. **(A) Consensus** — this is a widely acknowledged, well-documented limitation of single-epoch virial mass estimators generally, and the authors state it plainly themselves. **(paper §1.8–1.9)**

> The BNsphere obscuration model assumes a fully spherical obscuring gas geometry, which the authors themselves describe as physically difficult to sustain in nature, and the fitted photon-index parameter for both sources is largely prior-dominated rather than independently data-constrained. **(A) Consensus** — both points are explicitly stated by the authors in their own methods discussion, not points a critic had to infer. **(paper §1.4–1.5)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

The paper reports the first confirmed dual active galactic nucleus found inside a Green Pea galaxy — a category of small, metal-poor, intensely star-forming galaxy used as a nearby stand-in for conditions in the early universe. The host system, J1622+3521, was already known as an oddly-shaped Green Pea; new Chandra X-ray imaging resolved it into two separate, luminous hard X-ray sources 8.4 kiloparsecs apart, and follow-up Keck spectroscopy, deliberately designed to cover both suspected nuclei in one slit, confirmed that both sit at the same redshift and each independently shows the signature of an actively accreting black hole: broad emission lines from gas whipping around close to the black hole, plus high-ionization "coronal" emission lines that essentially require an AGN's hard radiation field to produce, plus a classification squarely in the AGN region of the standard optical line-ratio diagnostic diagram. Careful joint X-ray spectral modeling, accounting for the two sources' proximity and mutual contamination, shows both are genuinely luminous and moderately obscured, not Compton-thick, and both are converted (via calibrated but uncertain scaling relations) into black hole mass and accretion-rate estimates — around 20 million solar masses each, one accreting close to its theoretical maximum rate, the other somewhat more modestly.

The interpretive framing worth being explicit about: this is a proof-of-existence result, establishing that dual, simultaneously accreting supermassive black holes *can* occur in a compact, low-mass, metal-poor merging galaxy — a host-galaxy regime with no previously confirmed dual-AGN members — not a demonstration that this is common, or a population-level statement about how often such systems occur. The paper's own comparisons to the broader dual-AGN literature and to JWST's recently discovered population of compact, obscured, high-redshift AGN ("Little Red Dots" and related populations) are framed as suggestive context establishing why this local system matters, rather than as statistically rigorous population comparisons — which is an honest and appropriate framing for a single-object discovery paper, but worth keeping distinct from a claim about how representative or common this phenomenon is.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. Astronomers found two separate, actively feeding supermassive black holes — not one — inside a single small, metal-poor "Green Pea" galaxy, confirmed by combining spatially resolved X-ray imaging with optical spectroscopy that independently shows each nucleus has its own broad-line region and AGN-only emission lines.

2. Every previously confirmed dual-black-hole system had shown up in a massive, gas-rich galaxy merger; this is the first one confirmed in the kind of small, compact, star-forming galaxy that's used as a nearby stand-in for how galaxies looked in the early universe — extending the known "habitat" for this phenomenon into new territory.

3. It's a proof of existence, not a population count: one confirmed system establishes that dual black-hole growth *can* happen in this kind of galaxy, not how often it does — and the specific black-hole-mass and accretion-rate numbers carry real systematic uncertainty (roughly a factor of 2–3) underneath their precise-looking statistical error bars.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Astronomers have confirmed something new: a single small galaxy hosting two separate, actively growing supermassive black holes at once. The host is a "Green Pea" galaxy — a category of small, metal-poor, intensely star-forming galaxy that astronomers use as a nearby stand-in for what galaxies looked like in the universe's first billion years, since we can study these local examples in far more detail than anything that far away and long ago.

Using the Chandra X-ray telescope, the team spotted two separate, bright X-ray sources inside this one galaxy, about 8,400 light-years apart. Follow-up spectroscopy with the Keck telescope in Hawaii confirmed both sources sit at the same distance and each independently shows the telltale signs of a feeding black hole: broad, fast-moving gas near the center, and specific types of light that essentially only an actively accreting black hole can produce. Combining these two independent lines of evidence, rather than relying on a single more ambiguous signal that earlier studies have leaned on, gives a much stronger case than most previous "candidate" claims of this kind.

What makes this particular discovery notable is the setting. Every confirmed example of two black holes growing together before had shown up in massive, gas-rich galaxy collisions — big galaxies crashing into big galaxies. This is the first time it's been confirmed happening inside a small, compact, metal-poor galaxy, extending where scientists know this process can occur. Because this kind of small galaxy is also thought to resemble the conditions present when galaxies were first assembling billions of years ago, the discovery offers a rare close-up look at how black holes might have grown inside the young universe's earliest galaxies.

Both black holes here are modest by supermassive-black-hole standards — roughly 20 million times the mass of the Sun each — and both are feeding efficiently, one especially so. This is a single confirmed example, not a survey: it shows this kind of dual growth *can* happen in a small galaxy, not how often it does. The full picture of how common this is, and what happens as the two black holes eventually spiral together, awaits future observations of this and similar systems.
