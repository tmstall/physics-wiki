## 1. Holes in the Cosmic Fluid: Can the Sky Hide Negative Mass?

**Hook:** Nojiri and Odintsov argue that negative-mass objects don't need exotic matter. A star sitting in a fluid with negative pressure could push out more mass than it contains, leaving an effective negative-mass "bubble." But the paper's own numbers show the fluid would have to be denser than an atomic nucleus.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Negative mass has been a legitimate question in gravity since Bondi's 1957 analysis. Bondi separated three kinds of mass: inertial (resistance to acceleration), passive gravitational (response to a field) and active gravitational (source of a field). He showed that giving all three a negative sign is not self-contradictory. It does produce strange dynamics. The famous case is a +m / −m pair that accelerates forever in one direction with zero total energy. Relativity then added a hard wall. The positive mass theorem (Schoen–Yau 1979, Witten 1981) proves the total (ADM) mass cannot be negative in an asymptotically flat spacetime, provided all matter satisfies the dominant energy condition (DEC).

So any serious negative-mass construction has to get through one of two gaps in that theorem. **Gap 1:** the background is not asymptotically flat, for example de Sitter or anti-de Sitter. **Gap 2:** the matter violates the energy condition. Paranjape and collaborators used Gap 1 (2013–2019) to build negative-mass bubbles in de Sitter space that satisfy the DEC. This paper uses both gaps. Section II uses a fluid in a Λ < 0 (anti-de Sitter-like) background, which is Gap 1. Section III reverse-engineers scalar-field matter that produces an asymptotically flat object with negative ADM mass, which is Gap 2.

The topic is active in 2026. Manfredi, Rouet & Miller (the paper's Ref. [12]) proposed a "Bondi universe" in which negative mass drives cosmic expansion. There is also a numerical-relativity simulation of the Bondi dipole, work on gravitational-wave signals from negative-mass binaries, and shadow calculations for negative-mass wormholes. This paper adds a construction route and a qualitative sketch of what such objects would look like.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is a theoretical existence-and-phenomenology paper. It builds candidate negative-mass solutions in GR with fluids and in two modified-gravity theories, then sketches how light and matter would move around them. The stakes are conceptual, "is negative mass really that exotic?", not a claim that NMOs have been detected.

**Prior Belief Check:** The findings do not really contradict mainstream consensus, although the framing suggests they might. The consensus is that ordinary matter in an asymptotically flat universe cannot produce negative total mass. The paper doesn't overturn that. It moves negative mass into (a) a deficit measured against a background that has been fine-tuned to cancel, or (b) matter sectors designed to violate the energy conditions. Experts would not be surprised by either. The Section III reconstruction method is the authors' long-standing technique, and it can realize essentially any spherically symmetric metric by design. The one mildly surprising result is the vanishing-mass object in §VIII, which has a finite shadow even when its horizon shrinks to zero. That result is small and self-contained.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is a single-group result. The claims are mathematical constructions, so "independent confirmation" means someone else checks the derivations and, more importantly, runs a perturbative stability analysis the paper doesn't do. My own spot-check found one computational error, in §IV (see §4.5 below). That is a reason not to take the numerical details on trust.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

GR and cosmology are tier 3 for you, so I build these from the ground up. The AdS pieces lean on your tier-2 holography footing.

### 3.1 Bondi's negative mass and the runaway pair

In Newtonian gravity, an object's acceleration is (force) ÷ (inertial mass), and the force scales with the product of the two gravitational masses. Give one body mass −m and the other +m:

- The positive body is **repelled** by the negative one, so it moves away.
- The negative body feels a repulsive force too. But its inertia is negative, so it accelerates *toward* the positive body.

**Worked example:** Put +1 solar mass and −1 solar mass 1 AU apart. Each body accelerates at GM/d² ≈ 5.9 × 10⁻³ m/s², in the *same direction*. After 5.4 years both are moving at about 1,000 km/s and the gap between them hasn't changed. Total momentum stays zero, because (+m)v + (−m)v = 0. Total kinetic energy also stays zero, because ½mv² − ½mv² = 0. No conservation law is violated.

**Analogy:** A hardware prefetcher locked a fixed stride ahead of a streaming load. The demand stream advances, the prefetcher stays exactly one stride ahead, and both march through memory forever at a constant separation.

**Breaks when:** you ask who pays. A prefetcher uses real bandwidth and real power. The Bondi pair runs forever for free, because the negative body's negative kinetic energy exactly cancels the positive body's gain. The vehicle has no category for "self-propelled at zero net cost."

### 3.2 Pressure gravitates: the equation of state w and the ρ + 3p rule

In GR, pressure also sources gravity. A fluid's equation of state is written p = wρ (pressure = w × energy density, with c = 1). Dust has w = 0, radiation w = ⅓, a cosmological constant w = −1. What sets the Newtonian pull of a static region, or the acceleration of the cosmic expansion, is **ρ + 3p = (1 + 3w)ρ**, not ρ alone.

**Worked example:** A fluid with w = −⅓ has ρ + 3p = (1 − 1)ρ = 0. At weak-field order it exerts no pull on slow test particles at all. For w = −1 (dark energy), ρ + 3p = −2ρ, which is net repulsion. That is why dark energy accelerates the expansion.

**Analogy:** A bill with a base price (ρ) plus a surcharge charged once on each of the three axes (p per axis). A negative surcharge, which here means tension, can cancel the base price exactly. That happens at w = −⅓.

**Breaks when:** you look at light or at the spatial geometry. The ρ + 3p rule governs the time-time part of the metric, which is what slow particles feel. Spatial curvature, and so half of light bending, is set by ρ alone. A w = −⅓ deficit is invisible to a slow planet but not fully invisible to a photon.

### 3.3 ADM mass, energy conditions and the positive mass theorem

The **ADM mass** is the total mass read off from the gravitational field far away, at the asymptotically flat boundary. **Energy conditions** are local inequalities on matter. The dominant energy condition says, roughly, that energy density is non-negative and energy never flows faster than light. The **positive mass theorem** says: if every point satisfies the DEC and the spacetime is asymptotically flat, the ADM mass is ≥ 0.

**Analogy:** An audited ledger with a checksum. If every local entry satisfies the invariant, the total read at the boundary cannot go negative.

**Breaks when:** you think the theorem is just addition. Gravitational binding energy is *negative*, so the total is not a simple sum of non-negative entries. The theorem is hard because GR could plausibly have let binding energy drive the total below zero, and it turns out not to. The vehicle's arithmetic has no counterpart for that.

### 3.4 Reconstruction and frozen scalar fields

The Nojiri–Odintsov **reconstruction** method runs GR backward. You choose the metric you want, then solve algebraically for the scalar-field functions A, B, C and potential V that make it a solution. The scalars are fixed by hand to track the coordinates (φ = t, χ = r). Left alone, the reconstructed fields often turn out to be **ghosts**: kinetic terms with the wrong sign, meaning negative-energy modes that make the theory inconsistent. To remove them, the authors add **Lagrange-multiplier constraints** (Eq. 50). These pin the scalar gradients so that the scalars cannot propagate. This is closely related to the "mimetic gravity" constraint.

**Analogy:** Trace-driven replay versus execution-driven simulation. Reconstruction takes a desired output trace and synthesizes a "program" (A, B, C, V) that reproduces it exactly. The constraints make the replay read-only, so nothing can deviate from the trace.

**Breaks when:** you ask what the replay predicts. A replayed trace is perfectly stable because nothing in it executes, but for the same reason it tells you nothing about what a real program would do. The vehicle correctly captures "stable by construction." It also shows why that stability carries no dynamical information, which is a warning more than a mapping failure.

### 3.5 The central picture: a hole in a filled band

> **Concept:** In Section II the NMO is a *deficit*. A uniform fluid with positive density ρ₀ fills space, and a negative cosmological constant (Λ < 0) almost cancels that density so the total is near zero. A star depletes the fluid around it. The "negative mass" is the missing fluid minus the star's own mass, counted against the cancelled background. Every local energy density stays positive. The Section III NMO is a *different object*: an asymptotically flat solution whose local energy density is negative everywhere. It is not a deficit relative to anything.
>
> **Vehicle:** A nearly full valence band in a semiconductor. The band's huge negative electron charge is neutralized by the positive ion lattice. Remove one electron and you get a hole. Transport physics treats the hole as a particle with *positive* charge and *positive* effective mass, even though the electrons near the top of the band individually have negative effective mass. The hole's sign is set by bookkeeping relative to the neutral full band.
>
> **Analogy:** The filled band corresponds to the uniform fluid ρ₀. The ion lattice cancelling the band's charge corresponds to Λ < 0 cancelling the fluid's density. Missing electrons correspond to displaced fluid. The hole's flipped charge sign corresponds to the NMO's flipped mass sign. An electric field pushing the hole opposite to the electrons corresponds to gravity pushing the deficit opposite to ordinary matter, which is buoyancy, the paper's own bubble-in-water picture.
>
> **Breaks when:** you reach Section III. A hole is always missing *something* from some filled reference state. Band theory has no category for a lump whose energy is negative relative to the true vacuum, with no filled sea behind it. The Section III NMO needs exactly that, so the analogy covers Section II and says nothing about Section III.

**Central analogy for this paper:** A hole in a filled band.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### 4.1 Mechanism A: a star pushes negative-pressure fluid away (§II.A)

They put a perfect fluid (p = wρ, with −1 < w < 0) into a fixed Schwarzschild background and solve hydrostatic balance.

$$\rho(r) = \rho_0\, e^{-\frac{1+w}{w}\,\nu(r)}, \qquad e^{2\nu} = 1 - \frac{2M}{r}$$

**Symbol definitions:**

- $\rho(r)$ : fluid energy density at radius r
- $\rho_0$ : fluid density far from the star
- $w$ : equation-of-state parameter (pressure ÷ density), negative here
- $\nu(r)$ : gravitational potential term in the metric; it rises toward zero far away
- $M$ : the star's mass (geometric units, G = c = 1)

**What this actually means:** for −1 < w < 0 the exponent's prefactor is positive, so density *rises* with distance. The fluid is thinner near the star and denser far away. The star acts like a heat source in a room of negative-pressure gas: the gas is pushed out of the region around it. The "lost mass" is the missing fluid integrated over the depleted region.

Integrating out to infinity diverges, so they either cut off at a radius r_Λ or pick an artificial equation of state (Eq. 14) that keeps the integral finite. The finite version gives a clean result.

$$M_{\rm total} = M - 32\pi M^3 \rho_0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad M_{\rm total} < 0 \;\text{ when }\; 32\pi M^2 \rho_0 > 1$$

**Symbol definitions:**

- $M_{\rm total}$ : net mass (star minus displaced fluid)
- $32\pi M^3\rho_0$ : total fluid displaced by the star (Eq. 16)

**What this actually means (worked box):** A black hole's mean density inside its Schwarzschild radius is M ÷ [(4π/3)(2M)³] = 3/(32πM²). So the condition says: **the surrounding fluid must be at least one-third as dense as the black hole itself.** For one solar mass that is ρ₀ ≳ 6.1 × 10¹⁸ kg/m³. The paper's §VII gives ≳10¹⁸. That is about 27 times nuclear density, and about 10⁴⁴ times the mean density of the universe (~10⁻²⁶ kg/m³). The authors say plainly that it needs fine-tuning against Λ. The mechanism does what it claims in principle, but at parameters no astrophysical setting supplies.

The cutoff version (w = −⅓ exactly, Eq. 13) fares worse, by my own calculation. For large cutoffs, lost mass ÷ star mass ≈ 4πGρ₀r_Λ²/c². Take ρ₀ at the cosmic critical density and the paper's own suggested cutoff, r_Λ ~ inter-star spacing ~ 1 pc. The ratio comes out ≈ 8 × 10⁻²⁰. To reach 1 you need r_Λ ≈ 3.4 Gpc, which is roughly the Hubble radius. The paper's statement after Eq. 9, "Anyway, we find M_lost ≫ M," is given without numbers, and plausible numbers do not support it. The only way out is a fluid far denser than the cosmic average, cancelled by Λ, which is the same fine-tuning again.

One more point on the w = −⅓ example, from §3.2: this is exactly the value where ρ + 3p = 0. At weak-field order the displaced fluid carries no *active* gravitational mass, so a slow test particle feels no extra repulsion. The lost mass the paper counts (a Misner–Sharp mass, built from ρ alone) goes into the spatial geometry. Only part of it would show up as the "anti-gravity" force the paper is after. More generally, the Newtonian repulsion from the deficit is scaled by (1 + 3w), which is smaller than the ρ-only bookkeeping suggests.

### 4.2 Adding back-reaction (§II.B)

Next they let the fluid curve spacetime, solving the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff structure equations with Λ < 0. The only consistent large-r solution has the fluid density exactly offsetting Λ: ρ₀ = Λ / [κ²(1 + 3w)], which needs w < −⅓. The mass function goes negative for w < −⅔. The authors state honestly that this spacetime is **not asymptotically flat**. The "negative mass" belongs to the whole cosmological background, not to a localized object you could point a telescope at. That motivates Section III.

### 4.3 Mechanism B: reconstructing an asymptotically flat NMO (§III.A–B)

They take the Hayward regular black hole and flip the sign of the mass in the numerator only.

$$e^{2\nu} = e^{-2\lambda} = 1 + \frac{2Mr^2}{r^3 + 2M\lambda^2}$$

**Symbol definitions:**

- $M > 0$ : a mass parameter; the ADM mass is −M
- $\lambda$ : core-size parameter (length)
- $r$ : areal radius

**What this actually means:** far away the metric looks like Schwarzschild with mass −M. Near the center it is regular, with no singularity. The reconstruction (§3.4) then supplies scalar functions that make this an exact solution, and the constraints freeze the scalars.

The paper doesn't state the local energy density of this object, so I worked it out. It is **negative everywhere**: ρ = −3M²λ² / [2π(r³ + 2Mλ²)²]. At the center it approaches −3/(8πλ²), a constant negative vacuum-like density. That is an **anti-de Sitter core**. For your tier-2 footing: where the Hayward black hole has a de Sitter core, the anti-Hayward NMO has an AdS core. This is the "negative mass" of Gap 2 in §2, and the positive mass theorem requires it: negative ADM mass in flat asymptotics needs DEC-violating matter. The reconstruction supplies that matter by design.

**"Dynamical creation" (§III.B).** Eq. 57 multiplies the mass term by a smooth step in time. Flat space at t → −∞ evolves into the NMO at t → +∞, and positive energy flows outward along the way. The authors call this false-vacuum decay. But φ = t is imposed, so the "decay" runs on the coordinate clock set by the chosen profile. It is replay, not dynamics (the §3.4 analogy): nothing in the model decides *whether* or *where* an NMO forms. That is fixed by the ansatz.

**Scalar-Einstein–Gauss–Bonnet version (§III.C).** Same logic in a string-inspired theory. Solve for the coupling f(ξ), potential V and kinetic function A that support any chosen a(r), then add a constraint to kill the ghost. It is the same existence-by-construction.

### 4.4 Massive particles: repulsive outside, AdS trap inside (§V)

In the Newtonian limit the NMO potential is Ψ = Mr² / (r³ + 2Mλ²). Beyond r* = (4Mλ²)^{1/3} it *decreases* outward, which is repulsive. Inside r* it *increases* outward, which is attractive. The paper finds bound geodesics only inside r*. This fits §4.3: near the center Ψ ≈ r²/(2λ²), a harmonic well, and that is how AdS behaves. AdS works as a confining box. The core has negative ρ but positive pressure, so ρ + 3p = −2ρ > 0 and the core *attracts*. The sign of the object's total mass and the sign of its active mass differ locally.

### 4.5 Photon orbits: a concave lens, and a calculation error (§IV)

The qualitative result holds. U′(r) < 0 everywhere (Eq. 83), so there is no photon sphere, and light is deflected outward. The NMO acts as a diverging gravitational lens.

The paper's exact special case, with impact parameter b = L/E = 3√3M in the small-core limit, has an error. They substitute r = 3M cosh ζ, but the integrand (Eq. 89) needs **r − 3M = 3M cosh ζ**. Done correctly:

- The quartic under the square root factors as (r + 3M)²(r − 6M), so the **closest approach is r = 6M, not 3M**. The paper's "maybe accidental" match with the Schwarzschild photon-sphere radius is an artifact of the error.
- The deflection angle is

$$\Phi = \pi - 2\ln\!\left(2+\sqrt{3}\right) \approx 0.508\ \text{rad} \;(29.1^\circ)$$

**Symbol definitions:**

- $\Phi$ : outward scattering angle of the photon
- $\ln(2+\sqrt3) = 2\,\mathrm{artanh}(1/\sqrt3)$ : half the angle the photon sweeps around the NMO

**What this actually means:** the paper's π − √3 ≈ 1.41 rad (81°) overstates the bending by nearly a factor of 3. I checked by numerical integration: at b = 3√3M it gives 0.5077 rad, and at large b it reproduces the weak-field value 4M/b (0.07560 vs 0.08 at b = 50M; 0.003988 vs 0.004 at b = 1000M). The qualitative lens claim is unaffected.

### 4.6 Newtonian two-body mechanics (§VI)

For m₁ > 0, m₂ < 0 with net mass M = m₁ + m₂ > 0, the relative orbit is an ordinary Kepler problem with total mass M. The center of mass lies outside the pair, on the far side of the positive body. Both bodies accelerate toward that point, so a **bound state** exists. I checked the force directions and they are correct in Newtonian terms. For m₁ = −m₂, the paper derives a solution with constant nonzero relative velocity v₀ that ends in a collision. It divides by |v₀|³, which excludes v₀ = 0. That excluded case is **Bondi's classic self-accelerating pair**, the textbook runaway (§3.1), and the paper never discusses it directly.

### 4.7 Observability proposals (§VII, §IX)

These are qualitative: a bound pair flashing a bright background as it orbits, galaxies "screened" by NMO clouds like a nucleus screened by electrons, outward energy flux during NMO creation, perturbed AGN jets, holes drilled into rocks by small NMOs falling to Earth's center, and a Rutherford-like scattering cross-section that doesn't depend on the sign of the masses. None comes with a number.

### 4.8 The vanishing-mass object (§VIII)

The metric e^{2ν} = 1 − γM²/(r² + M²) falls off like 1/r², so its ADM mass is zero. It acts like a Reissner–Nordström object with no mass and a "charge-squared" term of the wrong sign. For γ > 1 it has a horizon at r_h = √(γ−1)M, a photon sphere and a shadow of radius r_sh = (√γ + √(γ−1))M. I verified the photon-sphere and shadow formulas numerically for γ = 1.01, 2 and 5, and they match exactly. The neat result: as γ → 1 the horizon and photon sphere shrink to zero, but the **shadow stays at radius M**. The elliptic-integral orbit formulas (Eqs. 123–129) are exact but don't lead to any stated observable.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** The reader likely assumes "NMO" means one kind of object with negative energy. **The paper actually uses the label for two different objects.** Section II's is a deficit relative to a cancelled background, with all local densities positive. Section III's has negative energy density everywhere and an AdS core. The "not so exotic" argument applies only to the first, and the first fails its own numbers (§4.1). The asymptotically flat NMO that the lensing and orbit sections study is the exotic one.

> **Watch:** The reader likely assumes the equivalence principle forces an NMO to have negative *inertial* mass. **The paper asserts this for NMOs in general (§I, §V), but its own motivating analog contradicts it for composite objects.** An air bubble in water is pushed up by buoyancy ≈ ρ_water·V·g, and its inertia is dominated by the "added mass" of water it has to move, ≈ ½ρ_water·V. So it accelerates *upward at about 2g*: negative effective gravitational response, **positive** inertia. A Section II NMO likewise can't move unless the depleted fluid flows. Nothing in the paper shows that this composite has negative inertia, and the semiconductor hole in §3.5 also has positive effective mass. The equivalence argument applies cleanly only to a single object with flat asymptotics, which is the Section III kind.

> **Watch:** The reader likely assumes "stable" means dynamically stable under perturbations. **The paper means two weaker things.** In Section III, the constraints freeze the scalars, so no perturbation can propagate by construction. In Section II, stability is simply asserted ("Such a configuration is stable in general"). But a barotropic fluid with p = wρ and w < 0 has sound speed squared c_s² = w < 0, which is the classic gradient instability: small density ripples grow instead of oscillating. No perturbation analysis appears anywhere in the paper.

> **Watch:** The reader likely assumes the positive mass theorem rules all of this out, or that the paper got around it cleverly. **The paper never names the theorem.** It gets around it silently: through non-flat asymptotics in Section II, and through reconstructed matter that violates the energy conditions in Section III. Both are legitimate gaps, but the reader has to supply the map.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

1. **Negative mass as buoyancy against a cancelled background.** The paper uses a negative cosmological constant to cancel a positive fluid density, so a local deficit reads as net negative mass, and it ties this to the bubble-in-water intuition. The idea is intuitive and *new in this specific pairing* (Λ < 0 plus a negative-pressure fluid plus a compact star). But it is conceptually close to Paranjape's negative-mass bubbles in de Sitter space, which the paper doesn't cite, so it is new to the reader more than to the field.
2. **An asymptotically flat, regular, "anti-Hayward" NMO with lensing and a scripted creation history.** The reconstruction plus ghost-freezing constraints yields a singularity-free negative-ADM-mass object with a clean concave-lens profile. The vanishing-mass object's finite shadow at zero horizon size is the most transferable technical nugget.

### Predictive Content Check

**1. Falsifiable handle.** It is weak. The nearest concrete prediction is the **concave-lens signature**: outward deflection ≈ 4|M|/b in the weak field (exactly 29° at b = 3√3M, after my correction). A compact NMO in front of a source would dim rather than brighten it. For the vanishing-mass object, the shadow radius (√γ + √(γ−1))M is a clean number, but γ and M are free. The paper gives no NMO mass scale, abundance or spatial distribution, and its only numerical estimate (§VII) shows the Section II mechanism *doesn't* work for stellar masses. Negative-mass lensing light curves were worked out decades ago (Cramer et al. 1995), and survey upper limits on abundance exist (e.g. Takahashi & Asada 2013, SDSS quasar lenses). The paper connects to none of it. Verdict: **this sketches possibilities rather than predicting**, and it has no quantitative handle a survey could test that the prior lensing literature doesn't already cover.

**2. Formalism load.** This half **fires**. Reconstruction can realize *any* spherically symmetric metric, so producing an NMO solution carries no information about whether NMOs exist. It shows only that the theory space was made flexible enough to include one. The scalar functions A, C, V and f(ξ) have no independent physical motivation. The "dynamical creation" follows a schedule fixed by the ansatz φ = t. The elliptic-integral photon orbits (Eqs. 123–129) end without a stated observable. Delete all of Section III and the empirical content of the paper doesn't change: in the Newtonian/lensing limit, a point mass of −M would reproduce §§IV–VII.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

1. **The Section II mechanism needs fluid densities ≳ 6 × 10¹⁸ kg/m³ (about 27 times nuclear) for a solar-mass star, and fine-tuning against Λ to about 44 orders of magnitude.** **(A) Consensus.** The paper computes this itself, and no known astrophysical fluid comes close. **(paper §VII)**

2. **The claim "M_lost ≫ M" with a cutoff (after Eq. 9) is unsupported.** With cosmic-density fluid and a ~1 pc cutoff, the ratio is ~10⁻¹⁹. Reaching 1 needs a Hubble-scale cutoff. **(C) Speculative.** This is my own order-of-magnitude calculation from the paper's Eq. 13, assuming ρ₀ near critical density. A much denser Λ-cancelled fluid changes it, at the price of the fine-tuning in item 1. **(analyst inference)**

3. **Stability is asserted, not shown.** A barotropic fluid with w < 0 is gradient-unstable, and freezing the scalars with constraints makes perturbations non-dynamical by fiat. Mimetic-type constraints have their own documented ghost and gradient problems once matter couplings and perturbations are included. **(B) Contested.** The fluid instability is textbook, but whether constrained ("mimetic-like") theories are healthy is actively debated. **(broader literature)**

4. **The Section III NMO has negative energy density everywhere (an AdS core), and the paper never engages the positive mass theorem or energy conditions for it.** **(A) Consensus.** The theorem requires DEC violation for negative ADM mass with flat asymptotics. The density profile is my derivation from the paper's metric. **(broader literature + analyst inference)**

5. **The negative-kinetic-energy problem is dismissed.** An NMO with negative inertia is a negative-energy degree of freedom. Coupled to ordinary matter or radiation, it lets both sectors grow without limit while conserving energy, which is the physical content of the "ghost" the paper sets out to avoid. The paper omits Bondi's v₀ = 0 runaway and treats the outward energy flux during creation as an observable rather than a sign that flat space is unstable in the model. **(A) Consensus** that negative kinetic energy is the core pathology. The paper acknowledges only that its treatment is "completely classical." **(paper §IX + broader literature)**

6. **The inertia of a composite NMO is unaddressed.** The bubble analog and the added-mass effect suggest a Section II deficit has positive inertia, which would undercut §V–VI's dynamics for that class. **(C) Speculative.** This extends fluid added-mass reasoning to a GR fluid background with non-flat asymptotics, and a full treatment could change the answer. **(analyst inference)**

7. **The §IV exact deflection angle and closest approach are wrong: 0.508 rad at 6M, not 1.41 rad at 3M.** **(C) Speculative** under the tag rules, since it is my own derivation. It is cross-checked by numerical integration and by the weak-field limit. The qualitative concave-lens conclusion is unaffected. **(analyst inference)**

8. **The observational proposals are qualitative and disconnected from existing negative-mass lensing searches.** **(B) Contested.** How strongly a conjecture paper should be held to quantitative predictions is a matter of taste, but the prior lensing constraints do exist. **(broader literature)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

**Summary.** The authors ask whether objects with negative total mass could exist in the universe without breaking the consistency of gravity theory. They start from buoyancy. A bubble in water rises as if its mass were negative, because the water around it is pulled down. By analogy, a compact star in a fluid with negative pressure pushes that fluid away. If a negative cosmological constant nearly cancels the fluid's background density, the depleted region plus the star can add up to a net negative mass. They compute when this happens. The displaced fluid outweighs the star only if the ambient fluid is at least a third as dense as a black hole of the same mass, far beyond anything in nature, as their own estimate confirms. When they let the fluid bend spacetime self-consistently, the negative mass spreads over the whole cosmological background instead of forming a localized object.

To get a localized object in otherwise flat space, they switch to modified gravity. Using a reverse-engineering method, they choose a target geometry: a regular black-hole geometry with its mass sign flipped. Then they solve for scalar-field ingredients that produce it, and freeze those fields with constraints so they can't misbehave. The object that results has negative energy density throughout, with a core that behaves like anti-de Sitter space. Around it, light bends outward, so the object acts like a diverging lens. Slow particles are pushed away from its outskirts but can be trapped near its core. In Newtonian two-body mechanics, a positive mass and a lighter negative mass can orbit each other. They close with an object whose total mass is exactly zero yet which can still have a horizon and cast a finite shadow.

**Explanation.** I framed this around the split between *relative* and *absolute* negative mass (the hole-in-a-filled-band analogy) because the paper's persuasive claim, "not so exotic," rests on the relative version. The objects it actually studies in the lensing and orbit sections are the absolute version. The relative version is physically tame but quantitatively impossible. The absolute version is exotic by construction, since it has to violate the energy conditions that underwrite the positive mass theorem. The paper is best read as a clear sketch of how negative-mass phenomenology *would* look, with an existence proof that is guaranteed by the reconstruction method rather than earned. The corrected lensing angle and the vanishing-mass shadow are the parts worth keeping.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the two arguments I built myself from weak-field reasoning. These are the (1 + 3w) / ρ + 3p point that the w = −⅓ deficit is gravitationally inert for slow particles, and the added-mass argument that a composite Section II NMO has positive inertia. Both rest on linearized or Newtonian intuition carried into a background that isn't asymptotically flat, and a full GR treatment could change them. The §IV correction, by contrast, is checked numerically and I'm confident in it.

---

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **"Negative mass" here comes in two kinds: a bubble and a genuine anti-mass.** The bubble kind is harmless but needs a surrounding fluid denser than an atomic nucleus. The genuine kind needs matter built specifically to break the energy rules that normally forbid it.
2. **If a negative-mass object existed, it would act as a diverging gravitational lens.** Light bends outward, and a compact one in front of a star would make a dark spot instead of a bright ring. The paper overstates the bending by nearly a factor of three, but the direction is right.
3. **The paper's modified-gravity method can build any geometry you ask for, so finding a negative-mass solution shows the theory is flexible, not that such objects exist.** The open question isn't whether negative mass can be written down. It's whether it stays stable once it interacts with ordinary matter.

---

## 9. Shorter Summary

Could objects with negative mass, which push ordinary matter away instead of pulling it, exist in space? Nojiri and Odintsov argue they might not be as exotic as assumed. Their starting picture is a bubble rising in water. It moves as if its mass were negative because the water around it falls.

Their first construction puts a star inside a hypothetical cosmic fluid with negative pressure, whose background density is cancelled by a negative cosmological constant. The star pushes the fluid away, and if enough fluid is displaced, the combined system has a net negative mass. The catch, which the authors themselves compute, is that the fluid would have to be about a third as dense as a black hole of the same mass, roughly 27 times denser than an atomic nucleus. Once the fluid's own gravity is included, the negative mass spreads across the whole universe instead of forming a single object.

Their second construction uses modified gravity. They pick a target shape of spacetime, a regular black hole with its mass sign flipped, and solve backward for the ingredients that produce it. The result has negative energy everywhere inside it. Light passing nearby bends outward, so the object behaves like a diverging lens, and a positive and a negative mass can orbit each other. The backward-solving method can produce any shape on demand, so this shows possibility, not likelihood. My independent check found that the paper overstates the light-bending angle by nearly a factor of three, though the outward direction holds.

The paper's most novel result is a separate object with exactly zero total mass that can still cast a shadow.

The paper offers no specific numbers an astronomer could test, and it doesn't engage earlier searches for exactly this kind of outward-lensing signature. Its main value is a clear sketch of what negative mass would look like, not evidence that it exists.
