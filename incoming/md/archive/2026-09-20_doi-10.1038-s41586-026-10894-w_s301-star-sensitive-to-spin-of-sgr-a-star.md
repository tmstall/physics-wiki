## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**A Star Threading the Needle at 8% Light Speed — and Setting Up the First Real Shot at Weighing How Fast Our Galaxy's Black Hole Spins**

GRAVITY+ has found a faint, unremarkable-looking main-sequence star, S301, whose orbit happens to be so tight and so eccentric around Sagittarius A* that within about a decade of continued monitoring, it could deliver the first dynamical measurement of a supermassive black hole's spin from stellar motion alone.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

**Paper type & stakes:** this is an observational discovery paper — VLTI/GRAVITY near-infrared interferometric astrometry — that also does substantial theoretical forecasting work. It reports finding a new star on an extreme orbit, and the stakes are specific and near-term: no previously known star has had a small enough pericenter distance to make the black hole's *spin* (as opposed to just its mass and the non-spinning general-relativistic effects) dynamically detectable on a human timescale. S301 is the first one that plausibly does.

Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*), the 4.297×10⁶-solar-mass black hole at the Milky Way's center, has been probed for nearly 30 years via the orbits of "S-stars" — ordinary stars that happen to swing close to it. That program has already delivered: sub-percent mass precision, the gravitational redshift and transverse Doppler shift (order β² = (v/c)² effects, confirmed via S2 in 2018), and the Schwarzschild precession — the non-spin general-relativistic advance of the orbit's pericenter each lap (order β³, confirmed via S2 in 2020, ref. 8). What none of this has touched is *spin*. A black hole's spin is a second free parameter (mass and spin are the *only* two a real astrophysical black hole is allowed by GR to have — no hair, per the Kerr solution), and it's a much harder thing to measure: for stellar dynamics, the spin-induced effect (Lense–Thirring precession, "frame dragging") only becomes large enough to detect if a star gets close enough to the black hole for long enough. Every known S-star before this one, including S2, has had too large a pericenter distance for that.

S301 changes this. It's an 8.7-year-period star with an eccentricity of ~0.98 and a pericenter roughly ten times closer (in Schwarzschild radii) than S2's — close enough that the paper's own forecast, using realistic near-future GRAVITY+/ELT-MICADO precision, projects a >5σ spin detection within about a decade.

**Prior Belief Check:** this is not a result that overturns anything in the field's theoretical understanding — nobody doubted that black hole spin should in principle be measurable this way, and stars like this were already anticipated in the literature (a companion theoretical paper on exactly this idea, "S301 and friends: Measuring the spin of Sgr A*," appeared on arXiv within weeks of this one). What *is* genuinely surprising, in the sense of being a stroke of observational luck combined with real technical achievement, is that such an extreme, favorably-configured object actually exists and was found: an ~10x tighter pericenter than the best previously known S-star, on a star faint enough (K-band magnitude 19.3) that it required interferometric imaging at the edge of current sensitivity to catch. It's best described as an incremental-in-theory, field-changing-in-practice result — the theoretical target existed on paper; this paper is the first to show a concrete, reachable example of it.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** this is currently a single-facility, single-collaboration result. All astrometry comes from GRAVITY/GRAVITY+ at the VLTI, cross-checked internally by two independently-implemented fitting codes plus a classical CLEAN-algorithm reconstruction (Extended Data Fig. 2) — a meaningful internal check, but not independent replication by another instrument or group. Worth flagging explicitly: Galactic Center astrometry has real precedent for *disputed* short-period-star claims — the paper itself directly addresses and rebuts prior claims of similarly short-period stars (S4711, S62) made by a different group using adaptive-optics speckle imaging (ref. 49, 50), noting those claimed orbits don't match what GRAVITY's higher-resolution data show and that no flux consistent with those claimed objects appears in GRAVITY's own reconstructed images. That context doesn't cast doubt on S301 itself — the discovery here rests on multiple independent epochs, an internally-consistent orbit fit, and two independent imaging/fitting pipelines — but it's a useful reminder that this specific subfield has a track record of claims needing outside confirmation before they're treated as settled. Independent confirmation for S301 would look like: detection with a different high-resolution technique (future ELT/MICADO imaging is explicitly named in the paper as a near-term candidate), or a radial-velocity measurement, which would also resolve the orbit-orientation ambiguity discussed below.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Why stellar orbits can probe a black hole at all.** A star on a bound orbit is, in effect, a free-falling clock and ruler dropped into the black hole's gravitational field. As long as you can track its position (and ideally velocity) precisely enough over time, its orbit encodes exactly how spacetime is curved where it travels. The closer the star gets to the black hole at pericenter, the stronger the relativistic effects it picks up — which is why "how small a pericenter distance can we find a star at" is the single number that has driven this entire research program for three decades.

**The two-precession picture — Schwarzschild vs. Lense–Thirring.**

**Concept:** In Newtonian gravity, a bound two-body orbit around a point mass is a perfectly closed ellipse — trace it once, and it retraces itself exactly forever. General relativity breaks this in two physically distinct ways. First, even for a *non-spinning* black hole (the Schwarzschild solution), spacetime curvature alone makes the ellipse's long axis slowly rotate, lap after lap — the point of closest approach (pericenter) advances a little further around each orbit. This effect exists regardless of anything about the black hole's spin; it's purely a function of how deep the star dips in (formally, order β³ in v/c). Second, if the central mass is *spinning* (the Kerr solution), a wholly separate, additional precession appears — frame dragging, formally the Lense–Thirring effect — caused specifically by the black hole's rotation dragging the local inertial frames around with it. This term depends on the spin magnitude (a dimensionless number χ between 0 for non-spinning and 1 for maximally spinning) and on the angle between the spin axis and the star's orbital plane. Crucially, these two precessions are additive and separable in principle, but the spin term only becomes large enough to disentangle from the (much bigger) Schwarzschild term if the star's pericenter is small and if enough orbits are tracked.

**Vehicle:** Picture a single-core process issuing the exact same memory operation against a fixed address, over and over, with nothing else running. Now impose a background thermal-throttling ramp that quietly adds a small, constant extra delay to *every* operation, no matter what's being accessed and no matter what else is happening on the chip — present even with total isolation. That's a baseline systemic skew, independent of any other component's state. Now add a second effect: a neighboring hardware unit — say, a prefetcher — that is itself cycling through an internal rotation at some rate. An access that happens to land while that unit is in a particular phase of its own cycle picks up a *second*, smaller, separate timing skew, one that specifically depends on how fast that neighboring unit is cycling and how your access's timing lines up with it. If the neighboring unit isn't cycling at all, this second skew vanishes entirely; the baseline throttle delay does not.

**Analogy:** baseline throttle delay ↔ Schwarzschild precession (always present, doesn't care about spin). Neighboring unit's own rotation rate ↔ black hole spin magnitude χ. How your access's timing lines up with that rotation ↔ the angle ξ between spin axis and orbital angular momentum. The second, smaller timing skew that only exists because something else is spinning ↔ Lense–Thirring precession.

**Breaks when:** you ask the vehicle to explain *why* either delay exists geometrically. In the hardware picture, both delays come from resource contention and scheduling — physical processes happening *in* a fixed background spacetime. Frame dragging isn't a delay imposed on the star by some rotating object nearby; it's the coordinate system itself — spacetime — being twisted by the black hole's rotation. There's no hardware equivalent for "the reference frame you're measuring against is itself being dragged around" — pushing on this doesn't reveal a better vehicle detail to add, because timing-skew vehicles don't have a slot for "the ruler itself rotates." That's a categorical gap, not an imprecision.

**Central analogy for this paper:** Two additive timing skews — one baseline, one spin-linked.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**How S301 was found.** GRAVITY has monitored the central arcsecond around Sgr A* since 2017 on ~monthly campaigns. In spring 2023, image reconstruction (the collaboration's own Bayesian forward-modeling code, "GRAVITY-RESOLVE," G^R) turned up a faint, previously unknown point source 15 mas northwest of Sgr A*, moving fast — later designated S301. Four 2023 positions gave a preliminary orbit fit (via Markov-chain sampling), which the team then used *predictively*: they generated a probability cloud for where S301 should have been in earlier archival data (2021, then 2017) and went looking there specifically, rather than re-scanning blindly. Both post-dicted searches succeeded (Extended Data Figs. 3–4) — a nice example of using a provisional model to recover marginal detections that a blind search likely would have missed, since the 2017 detection in particular is only "weakly inferred." The final orbit rests on 19 astrometric positions spanning 2017–2025, cross-validated by a second, independently coded fitting method and a classical CLEAN-algorithm image reconstruction.

**The orbit itself, and its one real ambiguity.** The fit gives a semi-major axis of 83 mas (≈687 AU at the adopted distance of 8,277 pc), a period of 8.68 ± 0.11 years, and — the headline number — an eccentricity of 0.9832 or 0.9821 (two statistically indistinguishable solutions; more below). That translates to a pericenter distance of only 136–142 Schwarzschild radii, roughly ten times closer than S2's 1,400 Rs. Because GRAVITY has only 2D astrometry for S301 (no radial velocity — spectroscopic attempts with ERIS haven't detected the star at all, being too faint), the orbit's 3D orientation is degenerate: two mirror-image orientations (differing in inclination, ascending node, and argument of pericenter) fit the sky-projected data equally well and are currently indistinguishable. This is a real, paper-acknowledged gap — not a subtlety being added here — and the paper notes that a future Rømer-delay measurement (light-travel-time differences across the orbit) could in principle break the degeneracy, though current data isn't precise enough.

**A worked check — do the numbers actually hang together?**

The paper states S301 reaches about 25,000–25,600 km/s (8.3–8.5% of light speed) at pericenter. This is checkable directly from Kepler's laws, and doing so is worth it — it's the same relation underlying everything else in the paper (the precession forecast, the tidal analysis, the Hills-capture argument), so seeing it work once with real numbers is worth more than restating it as a black box.

The vis-viva relation gives orbital speed at any point; at pericenter specifically it simplifies to:

$$v_{\rm peri} = v_\oplus \sqrt{\frac{M}{M_\odot}\cdot\frac{1}{a[\mathrm{AU}]}\cdot\frac{1+e}{1-e}}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $v_{\rm peri}$ : orbital speed at pericenter (closest approach)
- $v_\oplus$ : Earth's own orbital speed around the Sun, 29.78 km/s — this is just the natural unit for "one AU around one solar mass," so it drops in as a conversion constant
- $M$ : mass of the central body (here, Sgr A*, 4.297×10⁶ M_☉)
- $a$ : semi-major axis, in AU
- $e$ : orbital eccentricity

**What this actually means:** it's ordinary Kepler's-third-law bookkeeping, scaled by how much more massive Sgr A* is than the Sun, and boosted by the eccentricity factor $(1+e)/(1-e)$, which blows up as $e\to 1$ — that's the whole reason near-parabolic orbits produce huge pericenter speeds even at modest energy.

Plugging in $M/M_\odot = 4.297\times10^6$, $a = 687$ AU, $e=0.9832$: the eccentricity factor $(1+e)/(1-e) = 1.9832/0.0168 \approx 118$. Inside the square root: $4.297\times10^6 \times 118 / 687 \approx 7.38\times10^5$. Square root: ≈859. Multiply by 29.78 km/s: **≈25,590 km/s** — matching the paper's own 25,600 km/s to within rounding. That's a genuinely reassuring level of internal consistency between the quoted orbital elements and the quoted peak velocity, using nothing but 19th-century celestial mechanics.

**The Lense–Thirring forecast — the paper's actual payoff.** The orbit-averaged in-plane spin precession is:

$$\Delta\varpi_{\rm LT} = -8\pi\chi\cos\xi\left(\frac{GM_{\rm MBH}}{a(1-e^2)c^2}\right)^{3/2}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\Delta\varpi_{\rm LT}$ : Lense–Thirring shift in the pericenter's orientation, per orbit
- $\chi$ : dimensionless spin parameter, 0 (non-spinning) to 1 (maximally spinning)
- $\xi$ : angle between the black hole's spin axis and the star's orbital angular momentum
- $G$, $c$ : Newton's constant, speed of light
- $a(1-e^2)$ : the orbit's semi-latus rectum — effectively, a size scale that's smaller for tighter, more eccentric orbits

**What this actually means:** everything about how detectable the spin is comes down to how small $a(1-e^2)$ is — i.e., how deep and how eccentric the orbit is. The whole reason S301 is special is that it makes this quantity small enough to matter. For S301, this works out (per the paper) to about 0.11° of extra pericenter drift per orbit for a maximally spinning, favorably-aligned black hole — small, but stacked against a baseline (non-spin) Schwarzschild drift of about 1.9–2.0° per orbit that GRAVITY+ can already track to high precision. Over roughly a decade (~1.2 orbits), that's the signal the paper's mock-data forecast says is recoverable to >5σ with a spin-magnitude uncertainty below 0.2, assuming ~100 μas astrometric precision and 1 km/s radial-velocity precision (the latter still needing to actually be achieved — ERIS hasn't managed it yet).

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** the reader likely assumes this paper *measured* Sgr A*'s spin. It didn't. No spin value is reported. What's reported is a star whose orbit makes a spin measurement *feasible* within about a decade of further monitoring — the paper is a discovery-plus-forecast, not a measurement. The title's "sensitive to" is doing real, literal work, not looser marketing language.

> **Watch:** the reader might assume the two orbit-orientation solutions leave the discovery's headline properties (eccentricity, period, pericenter distance) uncertain. They don't — those quantities agree between the two solutions to within a percent or two; only the *3D orientation* (inclination, node, argument of pericenter) is genuinely degenerate, which matters for predicting the spin-precession geometry but not for "how extreme is this orbit."

> **Watch:** the reader might read "sets a new record for shortest known orbital period" as including the previously-claimed S4711/S62 (period ~4–5 years by another group's speckle-imaging analysis). It doesn't — this paper explicitly disputes those claims as inconsistent with GRAVITY's own higher-resolution data (see the Replication & Convergence Note above) and sets its record relative to *independently confirmed* S-stars only (S55/SO-102 at 12 years being the prior holder).

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**1. The recovery technique, not just the detection.** Finding a faint (mK≈19.3) point source 15 mas from a much brighter one is already hard; what's genuinely clever is using a *preliminary* orbit fit from the four 2023 detections to generate a targeted search region in *earlier* archival data (2021, then 2017), rather than re-processing those epochs blind. This let the team pull two additional, otherwise-marginal detections out of data already sitting in the archive, extending the time baseline from months to eight years and correspondingly tightening the orbit enormously. This is new to the specific application (this particular star), not a new method in the field generally — orbit-informed post-diction is a known technique in this literature — but it's executed cleanly here and is directly why the orbit is as well-constrained as it is.

**2. Identifying the first practically-reachable spin-sensitive star.** This is the paper's real contribution to the field: not a new technique, but a new *object* that happens to sit in exactly the regime (small pericenter, high eccentricity, sufficiently bright and stable to track for a decade) that makes the long-sought spin measurement newly tractable on realistic near-future instrumentation, rather than requiring an as-yet-undiscovered even-more-extreme star or a future-generation telescope that doesn't exist yet.

**Predictive Content Check**

*Falsifiable handle:* strong and explicit. The paper predicts that ~10 years of continued GRAVITY+ monitoring (10 astrometric points/year at ~100 μas precision, 10 additional near-pericenter points, plus 1 km/s radial-velocity precision from future instrumentation) should measure the spin parameter χ to an uncertainty below 0.2 at >5σ significance relative to the non-spinning case, for favorable spin-orbit alignments (Fig. 3a, Extended Data Fig. 9 maps out which alignments are favorable and which are not). This is a genuine, falsifiable, dated prediction: if by the mid-2030s the pericenter-advance track for S301 continues to follow the non-spinning (χ=0) curve within measurement error, that's a real result (either low spin, unfavorable alignment, or a problem with the forecast's assumptions), not something the paper can retroactively explain away.

*Formalism load:* fires, and is judged load-bearing rather than decorative. The Lense–Thirring formula (Eq. 1) directly generates the quoted 0.11° figure that the whole feasibility argument rests on; the tidal-disruption and relaxation-timescale machinery (Eqs. 2–11) directly generates both the "tidal effects are negligible" conclusion and the Hills-formation-rate estimate (~10⁻⁶ per year for S301-like orbits) that supports the paper's origin story. None of this reads as math dressing up a qualitative claim — remove it, and the specific quantitative predictions (both the spin forecast and the formation-rate consistency check) disappear with it.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> **The 3D orbital orientation remains genuinely degenerate**, pending either a radial-velocity measurement or a Rømer-delay detection precise enough to break the two-solution ambiguity. **(A) Consensus** — this is stated plainly by the authors themselves as a current limitation of the astrometry-only dataset. **(paper §Best-fit orbit, Methods)**

> **The Hills-disruption origin story is a plausibility argument, not a dynamical proof.** It rests on order-of-unity fudge factors ($f_1$, $f_2$ in Eqs. 4–5) for the captured binary's mass ratio, inclination, and internal eccentricity distribution that the paper doesn't pin down from first principles, and the paper itself explicitly notes there's a live, non-negligible alternative: in-situ formation with unusually incomplete relaxation (quoted at roughly a 3% chance under a thermal eccentricity distribution). **(B) Contested** — the authors present Hills capture as the more natural explanation but explicitly leave the alternative open rather than ruling it out. **(paper §Origin, "A Hills origin for S301")**

> **This is currently a single-instrument, single-facility detection** with no independent confirmation via another telescope or technique, and the star has not yet been detected spectroscopically (ERIS has failed to detect it as either a continuum source or via spectral lines) — meaning there's no radial-velocity data at all yet, and no orbit confirmation outside the GRAVITY imaging/fitting pipeline itself. **(A) Consensus** — explicitly acknowledged in the paper's own text regarding both the ERIS non-detection and the reliance on GRAVITY astrometry alone. **(paper §Observations and data analysis / §Spectral type, mass and age)**

> **The future spin-detection forecast is built on idealized, not-yet-achieved data quality** (100 μas astrometric precision, 1 km/s radial-velocity precision), and the paper's own text flags that a fully robust future spin constraint will need modeling to second post-Newtonian order to avoid systematic bias — work explicitly deferred to a future study, not done here. **(A) Consensus** — the paper states this limitation itself rather than it being an outside critique. **(paper §Spin sensitivity)**

> **An unseen population of stellar-mass black holes near Sgr A* could dilute or complicate the spin signal**, via ordinary Newtonian perturbations that mimic or partially cancel the Lense–Thirring signature. The paper's own N-body simulations (60 stellar-mass black holes in a plausible density profile) suggest this is sub-dominant compared to Lense–Thirring for moderate-to-high spin and favorable alignments, and that the two effects have different orbital-phase signatures (LT concentrated near pericenter, perturbations more spread toward apocenter) that could in principle separate them — but the real population size is only weakly constrained observationally (an upper limit, not a measurement), so this remains a live source of systematic uncertainty for the actual future measurement, not just this paper's simulated mock data. **(B) Contested** — the paper argues the effect is likely manageable but is working from a simulated, not observationally pinned-down, perturber population. **(paper §Newtonian perturbations of the spin measurement)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

The paper reports the discovery of S301, a faint (K-band magnitude 19.3 ± 0.3) late-A/early-F main-sequence star (mass roughly 1.1–1.5 M☉, radius 1.4–1.6 R☉) on an 8.68-year orbit around Sagittarius A*, found via VLTI/GRAVITY near-infrared interferometric imaging across 19 astrometric epochs spanning 2017–2025. The orbit is exceptionally eccentric (e ≈ 0.98) with a pericenter distance of only ~136–142 Schwarzschild radii — about ten times closer than the previous benchmark star, S2 — producing a peak orbital speed near 25,000 km/s, roughly 8.3–8.5% of the speed of light. Because only 2D astrometric positions exist (no radial velocity yet), the orbit's 3D spatial orientation has two statistically equivalent solutions, differing in inclination and orientation angles but agreeing closely on the orbit's intrinsic shape and size.

The central scientific claim is not a measurement but a demonstrated *opportunity*: S301's combination of small pericenter and high eccentricity puts the Lense–Thirring (frame-dragging) precession caused by the black hole's spin within reach of realistic near-future instrumentation — GRAVITY+ astrometry at ~100 μas precision plus future radial-velocity measurements from instruments like ELT/MICADO — over roughly the next decade. The paper backs this with a full mock-data forecast (simulated future observations through 2035) showing that the spin parameter χ could be constrained to an uncertainty below 0.2 at greater than 5σ significance for a wide range of spin-orbit alignments, which would represent the first-ever dynamical measurement of a supermassive black hole's spin from stellar orbits, as distinct from accretion-disk-based methods (X-ray reflection spectroscopy, which carry model-dependent assumptions) or gravitational-wave-based methods (which apply only to merging black holes, not isolated ones like Sgr A*).

A secondary but substantive thread of the paper addresses S301's likely origin. Given its extreme eccentricity and the star's dynamical relaxation timescale being far longer than its main-sequence lifetime, in-situ formation followed by gradual orbital relaxation is statistically disfavored (though not ruled out — roughly a 3% chance under standard assumptions). The favored explanation is the Hills mechanism: S301 is interpreted as the captured survivor of a tightly bound binary star system that wandered close enough to Sgr A* to be tidally disrupted, with its former companion ejected as a hypervelocity star. This is consistent with the star's inferred compact pre-disruption binary separation (~0.1 AU) and predicts a specific, testable equatorial rotation velocity (20–70 km/s) if the star was tidally synchronized before disruption — a concrete target for future high-resolution spectroscopy.

The paper is explanatory in framing, not defensive — it's a clean discovery-plus-forecast paper from an established, credible collaboration, with the one genuinely open empirical question (orbit orientation) plainly stated as such rather than glossed over.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the N-body simulation of Newtonian perturbations from a hypothetical population of ~60 stellar-mass black holes near Sgr A* (the section addressing whether an unseen dark-object population could dilute the spin signal) is the part I've taken most at face value. I've reported the paper's own conclusion that this effect is sub-dominant for favorable configurations, and I understand the qualitative argument for why (a different orbital-phase signature than Lense–Thirring), but I have not independently verified the simulation's density-profile assumptions or checked how sensitive that "sub-dominant" conclusion is to plausible variations in the assumed perturber population — a population whose actual size is, by the paper's own admission, only weakly constrained by current observations. That's the single place in this analysis where a specialist in stellar-dynamics N-body methods might have a more informed read than I can offer here.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **This paper didn't measure a black hole's spin — it found the first star capable of letting anyone measure it.** S301's orbit brings it about ten times closer to Sgr A* (in relative terms) than any previously known star, which is the specific ingredient that's been missing for three decades of trying to detect black hole spin via stellar motion.

2. **The star moves at 8.5% of the speed of light at closest approach — and that number checks out from first principles.** Basic 19th-century orbital mechanics (Kepler's third law, scaled for a 4-million-solar-mass central object and an eccentricity of 0.98) reproduces the paper's quoted peak velocity almost exactly, which is a good sign the reported orbital elements are internally self-consistent.

3. **The likely backstory is violent: S301 is probably the surviving half of a binary star system that got torn apart by the black hole's tidal field**, with its former companion flung out of the system entirely as a hypervelocity star — meaning S301's extreme orbit isn't the result of gradual gravitational shuffling over eons, but the leftover signature of one specific disruptive event.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Astronomers using the GRAVITY interferometer at the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope have discovered a faint star, named S301, orbiting Sagittarius A*, the supermassive black hole at the center of our galaxy. What makes S301 remarkable is how close it gets: at its nearest approach, it swings to within roughly ten times less distance (relative to the black hole's size) than any previously known star, reaching speeds over 8% of the speed of light.

That extreme closeness matters because it opens a door that's been shut for the entire history of this research program: the chance to actually measure how fast Sagittarius A* itself is spinning. Every previous star studied this way has only been close enough to reveal the black hole's mass and confirm basic predictions of Einstein's general relativity that don't depend on spin at all. S301 is different — its orbit is tight and eccentric enough that a subtle extra wobble caused specifically by the black hole's rotation, called frame dragging, should become detectable within about a decade of continued observation, using instruments that either already exist or are coming online soon.

The astronomers also worked out a likely backstory for S301: it was probably once half of a binary star pair that wandered too close to the black hole and got ripped apart by its gravity, with one star ejected out into the galaxy at extreme speed while S301 itself got captured onto this tight, wild orbit.

One real uncertainty remains: because the telescope currently sees the star's position but not its velocity toward or away from Earth, there are two mirror-image possibilities for exactly how the orbit is tilted in three-dimensional space. Resolving that — likely through future spectroscopic observations — will sharpen the case further, but doesn't affect the paper's central finding: this is the best-positioned star yet found for finally weighing the spin of our galaxy's black hole.
