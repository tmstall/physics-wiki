## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Building the Calibration Set the Reionization-Era Universe Never Left Behind**

TEMPOS is the first spectroscopic sample uniform and deep enough to ask an oddly basic question massive-star physics still can't answer: do the winds of the hottest, most massive stars just keep fading smoothly as you strip away their metals, or do they hit a wall?

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Massive O stars are wildly overrepresented in their effect on galaxy evolution relative to their numbers. They live for only a few million years, but in that time they pour out ionizing UV photons, drive fast line-accelerated winds, and end as supernovae — and together those three things regulate how a galaxy forms stars, how it heats and ejects its own gas, and how much of its ionizing radiation actually escapes into the intergalactic medium. That last point matters cosmologically: the current best guess for what reionized the universe around z ≳ 6 is exactly this kind of massive star, in exactly this kind of low-mass, low-metallicity dwarf galaxy — the closest present-day analogs we have to the galaxies JWST is now resolving at the edge of the observable universe.

The problem is that essentially everything we know quantitatively about how O-star winds and ionizing spectra depend on metallicity (Z) is calibrated using stars in the Milky Way, the LMC (~50% solar), and the SMC (~20% solar) — none of which reach anywhere near the ≲10–20% Z☉ regime that actually describes early-universe dwarf galaxies. Below the SMC, the literature is a patchwork: a handful of far-ultraviolet (FUV) spectra collected across many different HST programs, with different gratings, different resolutions, different signal-to-noise targets, observed for different original science goals. You cannot cleanly compare star to star or trend to trend across that patchwork, because you don't know whether an apparent difference is astrophysical or just a difference in how well each spectrum happened to be measured.

TEMPOS (The Treasury of Extremely Metal-Poor O Stars, HST-GO-17491, PI O. G. Telford) is a Cycle 31 HST Large Treasury program built to fix exactly that. It combines 12 newly observed O stars with 17 archival ones (mostly drawn from the ULLYSES Low-Z sample) across six nearby (≤1.6 Mpc), very metal-poor dwarf galaxies — Leo P, Leo A, Sextans A, WLM, NGC 3109, and IC 1613 — spanning roughly 3%–14% solar oxygen abundance, and re-observes or newly observes every one of them to a common minimum standard: at least two COS gratings, a guaranteed signal-to-noise floor at the key diagnostic wavelengths, covering the same ~1150–1750 Å window every time. This paper is the survey-design paper and the first data release: it does not yet deliver the detailed stellar-atmosphere modeling (mass-loss rates, full abundances) that is TEMPOS's ultimate goal — that's explicitly future work — but it does report the first empirical, model-independent measurements the raw spectra support: stellar radial velocities, ten photospheric line strengths, and two independent estimates of wind terminal velocity.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** this is a survey-methodology and first-data-release paper with a genuine, if preliminary, empirical results section attached — the stakes are providing massive-star and stellar-population-synthesis modelers their first internally consistent spectroscopic benchmark deep enough into the very-low-Z regime to actually test (rather than extrapolate) the radiation-driven wind and feedback prescriptions that reionization-era galaxy models currently have to assume.

**Prior Belief Check:** most of what TEMPOS reports here is *confirmatory*, not surprising, to the field: that winds get weaker at lower Z is exactly what radiation-driven wind theory (line opacity scales with metal abundance) has predicted for decades, and this group's own earlier, smaller studies of individual extremely metal-poor O stars (2021, 2023, 2024) had already found weak winds in this regime. What is a genuine, mildly surprising new wrinkle — one the authors themselves flag as preliminary — is that the wind velocities in the *most* metal-poor third of the sample (≲10% Z☉) don't just continue the smooth decline seen from the LMC through the SMC through the "metal-poor" (~10–20% Z☉) bin; they drop off *more steeply* than that smooth trend would predict. If that holds up under full atmosphere modeling, it would be a genuinely non-trivial result — a hint that something about wind-driving physics changes character, not just magnitude, below roughly a tenth solar metallicity.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** TEMPOS itself is a single-collaboration observational program — there is currently no second facility capable of collecting equivalent FUV spectroscopy of stars this faint, so "independent replication" in the usual sense isn't really available yet. What internal cross-validation exists: new and archival COS spectra of the same stars in the same grating were checked against each other for consistency (Section 4.2), and the sample's individual radial-velocity and equivalent-width measurements agree in the moderate- and low-resolution gratings within stated uncertainties for most stars. The wind-velocity–metallicity comparison additionally borrows an independently published comparison sample (43 SMC + 47 LMC O stars from Hawcroft et al. 2024, drawn from the ULLYSES HST program) and applies the *same* measurement method to it — a genuine, if modest, form of cross-sample consistency check, though it is a different kind of check than an independent group re-measuring the same TEMPOS stars. Independent confirmation of the low-Z wind-strength break specifically would mean either another group's non-LTE atmosphere modeling of these same spectra reaching the same conclusion, or (eventually) an equivalent survey of very-metal-poor O stars built by a different team.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**O stars and how they're classified.** O-type stars are the hottest, most massive class of "normal" star (surface temperatures roughly 30,000–50,000 K, masses above ~15–20 M☉). Astronomers classify them along two independent axes: spectral type (SpT, O2 through O9.7, essentially a temperature ladder — earlier numbers are hotter) and luminosity class (LC, a Roman numeral from V for a still-core-hydrogen-burning dwarf through III for a giant to I for a supergiant near the end of its life, tracking evolutionary stage rather than temperature). Both are read off patterns of absorption and emission lines in a star's spectrum, historically from *optical* light; TEMPOS instead works in the far-ultraviolet, where the diagnostics are different lines but the same underlying physical picture.

**Why FUV, and the moderate-vs-low-resolution grating trade-off.** The far-ultraviolet (roughly 1150–1750 Å) is where an O star's own light and the fingerprints of its wind and photosphere are strongest, but it's also where Earth's atmosphere is completely opaque — hence needing HST's Cosmic Origins Spectrograph (COS). COS offers a genuine trade-off familiar in any measurement system: a moderate-resolution grating (G130M/G160M, R ~ 12,000–16,000) resolves fine spectral detail — narrow interstellar absorption lines cleanly separated from broader stellar features, individual weak metal lines distinguished from their neighbors — but demands much longer exposure time to reach a usable signal-to-noise ratio, because spreading the same photon budget over many more resolution elements means fewer photons land in each one. The low-resolution grating (G140L, R ~ 1500–4000) collapses that fine structure but reaches adequate SNR far faster and covers more of the spectrum per exposure. It's the same choice a systems engineer makes between a fine-grained instrumented trace that captures every event precisely but costs enormous overhead, and a coarse sampling profiler that runs cheap and fast but blurs together things that happen close together in time. TEMPOS's design (Section 3.3) explicitly picks the low-overhead option (G140L) as the workhorse for full wavelength coverage and reserves the high-fidelity option (G130M, sometimes plus G160M) for the specific diagnostic windows — the C III 1176 Å complex, the C IV/Si IV/N V wind lines — where the fine structure is the whole point.

**Radiative line-driven winds — the paper's real physical throughline.**

**Concept:** A hot O star radiates enormous UV luminosity, but its own gravity is comparatively weak for a star that size — not weak enough to let the atmosphere simply float away on its own, but weak enough that a fairly modest additional outward push is enough to accelerate material off the star entirely, producing a continuous outflow (a "wind") that reaches some asymptotic, or *terminal*, velocity (v∞) far from the star. That extra push comes from photons being absorbed by the gas and handing over their momentum. Neutral hydrogen and helium, which make up the vast bulk of the gas by mass, are almost transparent to an O star's own UV continuum — their strong resonance transitions sit at wavelengths the star doesn't emit much of. What actually absorbs the momentum is the much smaller population of heavier elements (iron, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, silicon, and their various ionization states), which collectively have enormous numbers of UV resonance-absorption lines. As the wind accelerates outward, the Doppler shift continuously moves each line's effective absorbing wavelength deeper into the star's own spectrum, letting the same finite set of transitions sweep up momentum from a *moving* target rather than a fixed one — this is the essential trick that makes line-driving efficient at all. Critically, each individual transition has a hard ceiling: once it has absorbed essentially every photon available in its narrow wavelength slice, it is *saturated* and cannot contribute any more force, no matter how much more of that ion happens to be present. The net result (established theoretically since Castor, Abbott & Klein 1975, and not itself re-derived in this paper — TEMPOS cites it only as motivating background) is that total wind-driving force grows with metal abundance, but sublinearly — roughly as a fractional power of Z, not one-to-one — because the earliest, strongest lines saturate first and each additional one contributes less.

**Vehicle:** Picture a fixed total momentum "budget" the star's photon output could, in principle, hand off to outbound gas in a given interval — think of it as the maximum theoretical bandwidth of a shared bus. That budget can only actually be moved through the individual channels physically present to carry it: each metal ion's UV resonance line is one addressable channel, with its own fixed address (a wavelength) and its own maximum per-channel capacity (set by how strong that transition is and how much of that ion exists in the right ionization state). Adding more channels — more metal atoms, i.e. higher Z — lets more of the theoretical bus bandwidth actually get used, because more of the star's photon output now finds *some* channel able to absorb it. But each channel has a hard bandwidth ceiling: once it is saturated, it cannot carry more traffic even if you nominally add more capacity behind that same address. So total achieved throughput rises with channel count, but with steadily diminishing returns as the strongest, earliest-populated channels hit their ceiling first.

**Analogy:** star's UV photon output ↔ the bus's total theoretical bandwidth. Each metal ion's resonance line ↔ one addressable channel with a hard bandwidth cap. Higher metallicity ↔ more channels physically populated and available. Terminal wind velocity / mass-loss rate ↔ the aggregate throughput actually achieved. Line saturation ↔ a channel pinned at its bandwidth ceiling.

**Breaks when:** you push the vehicle to ask what happens as you keep removing channels indefinitely. In the bus picture, fewer channels just means smoothly, predictably lower throughput — two channels are worse than twenty, but not a *different kind* of system. The real physics may not cooperate: the whole "many lines summing smoothly to one aggregate force" picture is fundamentally a statistical argument, built on there being enough absorbing transitions that their combined effect behaves like a smooth continuum quantity. At genuinely extreme metal deficiency, so few ions of the right species survive in the right ionization state that this statistical premise itself can start to fail — the system may not shrink gracefully into a smaller version of the same wind, it may transition into something qualitatively patchier or less efficient. That is exactly the live, unresolved question TEMPOS's own low-Z result (Section 5 below) puts pressure on: the extremely metal-poor sample's winds don't sit a little below the smooth multi-galaxy trend, they appear to fall off "more dramatically" — a kind of departure the clean channel-counting bus picture, by construction, has no way to represent, because it assumes channel-count and per-channel capacity are the only two things that matter.

**Central analogy for this paper:** radiative line-driving as a shared, saturating channel bus.

**A second, simpler diagnostic worth naming: the "iron forest."** Beyond the individually resolved lines used for radial velocities, TEMPOS also measures the *total* absorption within two crowded wavelength windows (around Fe V 1370 Å and Fe IV 1569 Å) packed with dozens of overlapping, individually unresolvable iron transitions — nicknamed "iron forests." Rather than trying to measure each tiny tree in the forest, the paper integrates the whole canopy at once as a coarse proxy for how much iron absorption is present overall — the equivalent of counting aggregate cache-miss events across a dense cluster of similar memory accesses instead of profiling each instruction individually, when the instructions are too tightly packed to disentangle one at a time. **Breaks when** you want to convert that aggregate number into an actual iron *abundance*: how deep the forest looks also depends on the star's temperature (which sets what fraction of iron sits in the Fe IV vs. Fe V ionization state — Figure 4 shows Fe IV strengthening and Fe V weakening toward cooler, later-type stars for exactly this reason), so a raw EW comparison across different spectral types conflates ionization-balance effects with actual abundance differences; the paper is careful to compare only at fixed spectral type.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**Sample construction.** TEMPOS assembled its 29-star sample in two pieces. The *Archival* sample (17 stars) draws mostly from the ULLYSES Low-Z sample plus a few from other archival HST programs, filtered to require: confirmed O-star spectral classification from ground-based optical spectroscopy; a host galaxy within 1.6 Mpc (beyond which individual stars become too faint to observe efficiently); host oxygen and iron abundances ≲20% Z☉; and minimal Milky Way dust extinction. The *New* sample (12 stars, all newly observed by TEMPOS between 2023 December and 2026 February, 110 orbits across 42 visits) fills gaps the archival sample left uncovered — stars previously observed in only one grating, stars with spectral types not yet represented, and four stars newly identified from ground-based Keck imaging/spectroscopy (KCWI, DEIMOS) that had never been targeted with COS at all. Every star, new or archival, ends up covered in at least two COS gratings meeting a common SNR floor — the uniform-quality-control step that is the survey's whole methodological point. The resulting sample splits into two working metallicity bins: "extremely metal-poor" (≲10% Z☉ — Leo P, Leo A, Sextans A) and "metal-poor" (~10%–20% Z☉ — WLM, NGC 3109, IC 1613), based on their host galaxies' *nebular/supergiant* oxygen and iron abundances (Table 1) — not yet the O stars' own abundances, which require the future atmosphere modeling.

**Data reduction and coaddition.** Individual exposures for a given star/grating were downloaded from MAST, quality-checked (rejecting datasets with failed target acquisition, incomplete exposure time, or spectra dominated by noise), then coadded using the ULLYSES team's own software, resampled onto a common wavelength grid and averaged weighted by exposure time and throughput. Because individual TEMPOS spectra are faint (typical continuum SNR < 10 per native pixel), the coadds were additionally binned by six pixels — one full COS resolution element — to make weak stellar lines detectable and measurements more robust; this binned version is what every subsequent measurement in the paper uses.

**Thread 1 — Stellar radial velocities (Section 6.1).** The team fit Gaussian profiles simultaneously to up to eight photospheric absorption lines (C III 1176 Å, N III 1184 Å, C III 1247 Å, O IV 1342/1343 Å, S V 1502 Å, He II 1640 Å) to measure each star's radial velocity, doing this separately for the moderate-resolution gratings (G130M, or G130M+G160M together) and for G140L, since the two have very different nominal wavelength-calibration accuracy (3 km/s vs. 150 km/s). In the process they discovered something the paper explicitly flags as new and previously unreported: G140L observations taken at a central wavelength setting of 800 Å show a *systematic* blueshift relative to the moderate-resolution velocities — up to ~200 km/s in the worst cases — that is far larger than random measurement noise and does not depend on wavelength across the line list used. This isn't merely "our measurement was imprecise"; it looks like an actual wavelength-calibration bias specific to that COS configuration, confirmed via HST Help Desk contact to be present in other, unrelated 2025-era datasets taken at the same cenwave. The team's practical fix: always adopt the moderate-resolution v_rad as the star's true radial velocity, and use the G140L value only internally, to shift and normalize the low-resolution spectrum for other measurements (never as a physical result on its own).

Once each star's true systemic velocity was pinned down, the team compared it against its host galaxy's known rotation curve. Eleven of 29 stars (38%) show offsets from the local rotation-curve-predicted velocity larger than 17 km/s — the conventional "runaway star" threshold (Blaauw 1961; corresponds to a 3D space velocity of ~30 km/s under isotropic assumptions). Digging further: seven of those eleven have offsets in the *wrong direction* relative to the galaxy's own rotation (i.e., can't be explained away as simply being on the "fast" side of a rotating disk), one has an offset roughly 30 km/s larger than the galaxy's maximum rotation speed, and the remaining three could plausibly be lower than 17 km/s once the rotation curve is properly accounted for. That leaves eight stars (28% of the full sample) as genuine, high-confidence velocity outliers — a strikingly high fraction for a set of relatively isolated dwarf galaxies, where dynamical crowding is not expected to be a major driver. The two leading explanations offered (both standard in the runaway-star literature, neither confirmed here): ejection from a supernova in a binary system, or dynamical ejection from a small stellar cluster.

**Thread 2 — Photospheric equivalent widths (Section 6.2).** For ten photospheric absorption features (including the iron forests described above), the team measured equivalent width (EW) — a single number summarizing how much flux a line or line group removes from the underlying continuum, integrated over a fixed rest-wavelength window.

Break sentence before equation:

EW = ∫ (1 − f_λ / f_λ^cont) dλ

**Symbol definitions:** EW : the equivalent width (Å) — the width, in wavelength, that a hypothetical fully black (zero-flux) rectangular notch of the same total absorbed flux would have; f_λ : the observed flux density at wavelength λ; f_λ^cont : the locally fit continuum flux density (the flux the spectrum would have shown at that wavelength with no absorption at all); λ : wavelength (Å), integrated over a fixed window bracketing the feature.

**What this actually means:** this is exactly the same move as summarizing a dip in a performance counter's time series by its total integrated area under a fitted baseline rather than reporting every individual sample point — you trade the detailed shape of the dip for one clean, comparable number you can plot against spectral type or metallicity across the whole sample, which is precisely what Figure 4 does for the iron forests.

Using this measure, the paper reports that Fe IV 1569 Å EW rises and Fe V 1370 Å EW falls toward cooler, later spectral types (the expected ionization-balance effect), and — the actually interesting comparison — that at fixed spectral type, stars in the "metal-poor" (~10–20% Z☉) host galaxies show systematically *stronger* iron-forest absorption than stars in the "extremely metal-poor" (≲10% Z☉) hosts. Since individual O-star iron abundances haven't been separately measured for most of this sample, this is presented explicitly as a first, qualitative empirical signal — consistent with, but not yet a substitute for, the detailed abundance measurements that future atmosphere modeling will provide.

**Thread 3 — Terminal wind velocities (Section 6.3).** Using the C IV 1548, 1550 Å doublet (chosen because it's detectable across the widest range of spectral types in this sample — Si IV 1393 Å disappears in the hottest stars, N V 1238 Å weakens in the coolest ones), the team estimated v∞ two independent ways: the **blueward extent (BE)** method, simply reading off the bluest velocity at which the observed, continuum-normalized profile returns to the continuum level; and the **Sobolev-with-exact-integration (SEI)** method, a semi-empirical model fit (via a genetic-algorithm optimizer, pyGA) to the profile's shape, which additionally accounts for turbulent broadening in the wind.

**Worked numerical micro-example (Doppler shift, using the paper's own real numbers):**

Break sentence before equation:

v = c (λ_obs − λ_rest) / λ_rest

**Symbol definitions:** v : radial velocity along the line of sight (km/s); c : the speed of light, 299,792.458 km/s; λ_obs : the observed (measured) central wavelength of an absorption line; λ_rest : the line's rest-frame wavelength — where it would sit for a source at zero velocity relative to us.

**What this actually means:** this is a clock-skew measurement — a spectral line is a known, fixed reference marker (like an expected clock-edge timestamp), and any bulk motion of the source toward or away from us delays or advances where that marker actually lands, exactly the way you'd infer relative clock drift between two systems from an observed-vs-expected timestamp offset.

Take Leo P's O star (ECG LP26), whose adopted radial velocity is 273 km/s. Using the C III 1176 Å line as the reference: Δλ = λ_rest × v/c = 1176 Å × (273/299,792.458) ≈ 1.07 Å. That's the actual physical wavelength shift the team has to measure precisely to pin down v_rad to a few km/s — smaller than one COS resolution element is not the issue (it's still many resolution elements wide), but it shows why a *systematic* 200 km/s error, corresponding to Δλ ≈ 1176 × (200/299,792.458) ≈ 0.78 Å, is a genuinely large fraction of the separation between neighboring transitions in a crowded region like an iron forest (often only 1–5 Å apart) — exactly the kind of error that could plausibly cause real line mis-assignment or blending confusion if it went unrecognized in low-resolution data. **Generalizing:** this is why the paper treats the newly discovered G140L cenwave-800 systematic as a real, non-cosmetic data-quality finding rather than a footnote — an unrecognized ~0.8 Å bias is large enough to matter for exactly the kind of crowded-line diagnostics TEMPOS's whole low-Z sample depends on.

Across the 25 stars for which both methods could be applied, BE and SEI values correlate well but not identically: BE overestimates SEI by a median 15% (about 270 km/s), attributed to SEI's inclusion of turbulent line-broadening (which the pure blue-edge measurement doesn't capture) — a pattern consistent with an analogous offset noted decades ago between blue-edge and saturated-line velocity measures (Prinja et al. 1990). For four stars the C IV profile was too weak/purely photospheric to get a reliable SEI fit at all. Comparing the resulting SEI-based v∞ across four metallicity bins — LMC (~50% Z☉) and SMC (~20% Z☉) from the independently published Hawcroft et al. (2024) ULLYSES sample, plus TEMPOS's own "metal-poor" (~10–20% Z☉) and "extremely metal-poor" (≲10% Z☉) bins — the smooth, expected decline in typical wind speed from LMC through SMC through the metal-poor bin gives way to a noticeably sharper drop in the extremely metal-poor bin: it "sits apart" from the otherwise-smooth trend, with wind strengths that "fall off more dramatically" than the LMC→SMC→metal-poor progression alone would predict (Figure 8).

**Assumption Audit**

**Watch:** Reader likely assumes "terminal wind velocity" in Table 5 is a settled, precise physical quantity for each star. **The paper actually says** it is two different empirical/semi-empirical proxies (BE and SEI) that systematically disagree with each other by a median 15%, that for four stars neither method gave a usable measurement at all, and that even the better of the two (SEI) is explicitly described as a stand-in for the *real* answer — which will only come from full non-LTE atmosphere modeling, deferred to future papers.

**Watch:** Reader might assume every star's spectral type and luminosity class in Table 2 were freshly, homogeneously determined from this program's own spectra. **The paper actually says** most classifications are inherited from older ground-based optical catalog papers of varying vintage (spanning roughly 2007–2022) and will themselves be updated by TEMPOS's own future optical spectroscopy — the paper is explicit that this classification heterogeneity is part of why the wind-velocity-vs-SpT/LC trends in Figures 6 and 8 are "weak" with "large scatter," since SpT/LC only imperfectly and qualitatively trace the underlying stellar parameters (T_eff, luminosity, mass) that actually set wind strength.

**Watch:** Reader might assume the "extremely metal-poor" vs. "metal-poor" labels describe measured abundances of the O stars themselves. **The paper actually says** these categories are adopted from *each host galaxy's more evolved blue/red supergiant population* (a different, later stage of stellar evolution than the O stars, though drawn from the same interstellar gas reservoir) — a proxy standing in for a proxy, explicitly acknowledged, pending the individual O-star abundance measurements the future atmosphere modeling will provide.

**Watch:** Reader might read "28% of the sample are runaway stars" as a clean detection of dynamically ejected stars. **The paper actually says** this is a velocity-offset-above-a-threshold criterion that cannot, on its own, distinguish a true dynamically ejected runaway from an ordinary radial-velocity shift caused by orbital motion in an unresolved binary — a real ambiguity the paper raises itself (several TEMPOS targets already show independent evidence of likely unresolved companions) without resolving it.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Four things stand out as this paper's actual contribution, as distinct from what it merely reports using established methods:

**A genuinely uniform sample where none existed before.** The single hardest problem in this subfield hasn't been getting *any* FUV spectra of very metal-poor O stars — it's been that the spectra that exist were never collected to a common standard, so nobody could cleanly compare star to star. Enforcing the same minimum grating combination and SNR floor across all 29 stars, old and new data alike, is the paper's foundational (if unglamorous) achievement, and it's what makes every subsequent measurement in the paper actually comparable across the sample for the first time.

**A concrete instrumental finding useful well beyond this one paper.** The discovery of a previously unreported ~200 km/s systematic wavelength-calibration bias in COS G140L observations at cenwave 800 Å is a genuine, if narrow, contribution to HST/COS instrument characterization — it will matter to any other team using that specific configuration, not just to TEMPOS.

**The first four-way, same-methodology comparison of wind strength across a full order of magnitude in metallicity.** Combining this paper's own SEI measurements with the independently published Hawcroft et al. (2024) LMC/SMC sample, using the *same* wind-velocity measurement technique across all four bins, is new — and it's what actually reveals the tentative extremely-low-Z departure from the smooth trend, a result that wouldn't be visible (or trustworthy) without that methodological consistency.

**A usable empirical shortcut, quantified for the first time in this regime.** The BE-vs-SEI comparison (median 15%, or 270 km/s, offset) gives future work a concrete, quantified correction factor for using the much simpler, faster BE method as a stand-in for the more elaborate SEI fit when full atmosphere modeling isn't yet available — a small but genuinely useful calibration result in its own right.

**Predictive Content Check**

**Falsifiable handle (always):** the paper's own explicit near-term prediction is that detailed non-LTE atmosphere modeling of this same TEMPOS dataset (already announced as future work) will determine real mass-loss rates and O-star-specific abundances — and that modeling can directly confirm or refute the tentative claim made here, that wind strength falls off *more steeply* than expected below roughly 10% Z☉. If the effect survives detailed modeling, it's a genuine physical finding; if it instead traces back to the uneven spectral-type/luminosity-class coverage documented in Figure 2 (the extremely metal-poor bin, for instance, contains no early-O supergiants at all, unlike the metal-poor bin), the "break" would be explained away as a sampling artifact rather than new wind physics — a concrete, checkable fork the authors themselves have set up for their own future work to resolve.

**Formalism load (conditional):** skipped — this is fundamentally an observational measurement paper, not one that leans on new mathematical structure to generate its claims. The Gaussian line-fitting, EW integrals, and SEI opacity-law fitting are all pre-existing, standard tools (Lamers et al. 1987; Haser et al. 1995; Sundqvist et al. 2014) applied to new data, not decorative math dressing up a thin empirical claim, so there's nothing here for this half of the check to catch.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

The empirical wind velocities reported here (both BE and SEI) are explicitly acknowledged by the paper as likely overestimates of, or at best rough proxies for, the true physical terminal velocity that only full atmosphere modeling will pin down. **(A) Consensus** — this is stated directly by the authors themselves in Section 6.3.2, not an outside critique. **(paper §6.3.2)**

The spectral-type/luminosity-class coverage differs meaningfully between the extremely metal-poor and metal-poor subsamples (Figure 2 shows no early-O supergiants in the former, no late-O dwarfs in the latter), which means part of the apparent "extremely metal-poor winds fall off more steeply" result could reflect uneven parameter-space sampling between the two bins rather than a pure metallicity effect. **(B) Contested** — the paper explicitly discusses this general sampling asymmetry (Section 3.2.2) but does not itself quantify how much of the specific low-Z wind-velocity departure it could account for; a specialist could reasonably weigh this confound as either a minor caveat or a serious alternative explanation. **(analyst inference, building on paper §3.2.2 + Figure 2)**

Sample sizes within individual spectral-type/luminosity-class bins are small (as few as one or two stars in several TEMPOS bins), and even nominally similar stars show large star-to-star scatter in measured wind velocity — Figure 5 shows two O7.5 giants of similar classification with C IV profiles differing by hundreds of km/s. **(A) Consensus** — the paper's own text states plainly that "trends are weak and have large scatter, even within different metallicity categories," which is a direct acknowledgment of this statistical limitation. **(paper §6.3.1)**

The radial-velocity measurements underlying both the "runaway star" fraction and the rest-frame normalization used for every other measurement could be biased by unresolved binary companions, since orbital motion produces velocity shifts indistinguishable, from a single-epoch spectrum, from bulk systemic motion. **(B) Contested** — the paper raises this possibility explicitly (noting some TEMPOS targets already show independent evidence, such as anomalously high luminosity for their classification, suggestive of unresolved companions) but does not quantify what fraction of the 28% runaway-candidate rate this could account for. **(paper §6.1, analyst inference on the unresolved magnitude)**

The newly discovered G140L cenwave-800 wavelength-calibration systematic remains unexplained even after consultation with the HST Help Desk, and the paper explicitly recommends that this observing mode "should not be relied upon for velocity measurements" until it's better understood — an open instrumental issue for the whole HST/COS user community, not just this paper. **(A) Consensus** — stated directly and unambiguously by the authors, including the explicit recommendation against relying on this mode. **(paper §6.1)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

TEMPOS exists to solve a specific, structural problem: the massive-star models that underpin our understanding of both nearby dwarf-galaxy feedback and (by extrapolation) reionization-era galaxy evolution have essentially no direct empirical anchor below about 20% solar metallicity, because the FUV spectra that would provide that anchor were scattered across decades of unrelated HST programs with wildly inconsistent data quality. This paper is the fix: a purpose-built, 110-orbit HST Treasury program that combines new and archival COS spectroscopy of 29 O-type stars across six of the nearest very metal-poor dwarf galaxies, observed (or re-observed) to a single common standard, and released as science-ready, uniformly processed spectra.

Rather than waiting for the full detailed stellar-atmosphere modeling that will eventually deliver mass-loss rates and individual abundances (explicitly deferred to future papers), this first release reports what the raw, coadded spectra can already support without that heavier machinery: precise stellar radial velocities (which, along the way, turned up a previously unknown ~200 km/s systematic calibration bias in one specific COS observing configuration, and a surprisingly high 28% rate of likely runaway stars); ten photospheric line strengths, including two "iron forest" regions that show, for the first time in this sample, a qualitative signal of the expected metallicity split even before individual abundances exist; and two independent, model-independent-to-semi-empirical measurements of wind terminal velocity from the C IV 1548 Å line, cross-calibrated against each other and against an independently published LMC/SMC comparison sample.

That last comparison is where the paper's most scientifically interesting (if explicitly tentative) result lives: stacking TEMPOS's own two metallicity bins against the LMC and SMC gives, for the first time, a clean four-point trend spanning roughly a factor of 10 in metallicity — and the trend isn't perfectly smooth. The most metal-poor bin's winds fall off more steeply than the LMC→SMC→metal-poor progression predicts. Framed against the paper's own channel-bus picture of line-driving (Section 3), that's the kind of departure you'd expect if the "many lines summing smoothly" statistical premise itself starts to strain at sufficiently low metal abundance — though the authors are careful to present this as preliminary, flagging it explicitly as something future detailed modeling needs to confirm or refute, not a settled discovery.

I framed this summary around three parallel measurement threads (kinematics, line strengths, wind velocities) rather than following the paper's own Section 6.1/6.2/6.3 numbering literally, because that's genuinely how the content is organized and it makes the throughline — model-independent measurements as a stopgap ahead of the real atmosphere-modeling payoff — easier to see across all three at once. The key interpretive choice throughout: treating the low-Z wind-velocity departure as the paper's standout *result* while treating the G140L calibration discovery and the runaway-star fraction as valuable but genuinely secondary findings, which matches how much weight the paper's own text and structure give each of them.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the physical interpretation of *why* the extremely metal-poor sample's wind velocities fall off more steeply than the smooth multi-galaxy trend would predict. The paper itself is honestly agnostic here — it explicitly defers the explanation to future atmosphere modeling — and I've offered the channel-saturation/statistical-breakdown framing in Section 3 as a plausible physical story consistent with established line-driving theory, but that specific mechanism is my own extrapolation from CAK-theory basics, not something the paper derives or claims itself. It's equally possible, as flagged in Section 6, that the effect is substantially or entirely a spectral-type/luminosity-class sampling artifact rather than new wind physics, and the paper's own data can't yet distinguish between those two readings.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. For the first time, there's a genuinely apples-to-apples FUV spectroscopic sample of O stars below 20% solar metallicity — 29 stars, six galaxies, one common data-quality standard — replacing a scattered, incomparable patchwork that had quietly been limiting how well massive-star and reionization models could actually be tested at low metallicity.

2. Stellar winds keep getting weaker as metallicity drops, exactly as expected — but the very lowest-metallicity third of this sample shows winds fading off *faster* than the smooth trend from more metal-rich galaxies would predict, a real if still-preliminary hint that something about how radiation drives winds may qualitatively change once there are almost no metal atoms left to do the pushing.

3. Along the way, just measuring basic stellar velocities turned up two unplanned findings worth knowing about on their own: a previously unrecognized ~200 km/s calibration glitch in one specific Hubble spectrograph mode, and an unusually high (28%) rate of stars moving faster than their host galaxy's own rotation can explain — a population of likely runaways in galaxies too quiet to obviously produce that many.

## 9. Shorter Summary

TEMPOS is a new Hubble Space Telescope survey built to solve a specific gap: essentially everything known about how the winds and radiation of the hottest, most massive stars depend on chemical composition (metallicity) comes from stars far more metal-rich than the small, primitive dwarf galaxies that likely reionized the early universe. Below about 20% of the Sun's metal content, existing ultraviolet spectra of these stars are scattered across old, mismatched observing programs that can't be reliably compared to each other. TEMPOS fixes this by observing, or re-observing, 29 hot O-type stars across six nearby, very metal-poor dwarf galaxies to one consistent standard.

This first paper describes the survey design and reports the earliest results the raw data can support, before the heavier modeling work that comes later. The team measured each star's velocity (uncovering, in the process, a previously unknown calibration glitch in one Hubble instrument mode, and finding that an unusually large fraction of these stars — 28% — appear to be moving faster than their galaxy's rotation can explain, a possible sign of stellar ejection events). They also measured the strength of several absorption features, including crowded clusters of iron lines that give an early, rough sense of how much iron is present even before detailed modeling exists. Most importantly, they estimated how fast each star's wind blows outward, two different ways, and compared those speeds to published measurements from more metal-rich galaxies (the Magellanic Clouds).

The result: wind speeds decline with decreasing metallicity as theory predicts, but the very poorest galaxies in this sample show winds fading faster than the overall trend would suggest — possibly hinting that the physics driving these winds behaves differently once metals become extremely scarce. The team is careful to call this preliminary, pending more detailed follow-up modeling already planned. Either way, the survey itself — a clean, uniform dataset spanning a metallicity range no comparable sample has covered before — is likely to become a standard reference point for testing how massive stars, and the galaxies they help shape, behave under the most metal-poor conditions this side of the early universe.
