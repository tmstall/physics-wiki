## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Freezing the Quantum Fog: Turning Near-Exact Wavefunctions Back Into Dots You Can Draw Bonds Between**

A new sampling trick lets physicists take the most accurate quantum wavefunctions computed to date and squeeze them back down into old-fashioned Lewis-structure pictures — settling a 160-year debate about benzene along the way and revealing that graphene's textbook "mixed bond" cartoon has been wrong all along.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Chemistry and condensed-matter physics have long lived with two competing pictures of how electrons sit inside molecules and solids. The particle view — treating electrons as classical, localized point charges — is what most chemists actually draw and think with day to day: Lewis structures, valence bond (VB) theory, resonance structures, the whole vocabulary of "shared electron pairs" that makes a chemical bond intuitive. The wave function view, which took over as the fundamental theoretical picture after Schrödinger's equation in the 1920s, is exact but abstract: the wavefunction of an N-electron system lives in a space with 3N dimensions, and while it technically contains everything there is to know about the system, no human can look at it directly and see a bond.

Modern computational chemistry has mostly chased the wave function view harder and harder, using methods like density functional theory and post-Hartree-Fock approaches — but these all rely on approximations that get worse as systems get bigger, because the space they're searching over grows exponentially with electron count. The newest and most powerful approach doesn't fight that curse with cleverer approximations, but with brute representational power: neural-network-based quantum Monte Carlo methods (using architectures like FermiNet for molecules and DeepSolid for periodic solids) represent the many-electron wavefunction directly, in real space, using a deep neural network trained to minimize the system's energy. These methods now reach near-exact ground states for molecules and solids with over 100 electrons, sidestepping the basis-set truncation errors that plague older methods entirely.

But this power comes with a cost: the resulting wavefunctions are optimized as a black box (making it hard to see what to improve), and even once you have one, it's still a hopelessly high-dimensional object that resists direct human interpretation. This paper's move is to take a technique developed by a different group for isolated molecules — dynamic Voronoi Metropolis sampling (DVMS), which extracts classical, particle-like electron positions from a quantum wavefunction — and extend it, for the first time, to periodic solids. They then use this extended method, "periodic dynamic Voronoi Metropolis sampling" (PDVMS), to settle a genuinely old chemistry debate about benzene and to determine graphene's actual valence-bond structure for the first time.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is a methodological/computational physics paper — introducing a new technique (PDVMS) and applying it to resolve two separate open structural questions: benzene's long-debated bond-order structure, and graphene's never-rigorously-established valence-bond picture, with the graphene result offering a new, unified real-space explanation for several previously-known but separately-explained experimental magnetism observations.

**Prior Belief Check:** For benzene, the qualitative answer isn't new — a 2020 study using a lower-accuracy method (CAS-CI) had already found the "staggered" (Linnett) structure was mildly favored over the classic Kekulé alternating-bond picture, at 53%. This paper's contribution is a far more precise, near-exact confirmation of that same qualitative picture, landing at a strikingly closer-to-50/50 split (50.18%) than the earlier estimate — a genuine quantitative refinement, not a new discovery, and mildly surprising in how close to an even split the more accurate calculation lands. For graphene, the claim is genuinely new and would likely surprise most condensed-matter physicists and chemists: the commonly assumed "mixed single-double bond network" extension of benzene's Kekulé picture to graphene has apparently never been rigorously established, and this paper's identification of a spin-staggered structure instead (rather than a bond-order pattern) is a fresh, non-obvious claim about a famous, heavily-studied material.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is a single-group result (Peking University, with ByteDance Seed as a computational collaborator). The benzene portion is a partial independent convergence point with prior literature: a different, unaffiliated group's 2020 CAS-CI calculation and this paper's near-exact neural-network calculation now agree qualitatively (and are numerically close: 53% vs. 50.18%) on the staggered preference, which is a real form of methodological cross-validation. The graphene portion is entirely new and single-group, with no independently-computed comparably-accurate cross-check of the specific valence-bond structure — though its predicted physical consequences (hydrogenation-induced magnetism, edge magnetism) are checked against, and found consistent with, experimental measurements from separate, independent research groups (refs. 47, 48), which is real but indirect corroboration of the underlying picture's implications rather than of the structure itself.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Concept: Why you can't just average electron positions.**

Same-spin electrons are quantum-mechanically indistinguishable — the wavefunction's probability density is exactly unchanged if you swap the coordinates of any two same-spin electrons. If you naively try to compute "the average position of electron #3," you'd actually be averaging over every possible relabeling of which electron is "#3," and all the same-spin electrons blur into the same meaningless central point. It's like trying to compute the average position of "the second red token" in a data structure where the tokens are genuinely unlabeled and interchangeable — the question doesn't even parse until you first pick a consistent way to assign roles.

**Breaks when:** you push it to imply the electrons are secretly labeled all along and physics just hides the label from us. They're not — the indistinguishability is fundamental, not a bookkeeping inconvenience, which is exactly why the fix (below) has to work *with* the symmetry rather than pretend it away.

**Concept: Voronoi tiling as a "role assignment" trick.**

The fix (DVMS, from earlier work this paper builds on) is to partition the full configuration space into regions — one per possible consistent assignment of "which physical electron plays which role" — using a Voronoi diagram: each region contains all configurations closer (by a suitable distance) to one particular reference arrangement than to any of its electron-swapped equivalents. Within one such region, the electrons are effectively distinguishable, so the average position calculated there is meaningful. The reference arrangement itself is found by iteration: guess a reference, compute the region's actual average position, use that as the new reference, and repeat until it stops changing — a fixed-point loop very much like running k-means clustering to convergence, or an expectation-maximization step, just for one cluster.

**Breaks when:** you assume this uniquely and objectively identifies "the" true electron positions. It picks out one self-consistent classical snapshot per region, and — as the benzene results below show explicitly — there can be multiple equally valid regions the true quantum state resonates among, so no single snapshot is "the" answer on its own.

**Concept: Periodic (wrap-around) distance.**

In an infinite repeating crystal, "closest point" stops being well-defined in the ordinary sense, because every point has infinitely many periodic copies. The fix used throughout computational physics — and central to extending this method to solids — is the minimum-image convention: measure the distance between two points as the smallest distance across *any* combination of lattice translations, not just the raw straight-line distance. This is exactly the logic behind wrap-around (modular) addressing in a circular buffer or a hash ring: position 359 and position 1 on a 360-slot ring are genuinely only 2 slots apart, even though their raw numeric difference is 358.

**Breaks when:** taken to mean the crystal is literally curved or finite. It's a bookkeeping device for representing an infinite, exactly-repeating lattice using just one finite unit cell — the "wrap" is mathematical, not a claim about the material's real-space geometry.

**Concept: Why naive averaging fails on wrapped coordinates (and the circular-mean fix).**

This is the paper's second periodic-systems innovation, and it has an exact, classic analogue: averaging clock times or compass headings the naive way. If two data points sit at 350° and 10° around a circle, a plain arithmetic average gives 180° — the *opposite* side of the circle from where the points actually cluster (they're really only 20° apart, straddling the 0°/360° wrap point). The fix is to convert each angle to a point on the unit circle, average those points as vectors, and convert the resulting vector back to an angle — a "circular mean" instead of an arithmetic one. The paper's actual method (mapping the periodic cell to a rectangle, then wrapping it into a cylinder to compute the center of mass) is a geometrically dressed-up version of exactly this same trick, generalized to a full unit cell rather than a single circle.

**Breaks when:** you assume this is just a computational nicety with a small effect. Fig. 2(b) shows the naive answer isn't slightly off — the wrong ("red cross") and correct ("red star") centers-of-mass can land on visibly different sides of the cell entirely, which is exactly the 350°/10° → 180°-vs-0° failure mode above.

**Concept: Neural-network wavefunctions as a self-generating training loop.**

FermiNet and DeepSolid represent the many-electron wavefunction directly as a deep neural network (built from sums of Slater determinants, to respect the antisymmetry required of fermions), trained via variational Monte Carlo: the network's own current probability distribution is used to generate the Monte Carlo samples it's trained on, and the training objective is the quantum-mechanical energy expectation value rather than a labeled dataset. It's structurally similar to an on-policy reinforcement-learning loop or self-play training — the model generates its own training distribution from its current state, then updates itself to do better on samples drawn from that same distribution, round after round until convergence.

**Breaks when:** pushed toward the idea that this is somehow easier or less rigorous than traditional quantum chemistry. It's a different, higher-capacity way to search the exact same variational energy landscape traditional methods approximate with restricted basis sets — the "self-training" framing describes the optimization procedure, not a shortcut around the underlying physics.

> **Central analogy for this paper:** freezing a quantum probability cloud into classical dots.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**Framework and molecular baseline.** The neural-network wavefunction (FermiNet for benzene, DeepSolid for graphene) is trained via variational Monte Carlo to approximate the true ground-state wavefunction directly in real space; for periodic solids, periodicity is built into the network from the start by feeding it periodic electron-nucleus and electron-electron distances rather than raw Euclidean ones. Once a near-exact wavefunction is in hand, DVMS/PDVMS is applied as a separate, downstream analysis step: it doesn't change the physics, it extracts a classical reading from an already-computed quantum answer.

**The two new pieces needed for periodic systems.** Extending DVMS (originally built for isolated molecules) to periodic solids required two specific fixes, both described in the background section above and both essential — without either, the method breaks down for anything periodic. First, the Voronoi tessellation itself needs the periodic (minimum-image) distance rather than ordinary distance, since a naive Voronoi cell in a periodic system doesn't stay in one connected piece — it fractures into infinitely many disconnected copies related by lattice translations, and the analysis has to recognize these as the same cell rather than as separate regions.

**Worked example — why plain distance breaks in a periodic system.** The periodic distance metric used throughout (Eq. 1 in the paper) is:

l̃(p,q) = min over (n₁,n₂) of |p − q + n₁a₁ + n₂a₂|

**Symbol definitions:**
p, q : two positions being compared (e.g., an electron coordinate and a candidate reference site)
a₁, a₂ : the lattice vectors defining the repeating unit cell
n₁, n₂ : any integers — indexing every possible periodic copy of the cell

**What this actually means:** Take a simplified 1D stand-in: a unit cell of length 10, with a reference site at position 0.5 and an electron sampled at position 9.7. The ordinary (non-periodic) distance between them is |9.7 − 0.5| = 9.2 — nearly the whole width of the cell. But the electron at 9.7 is really just 0.8 away from the reference site's *next-door periodic copy* at position 10.5 (i.e., 0.5 shifted by one lattice vector of length 10): |9.7 − 10.5| = 0.8. The minimum-image rule automatically picks the smaller number, 0.8, as the true separation — correctly recognizing that the electron is sitting right next to the reference site, just on the "other side" of where the unit cell happens to be drawn. Get this wrong, and the whole downstream Voronoi tiling misassigns electrons to the wrong region, corrupting everything built on top of it — exactly the kind of off-by-a-wraparound bug familiar from any code that handles circular or modular indexing incorrectly.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** The reader might assume the "particle view" positions this method produces are literal claims about where electrons really sit, in tension with quantum uncertainty. They're not — each position is a position *expectation value* within one self-consistently-defined symmetry region of an inherently delocalized probability distribution, and (as the benzene results below show explicitly) the true state is understood as a resonance among several such classical-looking snapshots, not a single fixed answer.

> **Watch:** The reader might assume graphene's spin-staggered magnetism is a new experimental discovery reported here. It is not — the magnetism phenomena this structure explains (hydrogen-adsorption-induced local moments, zigzag-edge magnetism) were already measured in prior, independent experiments (refs. 47, 48). What's new is a specific, unified real-space mechanism that explains why those phenomena happen, not the observation that they happen.

> **Watch:** The reader might assume graphene's result carries the same head-to-head quantitative certainty as benzene's 50.18% number. For benzene, the paper computes an explicit, precisely-bounded preference between two named competing structures. For graphene, the evidence is convergence of the simulation to a single dominant structure (robust to how it's initialized) plus consistency with known experimental consequences — a different, less numerically pointed kind of evidence than benzene's direct percentage comparison.

> **Watch:** The reader might assume the choice of periodic unit cell size is a computational detail that doesn't affect the physics. The paper explicitly shows the opposite: using graphene's smaller, conventional 2-atom unit cell (standard in ordinary band-structure calculations) mathematically forecloses the symmetry-breaking pattern the true spin-staggered ground state needs, forcing the simulation to converge to the wrong, higher-energy Kekulé-like answer instead. The 6-atom unit cell used here isn't a neutral computational choice — it's a precondition for finding the right answer at all.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Two things stand out as genuinely new. First, the two specific mathematical fixes that make particlization work on a periodic lattice at all — the minimum-image Voronoi metric and the wrap-onto-a-cylinder center-of-mass calculation — are a clean, complete solution to a real problem (naive periodic averaging silently gives origin-dependent wrong answers) rather than an approximation or a workaround. Second, and arguably the more chemically interesting move, is discovering that the structural-preference metric doubles as a diagnostic for the neural network's own internal machinery: by tracking which of the 16 Slater determinants in benzene's FermiNet wavefunction favor which structure, the authors show that only 3 determinants meaningfully contribute, that individual determinants are systematically biased toward one structure or the other, and that combining determinants with *opposite* biases (rather than similar ones) is specifically what improves both the energy and the structural accuracy together. That's a genuinely new use for a particle-view technique — not just understanding the target molecule, but reverse-engineering how the neural ansatz itself represents the answer.

**Predictive Content Check**

*Falsifiable handle (always):* The benzene result gives a precise, directly checkable number — κ = 50.18% ± 0.02% — computed from a near-exact wavefunction using a specific, reproducible method. Anyone with an equally accurate independent wavefunction for benzene could recompute this and check for agreement; disagreement would be diagnostic of either the wavefunction's accuracy or the DVMS procedure itself. The graphene result is different in character and should be named plainly: it's primarily *explanatory* rather than predictive of new, not-yet-observed phenomena — it retrodicts and unifies already-known experimental magnetism observations (H-adsorption, nanoribbon edges) under one mechanism, rather than forecasting a new effect nobody has seen yet. The specific claim that could, in principle, be tested directly — that pristine, unperturbed graphene's ground state has this particular spin-staggered real-space structure — isn't paired here with a proposed direct experimental test of the unperturbed bulk material itself.

*Formalism load (conditional — fires here, given how much of the paper's contribution is the periodic-geometry mathematics):* The formalism is doing real, load-bearing work, not decoration. The periodic distance metric and the cylindrical center-of-mass construction aren't elegant restatements of something already obviously true — Fig. 2(b) shows the naive alternative gives a visibly, qualitatively wrong answer (the "red cross" sitting on the wrong side of the cell from the "red star"), and the explicit unit-cell-size result (2-atom cell forces the wrong Kekulé-like answer; 6-atom cell recovers the correct spin-staggered one) shows the geometric setup directly determines which physical answer the method can even reach. This is math generating the actual result, not math dressing one up.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> Benzene's reported structural preference, 50.18% ± 0.02%, is extremely close to an even 50/50 split. While the authors frame this as "qualitatively confirming" a staggered preference, the practical chemical significance of a 0.18-percentage-point margin is a separate question from its statistical precision. **(B) Contested** — reasonable chemists could disagree about whether this represents a meaningful structural preference versus a system that should be described as essentially perfectly balanced between the two resonance forms for most practical purposes; the authors themselves flag the number as "only marginally above 50%." **(paper § II.B)**

> No independent experimental confirmation exists of graphene's pristine, unperturbed spin-staggered ground-state structure itself — the corroborating experimental evidence cited confirms *consequences* of the picture (adsorption- and edge-induced magnetism), not the bulk structure directly. **(A) Consensus** — this is simply the actual evidentiary structure the paper presents; the authors describe the experiments as things the structure "helps explain," not as direct structural confirmations. **(paper § II.C)**

> Both applications rest on treating FermiNet and DeepSolid wavefunctions as "near-exact," a strong claim well-supported by these architectures' broader benchmark record but not independently re-verified within this paper against an alternative high-accuracy method specifically for graphene (unlike benzene, which is cross-checked against an independent CAS-CI calculation). **(B) Contested** — periodic solids are generally harder to converge to near-exactness than small molecules, so some computational chemists might want a comparable independent cross-check before treating the graphene wavefunction's accuracy as equally well-established as benzene's. **(analyst inference)**

> The "resonance among configurations" interpretation (four sites for benzene, an analogous multi-site picture implied for graphene) is a physically reasonable reading the authors apply on top of the raw convergence results, using standard valence-bond resonance concepts — the method itself outputs converged classical snapshots and symmetry relationships between them, not an automatic "this is a resonance" verdict. **(C) Speculative** — this is my own observation about how much interpretive framing versus raw computational output is doing the explanatory work, not a self-criticism the paper raises. **(analyst inference)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

This paper develops periodic dynamic Voronoi Metropolis sampling (PDVMS), extending an existing molecular technique (DVMS) so that it can extract classical, chemist-readable electron positions from near-exact neural-network wavefunctions of periodic solids, not just isolated molecules. The extension requires two specific fixes to handle periodicity correctly: a minimum-image distance metric for defining the Voronoi tessellation that assigns meaning to "electron position" despite quantum indistinguishability, and a cylindrical-wrapping method for computing a center of mass that doesn't depend on an arbitrary choice of coordinate origin. Applied first to benzene as a validation case, the method precisely quantifies the long-debated preference between the classic Kekulé alternating-bond structure and Linnett's 1960s spin-staggered alternative, finding a near-exact preference of 50.18% ± 0.02% for the staggered structure — closely matching, and substantially sharpening, an earlier lower-accuracy estimate. The same technique doubles as a window into the neural network's own internal representation, showing that only 3 of 16 determinants in the wavefunction meaningfully contribute, and that combining determinants with opposing structural biases (rather than similar ones) is specifically what yields both accurate energies and accurate structural preferences. Applied for the first time to a periodic solid, graphene, the method reveals that its true valence-bond ground state is a spin-staggered structure analogous to benzene's Linnett picture — each carbon-carbon bond a shared electron pair, plus one unpaired, alternating-spin electron per carbon atom — rather than the commonly assumed "mixed single-double bond" extension of Kekulé's benzene structure. This spin-staggered picture offers a unified, real-space explanation for several previously separately-explained experimental observations of graphene's emergent magnetism under hydrogenation and at nanoribbon edges.

The key interpretive choice in this summary is treating the benzene and graphene results as doing different evidentiary work: benzene is presented as validation (does the new near-exact method reproduce and sharpen an already largely-accepted qualitative picture?), while graphene is presented as genuine discovery (what actually is the valence-bond structure of a real, heavily-studied solid, when nobody had rigorously worked it out before?). The reader should take away that the method itself — cleanly and rigorously handling electron indistinguishability and periodicity together — is the paper's most durable contribution, since it's directly reusable for any other periodic material's electronic structure going forward, independent of whether the specific graphene finding holds up to further scrutiny.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the exact relationship between the paper's actual periodic center-of-mass algorithm (mapping to a rectangle, then wrapping into two cylinders) and the simplified circular-mean illustration I used in Section 3 is one I've asserted as mathematically equivalent in spirit rather than verified line-by-line against their cited reference [35] — the general principle (naive Cartesian averaging fails on wrapped coordinates; some form of circular/angular averaging fixes it) is solid and well-established, but the paper's specific implementation details (handling two dimensions simultaneously via nested cylindrical wrapping) go beyond what I've independently reconstructed from the text and figure alone.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. The most accurate quantum-mechanical calculations of molecules and solids today come from neural networks that represent the entire multi-electron wavefunction directly — but these wavefunctions are so high-dimensional that even after computing one exactly, nobody can look at it and see a chemical bond. This paper builds a bridge back to the intuitive picture chemists actually think in, by extracting classical, dot-like electron positions from the quantum fog.

2. Using this bridge on benzene, a structure debated since Kekulé's 1865 alternating-bond ring, the near-exact calculation lands almost exactly at a 50/50 balance (50.18%) between the classic Kekulé structure and a lesser-known 1960s alternative — confirming, with far more precision than ever before, that benzene's true ground state is a nearly perfectly poised resonance between the two.

3. Applying the same method to graphene for the first time reveals that its real electronic structure isn't the "mixed single-double bond" cartoon commonly assumed by analogy to benzene — it's a spin-staggered pattern, with one unpaired electron sitting above or below each carbon atom. This single real-space picture explains, in one unified mechanism, several previously separate experimental observations of graphene developing magnetism when chemically modified or cut into narrow ribbons.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Chemists have always pictured molecules two ways: as clouds described by quantum wavefunctions (the modern, exact but abstract picture), and as diagrams of dots and bonds you can actually draw (the older, intuitive picture chemists still use every day). This paper builds a rigorous bridge between the two, using the most accurate quantum calculations available — neural-network-based methods that can compute near-exact wavefunctions for molecules and solids with over 100 electrons — and a clever sampling technique that extracts classical, dot-like electron positions back out of those wavefunctions.

The technique itself required solving two tricky problems to work on solids rather than just isolated molecules: how to measure "distance" correctly in an infinitely repeating crystal (the same kind of wrap-around logic used in circular buffers or clock arithmetic), and how to compute a meaningful average position when there's no natural starting point to measure from (solved with a trick mathematically similar to correctly averaging compass headings, rather than naively averaging the numbers and getting a nonsensical answer on the wrong side of the circle).

Applied first to benzene — debated since 1865 — the method precisely confirms that benzene's true structure sits almost exactly halfway between two competing classical pictures (50.18% in favor of the less commonly taught one), settling the question with far more precision than previous calculations managed. Applied next to graphene, the same method reveals something new: contrary to the commonly assumed textbook picture (a simple extension of benzene's alternating-bond structure), graphene's real ground state has each carbon atom carrying one unpaired electron, arranged so their spins alternate across the lattice. This single, previously-unrecognized structural feature turns out to explain, in one stroke, several experimentally observed but previously separately-understood magnetic behaviors of graphene — why sticking a single hydrogen atom onto it creates a tiny local magnet, and why cutting it into ribbons with different edge shapes produces magnetic edges in some cases but not others.

Nothing here overturns established quantum mechanics — the underlying wavefunctions are computed by completely standard, previously validated methods. What's new is the lens: a rigorous way to translate the exact but unreadable quantum answer back into the simple particle picture chemists have relied on for over a century, applied here to solve one old puzzle and open up a new, previously unrecognized one.

## Appendix — Deep-Dive Discussion Log (2026-08-26)

**Q: What does the Assumption Audit line about "position expectation value within one self-consistently-defined symmetry region... resonance among several classical-looking snapshots" actually mean?**

Unpacked layer by layer. The underlying |Ψ|² is a genuinely delocalized probability distribution over the full 3N-dimensional configuration space — not peaked on one classical arrangement. A "self-consistently-defined symmetry region" is one Voronoi cell from the DVMS fixed-point procedure: guess a reference arrangement, keep only the sampled configurations closer to it than to any symmetry-equivalent relabeling, recompute the average, iterate to convergence. Inside one such region the labeling ambiguity is resolved, so "position expectation value" is the average electron position computed conditional on being in that region — not a literal instantaneous location. Crucially, the full wavefunction's probability mass isn't confined to one region; it's spread across several (e.g., a Kekulé-type region and a Linnett-type region for benzene), and the reported percentages (50.18%/49.82%) are literally the fraction of probability mass — operationally, the fraction of Monte Carlo samples — landing in each region. That's what "resonance among snapshots" cashes out to quantitatively: not a metaphor layered on top, but a direct weighted-mixture readout, in contrast to traditional valence-bond theory's qualitative resonance language.

**Q: What are "symmetry-equivalent relabelings"?**

Because same-spin electrons are indistinguishable, swapping which electron index is assigned to which position leaves |Ψ|² exactly unchanged. For N same-spin electrons there are up to N! such relabelings of one reference geometry — physically identical situations, just different index-to-position bookkeeping. The Voronoi tessellation is built directly on this: one cell per relabeling, and a raw Monte Carlo sample is sorted into whichever relabeled copy of the reference it sits nearest to. This was explicitly distinguished from a second, separate layer of symmetry in the paper — point-group/structural symmetry (e.g., rotating benzene's ring to turn one Kekulé structure into another), which is a different equivalence applied afterward when the authors decide which distinct classical-looking snapshots to pool into one reported percentage.

**Q: Concrete worked example of how cell-sorting resolves the labeling ambiguity.**

Toy 2-electron, 1D case: reference sites at positions 2 and 8. Raw QMC samples arrive with labels that flip arbitrarily between the two sites from sample to sample (e.g., (2.1, 7.8), (7.9, 2.2), (1.9, 8.1), (8.3, 1.8) for (electron-1, electron-2)) because the sampler has no fixed preference under the exchange symmetry. Naively averaging electron-1's raw position across these gives ≈5.05 — a meaningless blur at the midpoint, the same failure mode as the compass-heading example. Sorting each sample into whichever of the two possible labelings (index-1-near-2-vs-index-2-near-8, or the swap) it's closer to, then pooling the "near-2" values across both sorted groups and the "near-8" values separately, recovers sharp averages of ≈2.0 and ≈8.0 — the two clean dots the method is designed to produce.

**Q: What is a Slater determinant, as "the standard fermionic-antisymmetry-respecting building block"?**

Fermionic antisymmetry requires the wavefunction itself (not just |Ψ|²) to flip sign under exchange of two identical fermions: Ψ(...,i,...,j,...) = −Ψ(...,j,...,i,...); squaring removes the sign, which is why |Ψ|² came out invariant in the earlier discussion — that invariance is downstream of this stronger requirement. A Slater determinant guarantees the sign flip automatically by construction: for two electrons and orbitals φ₁, φ₂, Ψ(r₁,r₂) = (1/√2)[φ₁(r₁)φ₂(r₂) − φ₁(r₂)φ₂(r₁)] is literally a 2×2 determinant, and swapping r₁↔r₂ flips its sign exactly because swapping two rows/columns of a determinant always flips sign. It also encodes Pauli exclusion for free: if φ₁ = φ₂ the determinant is identically zero. This construction (Slater, 1929) underlies essentially all quantum-chemistry methods; FermiNet's variant is that the one-electron orbitals φ are flexible neural-network-parameterized functions rather than fixed atomic-orbital basis functions, and the full wavefunction is a weighted sum of several such determinants (16, for this benzene calculation) rather than just one.

**Q: Unpack the determinant-bias finding in Section 5 — reverse-engineering the neural ansatz.**

The same DVMS classical-snapshot machinery used on the full wavefunction can also be run on individual Slater determinants in isolation, asking "which structure would this one determinant favor if it were the whole wavefunction?" Doing this for all 16 determinants in benzene's FermiNet expansion reveals: (1) only 3 carry meaningful combination weight — most of the architecture's nominal capacity goes unused, a sparsity finding about the network's learned representation, independent of benzene's chemistry; (2) those individual determinants are each strongly biased toward one structure (Kekulé-type or Linnett-type), not near-50/50 the way the full combination is; (3) it's specifically pairing determinants with opposite structural bias — not similar ones — that improves both the energy and the structural accuracy together. Offered one added, non-paper analogy for intuition: this resembles why diverse ensembles (e.g., in ensemble learning) often outperform ensembles of near-identical members — accuracy gains come from combining members that disagree in complementary ways, not from combining members that already agree. Flagged that the analysis document doesn't spell out the precise mechanics of how one determinant's isolated contribution is fed through the Voronoi classification — that level of methodological detail would require checking the paper's methods section directly.
