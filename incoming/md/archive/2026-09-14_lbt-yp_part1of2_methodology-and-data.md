## 1. Punchy Title & Hook

**Building the Ruler Before Taking the Measurement**

Before anyone gets to announce a new primordial helium abundance, three papers' worth of unglamorous work has to happen first: pick the right galaxies, calibrate the telescope to fractions of a percent, and — critically — find a way to break a decades-old measurement degeneracy that has quietly capped the precision of every helium-based cosmology result before this one.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

**Paper Type & Stakes:** These are observational/methods papers — no new theory, no claimed discovery. They're infrastructure: sample selection, instrument calibration, and a new physical-conditions/abundance pipeline, all in service of a single number (Yₚ, the primordial ⁴He mass fraction) that gets reported two papers later.

Here's why that number matters. Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN) predicts how much hydrogen fused into helium in the first few minutes after the Big Bang, and that prediction depends sensitively on how many "kinds" of light, weakly-interacting particles were around at the time — conventionally parameterized as Nᵥ (or, more generally, Nₑff), the effective number of neutrino species. The Standard Model says Nᵥ = 3. If Yₚ is measured precisely enough, comparing it to the BBN prediction becomes a genuine test of physics beyond the Standard Model — a decoupling-era particle census taken from starlight. The problem has never been telescope time; it's been that Yₚ has to be pinned down to a fraction of a percent for the Nᵥ constraint to be worth anything, and getting there requires eliminating every systematic in a genuinely hard measurement chain. The stated goal of this project is to cut Yₚ's uncertainty from the best prior value of 1.3% down to ~0.5% — which propagates to cutting the implied uncertainty on Nᵥ from ±0.14 to ±0.08.

**Prior Belief Check:** Nothing here contradicts the standard cosmological picture — if anything, that's the point. This is a precision-engineering paper, not a paradigm-challenge paper. The mainstream expectation, stated explicitly in Paper I, is that Nᵥ should land at or very near 3.044 (the Standard Model value, slightly above 3 due to a residual heating effect from e⁺e⁻ annihilation). A surprise would be a Yₚ precise enough to statistically exclude 3.044 — that's the payoff paper (Paper V) still to come. What Papers I–III do is entirely un-surprising by design: they're supposed to be boring, reliable, and defensible, because the exciting part of this project depends on nobody being able to poke a hole in the plumbing.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is not a lone-wolf result. The paper explicitly benchmarks against roughly a dozen independent groups working the same problem with overlapping but distinct samples and methods — Izotov & collaborators (multiple papers, 2007–2021), Aver et al.'s own earlier work, Fernández et al. (2018, 2019), Hsyu et al. (2020), Kurichin & Ivanchik (2021, 2024, 2026), Matsumoto et al. (2022), and the very recent Yanagisawa et al. (2026) EMPRESS survey. All cluster in the Yₚ ≈ 0.24–0.26 range, broadly consistent with each other and with Planck-based BBN predictions — but with real, group-to-group scatter (0.2312 to 0.2551 across the cited studies) that traces back to methodological choices, not to independent physical measurements of different objects. That's an important distinction: this is a field with real convergence at the "helium is about a quarter of the universe's baryonic mass" level, but genuine, unresolved disagreement at the level of precision that actually matters for the Nᵥ test — which is exactly the gap this project is trying to close.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Concept 1 — How you "measure" a number from 13.8 billion years ago.** You don't observe the early universe's helium directly. Instead, you find present-day galaxies so metal-poor they're close to chemically pristine — extremely metal-poor (XMP) HII regions, star-forming clouds of gas around hot young stars — measure their helium-to-hydrogen ratio (Y) and their oxygen-to-hydrogen ratio (a proxy for how much stellar processing/"pollution" has happened since the Big Bang), and then extrapolate the Y-vs-O relationship back to zero oxygen. Where that line hits the y-axis is your primordial value.

**Analogy:** This is exactly a calibration-curve extrapolation, the kind you'd run to characterize a memory subsystem's *unloaded* latency. You can't measure latency with zero traffic on the bus — there's always some baseline contention — so you measure latency under a range of light loads and extrapolate the trend back to zero load. The lowest-load (lowest-metallicity) data points are disproportionately valuable because they sit closest to the number you actually want and require the least extrapolation.

**Breaks when:** you push this to ask whether the *relationship itself* is trustworthy near zero. The latency-vs-load curve is usually close to linear near the origin for good physical reasons (queueing theory). The Y-vs-O relationship is *assumed* linear by convention, but nothing guarantees it — helium and oxygen come from different stellar nucleosynthesis channels, so the "ΔY/ΔO slope" could in principle curve. This is a genuine, acknowledged weak point of the whole method (see Assumption Audit below), not just an analogy quibble.

**Concept 2 — Why this has historically been so hard: the temperature-density degeneracy.** To turn a measured emission-line brightness into a helium abundance, you need to know the temperature (Tₑ) and density (nₑ) of the gas that produced the line, because both affect how efficiently different transitions radiate. The problem: using only the standard optical helium lines, Tₑ and nₑ are degenerate — many different (Tₑ, nₑ) pairs can reproduce the same observed line ratios almost equally well. You have one blurred signal and two unknowns feeding it.

**Analogy:** This is the classic underdetermined-system problem from performance debugging — you see one output metric (say, elevated tail latency) that could be explained by either cache contention or memory-bandwidth saturation, and a single aggregate counter can't tell you which. You don't get to just stare harder at the one signal; you need an *independent* probe — a counter that's sensitive to one cause but not the other — to break the tie.

**Breaks when:** in a computer system, you can usually *instrument* your way out of a degeneracy by adding a new counter almost for free. In this measurement, the "extra counter" is a specific, physically rare spectral line that has to be hunted down with real telescope time, on real, often very faint galaxies — that's the entire subject of Paper III.

**Concept 3 — The instrument: He I λ10830.** There's one helium emission line, at 10830 Å (near-infrared), that comes from a metastable atomic level — an excited electron state that doesn't decay quickly and instead sits around long enough to get bumped further by electron collisions. Because that "bump rate" depends directly on electron density, this line's strength is unusually density-sensitive compared with the standard optical helium lines. Measuring it gives you the independent density constraint that breaks the Tₑ–nₑ degeneracy.

**Analogy:** think of the metastable level as a slow-draining holding queue rather than a fast pass-through. Most helium transitions are like a queue with near-zero dwell time — throughput tells you almost nothing about arrival rate, because nothing backs up. The metastable level dwells long enough that its *population*, and hence how much of it gets kicked further by collisions, scales with how "busy" the surrounding gas is (its electron density) — exactly the way a queue's backlog becomes a legible proxy for arrival rate only once dwell time is long enough for backlog to actually form.

**Breaks when:** you push it to ask about calibration transfer. A queue-depth counter is trivially precise; this line's *calibration* depends on getting its atomic emissivity tables right (a nontrivial, actively-revised piece of atomic physics — Del Zanna & Storey 2022 is cited as a meaningful recent update), and the raw measurement is hard for an unrelated reason: it sits in the near-infrared, where Earth's atmosphere is a mess of water and oxygen absorption bands that have to be modeled and removed (telluric correction, discussed at length in Paper III).

**Central analogy for this paper:** *An independent diagnostic probe breaking a two-variable degeneracy.*

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**Sample philosophy (Paper I §2).** The team explicitly optimizes for *object quality* over *sample size*, which is a deliberate, argued-for choice rather than a default. They prioritize the lowest-metallicity targets available (to minimize dependence on the assumed-linear Y-vs-O extrapolation), and impose hard observational cuts — Hβ equivalent width ≥ 100 Å, sufficient line flux — chosen to guarantee usable, low-uncertainty measurements rather than to maximize the raw target count. They cite Ferland et al. (2010) directly for the argument that piling on large numbers of lower-quality points, while it shrinks formal statistical error bars, doesn't actually fix the thing that's currently limiting the field: systematic floors, not sampling noise. The final combined sample spans 62 galaxies observed with the optical instrument (MODS) and 48–49 with the near-IR instrument (LUCI); a low-metallicity subsample of 15 objects ends up doing the actual Yₚ-determination heavy lifting in Paper IV.

**Instrumentation and calibration discipline (Paper I §3).** Optical spectroscopy comes from LBT/MODS (two twin spectrographs, blue+red channels, R ~ 1850–2300, full 3300–10000 Å coverage). The team tracked MODS's spectral-response calibration using HST flux-standard stars (G191B2b, GD153) continuously across the entire 2021–2024, 3-year observing campaign — catching and explaining real instrumental drift (mirror recoating cycles, a "blue haze" contamination event on one secondary mirror) rather than assuming a static calibration. The resulting flux-calibration uncertainty is ~0.5–0.6% over most of the optical range. This is the unglamorous but load-bearing part: a 0.5% calibration floor is a precondition for a 0.5% final Yₚ uncertainty — you cannot out-analyze a miscalibrated instrument.

**New emission-line-fitting methodology (Paper II §2.2) — a genuine methods contribution.** Standard practice fits emission lines with Gaussian profiles. The team found MODS line profiles are measurably non-Gaussian (the slit itself imposes a boxcar-like instrumental profile), which biased fits to blended or faint lines — normalized residuals exceeding 20% in cases like the blended [OII]λλ3726,29 doublet. They switch to a tunable-shape "super-Gaussian" profile (a Gaussian generalized with a shape-power parameter P, reducing to a standard Gaussian at P=2 but flat-topped for P>2), which cut residuals below 10% and, combined with an explicit two-component broad-wing fit, cleanly separated a small (1–3% of flux) broad emission component — possibly high-velocity outflow gas — from the narrow nebular lines that actually matter for the abundance analysis.

**Physical-conditions determination (Paper II §3–4).** The team adopts the standard three-zone ionization model (low/intermediate/high, corresponding to which ions are present given their ionization potential) and measures temperature and density from multiple independent line-ratio diagnostics per zone — [NII], [OII], [SII] for the low zone; [SIII], [ClIII], [ArIV] for intermediate/high. A genuinely new contribution here: they derive a new empirical Tₑ[SIII]–Tₑ[OIII] scaling relation, calibrated specifically in the high-temperature, low-metallicity regime that prior calibrations (Garnett 1992's photoionization-model relation; their own 2021 empirical relation) don't reach — useful to the field independent of this project's own Yₚ result. They also directly measure a real, non-trivial density stratification: the high-ionization-zone density tracer [ArIV] reads systematically ~10–15× higher than the low-ionization [SII]/[OII] densities. Rather than just noting this, they run the actual sensitivity test — recomputing O/H under the higher stratified densities — and show it changes the total oxygen abundance by less than the ~4% typical measurement uncertainty in all but one object, which is why they conclude it's safe to proceed with the simpler characteristic-density treatment.

**Oxygen abundance (Paper II §5).** With Tₑ and nₑ in hand, ionic (O⁺, O²⁺) and total O/H abundances follow via the PyNeb photoionization package, at a median 4% uncertainty across the sample — benchmarked against ~10 independent literature O/H measurement sources, finding a small mean offset (−0.006 dex) but a non-trivial scatter (0.095 dex, larger than the typical quoted per-object uncertainty) that the paper traces, object by object, to specific methodological differences (which Tₑ diagnostic a given study used, [OIII]λ4363 contamination by a neighboring [FeII] line, adopted density).

**The He I λ10830 density diagnostic at scale (Paper III).** This is the paper that actually delivers Concept 3 above. 48 new LUCI near-infrared spectra, reduced with PypeIt, wavelength-calibrated by hand off night-sky OH lines (not the standard arc-lamp approach — they found it was actually *more* accurate for these observations), with heavy manual intervention required on a majority of targets (30 of 48 needed non-default handling: misidentified slit edges, manual extraction for faint objects, deblending contaminating field stars). The critical measurement is the flux ratio of He I λ10830 to the adjacent H I Paschen-gamma line (they're only ~108 Å apart in wavelength, which makes their ratio essentially immune to reddening uncertainty). Median measurement uncertainty: 8% per object — worse than Izotov et al. (2014)'s 5% on a smaller sample, but the team explicitly cross-validates data quality against three independent surveys (Izotov+14, Hsyu+20, Yanagisawa+26/EMPRESS) using a hard physical floor: atomic-emissivity physics forbids the He I λ10830/Pγ ratio from dropping below ~2 for any realistic (Tₑ, nₑ). Three EMPRESS targets and one Hsyu target fall into this physically forbidden zone; *none* of the LBT Yₚ Project's own targets do — a direct, falsifiable data-quality check that the competing lower-resolution/lower-precision surveys visibly fail and this one passes.

**Assumption Audit**
 > **Watch:** the reader might assume this project is *directly observing* the early universe's helium content. **The paper actually says:** it's inferring a primordial value by measuring present-day, extremely metal-poor galaxies and extrapolating a Y-vs-O relationship back to zero metallicity — a local-universe proxy measurement, not a direct look at the early universe (only the CMB anisotropy spectrum genuinely constrains BBN-era physics from light that old).

> **Watch:** the reader might assume "more targets is strictly better." **The paper actually argues the opposite, explicitly:** citing Ferland et al. (2010), the team treats large low-quality samples (heavily SDSS-sourced, in some competing studies) as a liability, not an asset — because the field's current bottleneck is systematic floors that averaging over noisy data cannot fix, and large noisy samples can also *bias* the derived Y-vs-O slope by pulling in objects that don't cleanly satisfy the method's assumptions.

> **Watch:** the reader might assume the "three-zone ionization structure" is a directly measured fact about each galaxy's actual gas geometry. **It's a standard tractable model**, not a measurement — real HII regions have continuous, structured temperature and density fields, and the density-stratification result described above (high-ionization zones reading 10–15× denser) is itself evidence the real structure is messier than the three-zone approximation, even though the paper shows the approximation doesn't much affect the final O/H numbers.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

The standout contribution is the *combination* of (a) He I λ10830 deployed at real scale (48 targets, several times larger than prior λ10830 samples) with (b) an explicit, falsifiable data-quality floor — the "unphysical ratio < ~2" test — used to directly demonstrate this sample's data quality exceeds two contemporaneous competing surveys, rather than just asserting it. Breaking the Tₑ–nₑ degeneracy with this line isn't new in concept (Izotov et al. pioneered it in 2014); what's new here is doing it at scale with a hard, checkable quality bar.

A close second: the new Tₑ[SIII]–Tₑ[OIII] empirical scaling relation, calibrated specifically in a high-temperature/low-metallicity regime the existing literature relations don't cover — a genuinely reusable tool for the broader field, independent of this project's own Yₚ result.

**Predictive Content Check**

*Falsifiable handle:* These are methods/data papers, not hypothesis papers, so the honest answer is that they mostly don't predict new phenomena — they build and validate instrumentation. The one place a real, checkable quantitative claim appears is the new Tₑ[SIII]–Tₑ[OIII] relation (Paper II, Eq. 3): Tₑ[SIII] = 0.84(±0.04)×Tₑ[OIII] + 1700(±600) K over the calibrated range. That's a specific, falsifiable prediction — a future sample of high-Tₑ, low-metallicity HII regions with both temperatures directly measured could confirm or break it. Everything else in Papers I–III is better described as *re-measuring and re-validating* existing infrastructure at higher fidelity than as predicting anything new — which is a fair and honest characterization, not a knock on the papers.

*Formalism load:* Not applicable in the usual sense — there's no heavy theoretical formalism here to interrogate for decorativeness. The mathematical machinery (MCMC parameter estimation, super-Gaussian line fitting, orthogonal distance regression for the Tₑ scaling relation) is plainly load-bearing: it's literally what produces every tabulated number in these papers, not dressing on a separate argument.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> Real ISM density stratification exists (high-ionization-zone densities read ~10–15× above low-ionization-zone densities from [SII]/[OII]), which the standard three-zone model doesn't fully capture, even though the paper's own sensitivity test shows it doesn't much move the final O/H. **(A) Consensus** — this is explicitly measured, discussed, and quantified by the authors themselves, and density stratification in ionized nebulae is a well-known issue field-wide, not a fringe concern. **(paper §3.2, §5.1)**

> Two galaxies (SHOC133, WJ1205+4551) show an "inverted" temperature structure — Tₑ[NII] measured *higher* than Tₑ[OIII] — which standard stellar-photoionization models don't predict, hinting at non-stellar ionizing contributions (shocks, X-ray binaries, weak AGN) that aren't corrected for in the broader sample. **(B) Contested** — the paper names several candidate explanations without resolving which applies, and flags this as needing follow-up rather than treating it as solved. **(paper §4.2)**

> The whole Y-vs-O-extrapolated-to-zero method rests on an assumed-linear ΔY/ΔO relationship with no independently quantified systematic uncertainty from that linearity assumption. **(A) Consensus** — this is a longstanding, explicitly acknowledged structural limitation of the Peimbert & Torres-Peimbert methodology going back to the 1970s, not something this paper introduces; the project's strategy is to minimize its impact by pushing to the lowest achievable metallicities, not to solve it outright. **(paper §1.3, §6.2)**

> The new super-Gaussian/broad-wing line-fitting methodology, while shown to reduce fit residuals within this dataset, has not (in what's presented here) been cross-validated against an independent group's reduction of the same raw spectra. **(C) Speculative — analyst inference.** No external check is reported in Paper II itself; this is my own read of what's missing, not a paper-acknowledged gap, and a specialist in emission-line fitting might know of standard cross-checks that make this less of a concern than it looks from the outside. **(analyst inference)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

*A note on structure:* per the multi-paper adaptation of this framework, this section synthesizes across all three papers rather than running the 9-section structure three times — the sequence below reflects the actual dependency chain (methodology and target selection → optical data and physical conditions → near-infrared density diagnostic), not each paper's individual internal ordering.

Papers I–III together are the instrumentation phase of a five-paper project aimed at a 0.5%-precision measurement of the primordial helium abundance, Yₚ. Paper I lays out the philosophy and infrastructure: a deliberately quality-over-quantity sample of extremely metal-poor galaxies, three years of carefully-tracked LBT/MODS optical spectroscopy, updated atomic-physics inputs (new H and He emissivity tables, new radiative-transfer corrections), and an explicit acknowledgment that the field's historical problem has been underappreciated systematics, not insufficient data volume. Paper II does the heavy optical-data-reduction lifting: a new line-fitting methodology that better matches MODS's actual (non-Gaussian) line-spread function, careful multi-diagnostic measurement of electron temperature and density across three ionization zones, a new empirical temperature-scaling relation useful beyond this project, and the resulting oxygen abundances that will anchor the Y-vs-O extrapolation. Paper III supplies the piece that actually unlocks better precision: near-infrared spectra of the He I λ10830 line, whose density sensitivity breaks the temperature-density degeneracy that has limited every prior generation of this measurement, deployed and quality-validated at a scale several times larger than the line's previous best datasets.

The throughline connecting all three: this project's central bet is that the path to 0.5% precision runs through fixing systematics and breaking degeneracies with better diagnostics, not through brute-force sample growth. Every methodological choice described — the quality-first target cuts, the three-year calibration tracking, the new line-fitting profile, the λ10830 scale-up with its explicit unphysical-ratio quality check — is in service of that one bet. Whether it pays off is what Paper IV (the actual Yₚ derivation) will show.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the quantitative propagation of the various correction terms — the [FeII]λ4359 contamination correction to [OIII]λ4363, the density-stratification sensitivity test, the dielectronic recombination correction to [OII] — through to the final stated per-object error budgets. The papers report specific percentage impacts for each (e.g., "<4% change in O/H," "<3% reduction in [OIII] auroral intensity"), and I'm taking those stated conclusions at face value rather than independently re-deriving them from the underlying MCMC/PyNeb machinery, which I have not done. A specialist who has actually run this abundance-determination pipeline would be better positioned to judge whether these correction terms fully or only partially explain the ~0.095 dex literature-comparison scatter reported in Paper II.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **This is a "build the ruler first" project.** Three of five papers in this series are entirely about making the measuring instrument trustworthy — target selection, telescope calibration, and a new density diagnostic — before a single primordial-helium number is reported.

2. **The single most important move is deploying the He I λ10830 near-infrared line at real scale**, because it's the independent probe that breaks the decades-old temperature-density degeneracy that has capped the precision of every prior generation of this measurement.

3. **The team explicitly bets on quality over quantity** — a smaller, tightly vetted sample instead of pooling every available noisy archival spectrum — on the argument, backed by an explicit falsifiable quality check (the "unphysical ratio" test), that systematic floors, not statistical noise, are now the real bottleneck.

## 9. Shorter Summary

An international team used the Large Binocular Telescope to build the most precise measurement to date of how much helium the universe made in its first few minutes — a number (Yₚ) that, compared against Big Bang theory, tests how many kinds of light, ghostly particles (like neutrinos) existed back then. These three papers are the setup work before that final number gets reported.

You can't observe the early universe's helium directly, so the team measures it in nearby, chemically near-pristine galaxies and extrapolates back to zero contamination. Paper I lays out the strategy: pick the lowest-metallicity, highest-quality targets available rather than maximizing sample size, and calibrate the telescope obsessively over three years. Paper II processes the visible-light spectra of 62 galaxies, introducing a better line-fitting technique and deriving the temperature, density, and oxygen content of each galaxy's gas — oxygen serves as the "contamination clock" the helium measurement gets extrapolated against.

Paper III solves the field's oldest technical headache: standard helium measurements can't cleanly separate the effects of gas temperature from gas density, because both blur together in the same handful of spectral lines. The fix is a specific infrared helium line that's unusually sensitive to density alone, acting like an independent tie-breaker. The team measured this line in 48 galaxies — several times more than any prior study — and, notably, showed that none of their measurements fall into a range that atomic physics says is impossible, while a meaningful fraction of two competing recent surveys do.

None of this is a scientific discovery in itself. It's precision engineering: careful target selection, careful calibration, and a genuine methodological upgrade (the density diagnostic at scale) that together are supposed to cut the uncertainty on the final helium number roughly in half. Papers IV and V — not covered here — will report what that final number actually is and what it implies for physics beyond the Standard Model.

## Appendix — Deep-Dive Discussion Log (2026-09-14)

**Prompted by:** Mack quoting the Core Technical Explanation passage on the three-zone ionization model (Section 4, "Physical-conditions determination") and asking for it to be unpacked.

**What was expanded:**

**Why the zones exist at all — photon-budget depletion by threshold.** The paper gives the actual ionization-potential (IP) thresholds: low-ionization zone = N⁺, O⁺, S⁺ (IP ≲ 23 eV); intermediate zone = S²⁺, Cl²⁺, Ar²⁺ (23 ≲ IP ≲ 35 eV); high-ionization zone = O²⁺, Ar³⁺ (IP > 35 eV). Analogy: the star's radiation field is a finite budget of high-priority tokens spent greedily nearest the source — photons energetic enough to ionize O²⁺ get absorbed almost immediately by the innermost gas, so only sub-35 eV photons survive to reach the intermediate zone, and only sub-23 eV photons survive to reach the outer, low-ionization gas. The zones fall out of sequential photon-budget depletion by energy threshold (a Strömgren-sphere structure), not an arbitrary bookkeeping choice. **Breaks when:** pushed on sharp boundaries — real depletion is a smooth gradient, not a hard cutoff, which is exactly why the paper's own density-stratification result (zones reading different characteristic densities) shows the three-zone model is a tractable approximation, not literal discrete layering.

**Why Tₑ and nₑ are measured per zone rather than once for the whole nebula.** Different zones are physically different gas at different distances from the ionizing stars, with no reason to assume shared temperature/density — and different ionic species (needed for O⁺ vs O²⁺ vs He⁺ abundances) live in different zones, so a single averaged condition would mis-weight the abundance calculation.

**How a line ratio becomes a thermometer vs. a densitometer.** Temperature-sensitive (auroral/nebular) ratios, e.g. [NII]λ5755/λ6584: population of a hard-to-reach "auroral" energy level relative to an easy "nebular" one follows a Boltzmann factor, exponentially sensitive to Tₑ:

$$
\frac{n_{\text{auroral}}}{n_{\text{nebular}}} \propto e^{-\Delta E / k_B T_e}
$$

Symbol definitions: ΔE = energy gap between the two levels (fixed atomic property); k_B = Boltzmann's constant; Tₑ = electron temperature (the target unknown). What this means: small Tₑ changes produce large flux-ratio changes because Tₑ sits in the exponent — the same functional form as transistor leakage-current temperature dependence, or Arrhenius reaction-rate scaling (both familiar from Mack's CS/chemistry background), which is exactly why this ratio makes a precise thermometer.

Density-sensitive (doublet) ratios, e.g. [SII]λ6717/λ6731, [ArIV]λ4711/λ4740: two levels close in energy (so Tₑ barely matters) but with different critical densities — below critical density every collisional excitation radiates, so the ratio just reflects fixed statistical weights; above it, collisional de-excitation starts winning for the lower-critical-density level and the ratio shifts. Analogy: a queueing system's throughput-vs-load curve bending over as it approaches saturation — except "capacity" here is a fixed atomic property (critical density) rather than something provisioned.

**Why multiple independent diagnostics per zone, not just one.** Three failure modes a single diagnostic can't cover: the line can be too faint to detect in a given object (paper reports [NII]λ5755 detected in only 20/60 targets vs. 55 for [OII] and 45 for [SII]); a line can be contaminated by a neighboring blend (the [FeII]λ4359-on-[OIII]λ4363 contamination flagged in the main analysis); and independent diagnostics can genuinely *disagree*, which is itself informative — exactly what happened with SHOC133 and WJ1205+4551, where Tₑ[NII] came back ~2000 K hotter than Tₑ[OIII], surfacing the non-stellar-ionization-source possibility flagged in Section 6's Limitations. The new Tₑ[SIII]–Tₑ[OIII] scaling relation exists to solve the inverse problem: inferring a missing zone's temperature from a different zone's directly-measured one when the ideal direct diagnostic isn't available.

**Open thread offered, not yet taken up:** going deeper on the MCMC machinery that turns these Tₑ/nₑ diagnostics into a single coherent He/H estimate with correlated uncertainties, or on how PyNeb solves the two-parameter (Tₑ, nₑ) system from a line-ratio pair.
