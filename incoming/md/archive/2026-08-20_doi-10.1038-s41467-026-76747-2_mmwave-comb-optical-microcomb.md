## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Photonic Combs Go Straight to Radio — No Middleman Required**

A soliton pulse train from a chip-compatible optical microcomb, fed directly into an off-the-shelf terahertz antenna with zero extra amplification, converts itself into a broadband, multi-tone millimetre-wave frequency comb — turning "very sharp light pulses in" into "many clean radio tones out" as an almost-free side effect of how fast those pulses are.

---

## 2. Big-Picture Context

High-purity millimetre-wave (mm-wave) and terahertz (THz) sources are becoming infrastructure, not just lab curiosities. 6G wireless is expected to push carrier frequencies up into the sub-THz range (tens to hundreds of GHz) to get more bandwidth, and that requires sources with excellent phase noise, traceable timing, and — increasingly — the ability to generate not just one clean tone but several at once for channel-multiplexed links, broadband calibration, and multi-tone device testing. The same sub-THz sources are also the backbone of time-domain THz spectroscopy (TDS), used to probe everything from material properties to quantum states.

Photonics has become the best way to generate these signals with very low phase noise, via "optical frequency division": you build an ultra-stable optical frequency comb (evenly spaced laser frequencies, like sharp rungs on a ladder), beat two comb lines together, and the resulting electrical beat note inherits the comb's excellent stability while landing at a much lower, radio-friendly frequency. This works extremely well, but it has mostly been demonstrated for generating a *single* ultra-clean tone. Photonic integrated "microcombs" — frequency combs generated in a tiny ring resonator instead of a tabletop laser — are especially attractive here because their natural pulse-repetition rate already falls in the sub-THz range, and because they're compact and low-power enough to imagine putting on a chip next to a wireless front end.

What this paper adds is the next step: instead of generating one microwave tone via division, it demonstrates *direct* generation of a full broadband comb of mm-wave harmonics, straight out of a microcomb, using the simplest possible conversion step — a standard photoconductive THz antenna, with no extra optical amplifier in the loop for the main result. It also shows that the antenna, once you're just measuring rather than driving it hard, can be scanned to observe long-term stability, locked to an external clock for better timing performance, and that the number and spacing of solitons circulating in the laser cavity can be used to reshape which harmonics come out strong.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is an experimental, proof-of-concept engineering-physics paper — a photonics/THz-engineering demonstration, not a new fundamental prediction. What's at stake is practical: whether a compact, photonic, chip-compatible microcomb can be a *direct*, broadband mm-wave comb source for next-generation wireless, sensing, and metrology systems, without needing bulky RF synthesis chains.

**Prior Belief Check:** The result is broadly *consistent* with the existing consensus that microcombs make excellent low-phase-noise mm-wave sources — that part isn't surprising, it's been shown before via optical division for single tones. What's genuinely a step past current practice, and would likely raise eyebrows among specialists who assumed you needed extra gain or dedicated RF electronics to get useful output at these repetition rates, is that a full multi-harmonic comb comes out directly from photoconductive conversion of an *unamplified* ~5 mW soliton source, using antennas that were never designed for 50 GHz pulse trains. It's an incremental step in the broader roadmap (photonic mm-wave generation), but the specific mechanism — direct broadband comb generation without added amplification or division circuitry, plus using multisoliton states as a spectral-shaping knob — is a new wrinkle, not confirmatory of an already-established result.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is a single-group (single collaboration) result — one closely coauthored team across UK (Loughborough, Sussex), Hong Kong, Canada (INRS), and Australia (Swinburne) institutions, with no independent replication yet. Independent confirmation would look like a different lab reproducing the broadband-comb-from-unamplified-microcomb result with a different laser-cavity-soliton (or Kerr) microcomb design and a different commercial or custom photoconductive antenna, and independently verifying both the long-delay coherence claim and the multisoliton spectral-reshaping effect. Given that the underlying physics (photoconductive antenna response, soliton interference) is well-established and the main novelty is architectural/systems-level rather than a new physical effect, the bar for replication is lower than for, say, a new particle-physics measurement — but it hasn't been cleared yet.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**What's an optical frequency comb, and why does a "soliton" matter?**

A mode-locked laser doesn't just emit one frequency — if you look at its output pulse train in the frequency domain, it's a set of perfectly evenly spaced spectral lines, like the teeth of a comb. The spacing between the teeth equals the pulse repetition rate (how often a pulse goes by). A "soliton" here is a specific, robust pulse shape that circulates in a nonlinear optical cavity without spreading out or decaying — its own nonlinear self-focusing exactly cancels the pulse-spreading (dispersion) it would otherwise suffer, so it just goes around and around, stable, for as long as the laser runs.

This paper uses a *laser-cavity soliton* (LCS): a fibre laser loop (with erbium-ytterbium gain fibre for amplification) whose cavity is closed through a tiny, high-quality nonlinear microresonator (a ring waveguide). The microring's own natural pulse-repetition rate (its free-spectral range, ~48.9 GHz — set purely by the ring's physical size and refractive index) acts as a spectral filter on the much longer fibre loop, forcing solitons to form and repeat at that microring rate rather than the fibre loop's own, much slower, natural repetition rate (~95 MHz). It's a hybrid: fibre-laser gain, microresonator-defined timing.

**Central analogy for this concept — Sharp clock edges radiate rich harmonic combs.**

**Analogy:** Think of the soliton pulse train like a digital clock signal on a circuit board. A clean sine-wave oscillator puts out energy at exactly one frequency. But a fast, sharply-edged clock signal is "harmonic-rich" — its sharp rising and falling transitions pack energy into the fundamental frequency *and* all its integer multiples (harmonics). This is exactly why fast digital clocks are notorious sources of broadband electromagnetic interference across many multiples of the base clock rate — the sharper the edge, the more harmonics it excites, and the higher in frequency they extend. The soliton pulses here are so short (sub-picosecond) that they're the optical equivalent of an extremely sharp clock edge; once converted to electrical current, they naturally populate a whole comb of mm-wave harmonics, not just one tone.

**Breaks when:** you push it to ask about *purpose*. In digital electronics, harmonic-rich EMI from clock edges is an unwanted byproduct engineers spend real effort suppressing (shielding, spread-spectrum clocking). Here, the harmonic richness is the desired output, deliberately exploited. The analogy also breaks on *shape control*: an idealized square-wave clock's harmonic content is essentially fixed by symmetry, while — as this paper shows — the microcomb's harmonic envelope can be deliberately reshaped by choosing how many solitons circulate and how they're spaced (see below), something a plain digital clock signal can't do without external filtering.

**How does a photoconductive antenna turn light into radio waves?**

A photoconductive antenna has a semiconductor gap (biased with a small DC voltage) that's normally non-conductive — a broken circuit. A very short optical pulse, tuned above the semiconductor's bandgap, briefly knocks loose a burst of free charge carriers, closing the gap for roughly a picosecond before the carriers recombine. That transient current pulse, radiated through the antenna structure, becomes the emitted THz/mm-wave field. Because the carriers recombine quickly, the emitted field tracks the *rate of change* of the optical pulse's intensity, not its absolute brightness — this is the differentiator relationship worked through numerically below.

**What's THz-TDS, and why the mechanical delay stage?**

**Analogy:** The pump-probe TDS setup works like an old equivalent-time-sampling oscilloscope characterizing a signal too fast to digitize directly in one shot. Rather than capturing an entire waveform in one go, it fires the same fast event repeatedly and records one data point per repetition, each time at a slightly different relative delay (set by a mechanical delay stage), then stitches thousands of these single-point samples together into a full reconstructed waveform. Here, one antenna ("emitter") generates the THz pulse from one branch of the laser output, and a second antenna ("receiver"), fed by a time-delayed copy of the *same* pulse train, acts as the sampling gate — its own transient photoconductivity briefly "opens the door" to record the instantaneous field at that particular delay.

**Breaks when:** you push on what's actually being sampled. A lab oscilloscope samples a pre-existing, independent voltage signal. Here, the *same* laser source both creates the THz pulse (via the emitter) and gates its own measurement (via the receiver) — it isn't sampling an external process. And the receiver's photoconductive response isn't an ideal, infinitely-fast sampling gate; it has its own finite pulse shape, so the recorded trace is mathematically a *convolution* of the true field with the receiver's own response, not a clean point-sample. That convolution measurably blurs which harmonics are visible (see Section 4).

**What happens when more than one soliton circulates?**

Multiple solitons can coexist in the same cavity, and where the field is dominated by two competing charge/spin/pulse patterns interacting, the analogy below applies.

**Analogy:** Picture two identical requests entering the same pipelined system at slightly different offsets. Individually each produces one clean output pattern, but because they share the pipeline, their effects overlap and interfere: some stages see reinforcement, others see cancellation, depending on the relative offset. Two solitons circulating in the same microring behave similarly — each one, alone, would produce one harmonic envelope when photodetected, but with two present at a specific relative spacing, their converted electrical outputs interfere: some mm-wave harmonics get reinforced, others suppressed, while the underlying comb-line spacing itself doesn't change at all, because that spacing is set by the cavity's physical size, not by where the solitons happen to sit inside it.

**Breaks when:** pushed on the "pipeline conflict" framing — a resource conflict in a real pipeline is usually a hazard engineers try to avoid, whereas soliton interference here is desirable and, as this paper shows, exploitable. It also breaks on granularity: pipeline hazards are typically discrete stall/no-stall events, while the real interference pattern here is a smooth, continuous function of the exact relative soliton delay (closer to a continuous comb-filter response than a binary stall).

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**The source (Fig. 1a, b).** A four-port, high-index doped-silica micro-ring resonator (FSR ≈ 48.9 GHz, linewidth <140 MHz, Q ≈ 1.3 million) is embedded in a polarization-maintaining, erbium-ytterbium co-doped fibre laser cavity. The ring's through-port output is bandpass-filtered at 1540 nm (12 nm width) to strip out a strong, undesired erbium amplified-spontaneous-emission background — important because that background would otherwise dilute the pulse energy actually available to drive the antenna. For the *headline* demonstration, this filtered output (~5 mW average power, driven by <1.5 W of electrical pump power) is sent straight to the THz-TDS system with no further amplification. A second, optional amplifier stage (boosting to ~120 mW) was used only to improve signal-to-noise ratio in some later measurements, not to enable the basic conversion.

**The conversion step (Eq. 1, Methods Eq. 3).** Feeding this optical pulse train straight into commercial fibre-coupled photoconductive antennas (Menlo TERA15-TX/RX) generates the mm-wave field:

$$E_{THz}(t) \propto \frac{dI(t)}{dt}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$E_{THz}(t)$ : the emitted terahertz/mm-wave electric field, as a function of time $t$
$I(t)$ : the optical pulse's intensity envelope — how bright the light is at each instant
$\frac{d}{dt}$ : the rate of change with respect to time

**What this actually means:** the antenna behaves like an edge-triggered differentiator circuit — a capacitor-coupled stage that only responds to a signal's *rate of change*, not its steady level. A constant light intensity produces nothing; it's the pulse's fast rise and fall that briefly opens the semiconductor switch and produces a current burst. Because the optical pulse rises and falls in well under a picosecond, that derivative is large and brief, which is exactly why the measured output (Fig. 1c) is a single-cycle bipolar pulse rather than a smooth waveform.

**Worked numerical check.** The paper reports a single-cycle THz pulse with a full peak-to-peak (half-cycle) duration of about 1.5–2 ps. A general rule of thumb — the same one used to estimate the highest useful clock harmonic from a rising edge's transition time in digital electronics — says a feature that completes in time $\tau$ carries frequency content up to roughly $1/\tau$. Using $\tau \approx 1.5$ ps:

$1 / (1.5\times10^{-12}\text{ s}) \approx 6.7\times10^{11}\text{ Hz} = 670\text{ GHz}$

That's within about 10% of the ~600 GHz of resolvable harmonic content the paper actually reports (Fig. 2d) — a nice sanity check that the observed comb bandwidth is set directly by how short the optical pulse (and hence the derivative spike) is, not by some separate limiting factor. It's also *why* pushing toward even shorter pulses is the natural lever for extending comb bandwidth further.

**Comb structure and multisoliton reshaping (Fig. 1d, e; Fig. 3).** In the single-soliton case, the Fourier spectrum of the converted mm-wave trace (Fig. 1d) directly shows a comb of harmonics spaced at the microring's ~48.9 GHz repetition rate, extending out past 0.6–1 THz. Switching to a two-soliton state (Fig. 1e; analysed in detail in Fig. 3) preserves that same line spacing exactly — because it's set by the cavity, not the solitons — but *redistributes* the harmonic envelope: some lines get stronger, others weaker, purely because of interference between the two solitons' individually-generated fields. The authors reconstruct the optical intensity profile from an autocorrelation measurement, predict the expected THz waveform from Eq. 1, convolve it with the receiver antenna's own response (Eq. 2, accounting for the "equivalent-time-sampling" blur discussed above), and get a good match to the actually measured trace — direct evidence the reshaping mechanism is understood, not just observed.

**Stability and evolving states (Figs. 2–4).** A one-hour scan of the single-soliton state shows consistent spectral and pulse properties, attributed to the LCS microcomb's inherent "self-recovery" (it naturally relaxes back toward the same soliton state after small perturbations — this is cited to earlier work by the same group, not re-derived here). A 30-minute scan of a stable two-soliton state (periodicity 20 ps, soliton spacing 4.46 ps) shows the same reconstruction-matches-experiment consistency. In a longer scan positioned deliberately near the boundary between single- and multi-soliton operating regimes, the system is observed drifting through several distinct two-soliton configurations (different relative spacings) before settling into a single-soliton state — each configuration leaving a visibly distinct THz waveform signature, confirming that the detected mm-wave waveform really is a direct fingerprint of the soliton configuration circulating in the cavity at that moment.

**Locking to an external reference (Figs. 5–6, Methods).** A piezo-wound fibre stretcher, driven by a PID loop, actively tunes the fibre-cavity length. The error signal comes from an electro-optic modulator that puts sidebands on each comb line; the beat note between adjacent lines' sidebands is locked, via the PID loop, to a 20.5 MHz reference that is itself ultimately GPS-disciplined. Locking dramatically improves timing jitter — from about 6.3 ps (unlocked, dominated by a specific low-frequency mechanical oscillation) down to under 150 fs (locked), roughly a 40x improvement — but leaves the actual TDS pulse shape, spectral content, and conversion efficiency essentially unchanged (Fig. 5d): the lock is about long-term timing traceability, not pulse quality. Selected individual harmonics at 50, 100, and 150 GHz were separately down-converted and their Allan deviation measured; locked operation shows dramatically reduced drift, down to a few parts in 10⁻¹² over 1–100 s averaging — though (important, see Assumption Audit below) this number describes the referenced locking chain's transfer coherence, not an independent absolute stability of the microcomb by itself.

**Long-delay coherence (Fig. 5e).** Feeding the receiver antenna via a fibre path 8 m longer than the emitter's — roughly 27 ns, or over 1,000 pulse periods, of extra delay — produces no statistically significant change in the measured mm-wave spectrum, even with the lock disengaged. That's a genuinely useful practical result: it means the source's own coherence is long enough that TDS systems built around it don't need tightly matched, synchronized delay paths.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** A reader might assume "broadband comb" here means something like a tunable, arbitrarily-shapeable RF synthesizer output. The paper actually shows the comb *line spacing* is fixed, always equal to the microring's ~48.9 GHz FSR — what multisoliton engineering tunes is only the *envelope* (which existing lines are strong or weak), not new frequencies or a different spacing.

> **Watch:** A reader might assume the whole demonstrated system is "amplifier-free" end to end, since the headline result avoids extra optical amplification at the conversion step. In fact the pump laser itself still needs an EDFA (<1.5 W electrical) to run at all, and the locking scheme needs an electro-optic modulator, PID electronics, frequency counters, and a GPS-disciplined reference — "amplifier-free" describes only the mm-wave conversion step, not the system as a whole.

> **Watch:** A reader might read the reported ~10⁻¹² Allan deviation as an absolute, self-contained frequency-stability figure for the microcomb source itself. The paper explicitly states that both the lock and the readout share the same GPS-disciplined reference, so this number is a measure of the *locking chain's transfer coherence* — how well the loop tracks its reference — not an independent absolute stability measurement of the source.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Two things stand out as the real contribution, distinct from "yet another microcomb-photonics demonstration":

**1. Direct, unamplified, broadband conversion.** Prior microcomb-based mm-wave work has mostly targeted a single clean tone, generated via optical frequency division (beating comb lines together and dividing down). This paper instead drives a commercial photoconductive antenna directly with the raw, unamplified soliton output and gets a *whole comb* of harmonics out, single-shot, because the LCS microcomb's high spectral efficiency (essentially no non-pulsed background diluting the pulse energy) concentrates enough peak power per pulse to make this work without extra gain. That's new to the field, not just to the reader — it sidesteps both the RF-synthesis-and-multiplication approach and the single-tone-division approach that dominate prior work.

**2. Multisoliton states as a source-level spectral-shaping knob.** Rather than treating multisoliton dynamics as a nuisance or noise source to avoid (which is how they're usually framed in Kerr-comb engineering), this paper shows the relative spacing between circulating solitons directly and predictably reshapes which mm-wave harmonics come out strong, with no added external hardware — genuinely new to the field as a practical mm-wave-comb-shaping technique, even though soliton interference itself is known physics.

### Predictive Content Check

**Falsifiable handle (always):** This is fundamentally a demonstration/engineering paper, not one making a new physical-law prediction, so the nearest falsifiable claims are quantitative engineering ones. The clearest: the paper attributes the poor photoconductive conversion efficiency to a mismatch between the antenna's electrical replenishment time constant (designed for ~100 MHz pump lasers) and the microcomb's much faster ~50 GHz pulse rate, and states that antenna/electrical redesign specifically tailored to microcomb repetition rates "may" improve conversion efficiency by "several orders of magnitude." That's a concrete, testable claim — a purpose-built antenna should show an order-of-magnitude-scale efficiency jump; if redesigned antennas show only marginal improvement, this specific explanation is wrong. A second, already-tested falsifiable claim: given a measured two-soliton spacing, Eqs. 1–2 predict a specific harmonic-envelope interference pattern, which the paper checks directly against the measured spectrum (Fig. 3) and finds matches — a prediction that could have failed but didn't.

**Formalism load (conditional):** Skipped — the paper doesn't lean on abstract mathematical structure to generate new results. Equations 1–3 are simple, standard photoconductive-antenna physics used only to interpret and reconstruct already-measured waveforms; removing them wouldn't change what was measured, only how transparently it's explained. The formalism here is plainly doing explanatory work, not decorative work, so this half of the check doesn't add anything and is left out per the framework's own guidance.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> Commercial photoconductive antennas are designed for pump lasers around 100 MHz, roughly 500x slower than this ~50 GHz microcomb; incomplete carrier depletion/replenishment between pulses at this rate bounds the achievable peak photocurrent, and the antennas' electrical supply design assumes charge replenishment on ~100 MHz timescales. **(A) Consensus** — this is explicitly acknowledged and analyzed by the authors themselves as the dominant efficiency bottleneck. **(paper, Methods/Discussion)**

> This is a single-group result with no independent replication of either the direct-broadband-conversion claim or the multisoliton-reshaping technique. **(A) Consensus** — straightforwardly true by construction; one paper, one collaboration, no external confirmation exists yet. **(analyst inference — obvious from the author list and absence of a second citing/replicating study, but not something the paper states about itself)**

> The reported sub-10⁻¹² Allan-deviation stability describes the transfer coherence of the locking chain relative to the shared GPS-disciplined reference, not an independently verified absolute stability of the microcomb source on its own. **(A) Consensus** — the paper states this limitation about its own measurement explicitly and clearly. **(paper, Results, discussion around Fig. 5c/Fig. 6)**

> The multisoliton spectral-reshaping technique is demonstrated via naturally-occurring soliton configurations that the system happened to settle into (and that "tend to pin to defects" and stay stable "if not strongly perturbed") rather than via a shown method for reliably dialing in a specific, desired soliton spacing on demand. Whether targeted, on-demand generation of a chosen spacing is straightforward engineering or a genuinely harder open problem isn't established here. **(B) Contested** — reasonable specialists could disagree on how large a gap this is for practical deployment, since related soliton-control techniques (e.g., via pump detuning or auxiliary lasers) exist elsewhere in the Kerr-comb literature but aren't demonstrated in this specific system. **(paper §Results/Discussion, extrapolated by the analyst to the broader question of practical control)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

The paper demonstrates a compact route to broadband millimetre-wave frequency comb generation: take a laser-cavity-soliton microcomb — a hybrid fibre-laser/microresonator source whose soliton pulses repeat at the microring's natural ~48.9 GHz rate — and feed its raw, unamplified output (about 5 mW) directly into a standard, commercially available photoconductive THz time-domain-spectroscopy antenna pair. The result is not a single clean radio tone (the usual target of prior microcomb-mm-wave work, generated via optical frequency division) but a genuine multi-harmonic comb, spanning roughly 600 GHz to nearly 1 THz of useful bandwidth, arising because the antenna essentially differentiates the optical pulse's intensity envelope, and an extremely short optical pulse has a correspondingly steep, harmonic-rich derivative. The single-cycle, carrier-envelope-offset-free nature of the output pulse (Fig. 1c) means the whole broadband comb comes along for free with the pulse shape itself — no separate synthesis of each tone is needed.

Beyond the base demonstration, the paper does three further things. First, it characterizes long-term stability: the source remains self-consistent over hour-long scans, and remains coherent enough that adding 8 metres of extra fibre delay between the transmit and receive paths (over 1,000 pulse periods) causes no measurable change to the mm-wave spectrum — a practically useful relaxation of the tight-synchronization requirements typical TDS systems assume. Second, it demonstrates active stabilization: locking the soliton repetition rate to a GPS-disciplined microwave reference (via a piezo-stretched fibre segment and a PID feedback loop referenced through an electro-optic-modulator beat-note scheme) drops the integrated timing jitter roughly 40-fold, from several picoseconds down to under 150 femtoseconds, and shows correspondingly tighter Allan deviation on individually-selected 50, 100, and 150 GHz harmonics — while leaving the actual pulse shape and conversion efficiency unaffected, showing the lock addresses long-term timing traceability rather than short-term signal quality. Third, and arguably the most conceptually interesting piece, it shows that when more than one soliton pulse circulates in the cavity simultaneously, their interference upon photodetection reshapes the comb's harmonic *envelope* — some harmonics strengthened, others suppressed — while leaving the fundamental comb-line spacing completely unchanged, since that spacing is fixed by the cavity's physical geometry rather than by the solitons' positions within it. The authors independently verify this mechanism by reconstructing a measured two-soliton configuration from an autocorrelation trace, predicting the resulting mm-wave waveform from first principles (a simple derivative-and-convolution model), and matching it against the actual measured trace.

The paper is honest about where the practical limitation currently sits: the photoconductive antennas used were designed and optimized for laser sources around 100 MHz, not ~50 GHz, so the achievable conversion efficiency here is well below what dedicated hardware could deliver — the authors explicitly flag "several orders of magnitude" of realistic headroom if antennas and their electrical supply circuitry were redesigned specifically for microcomb-scale repetition rates.

**Why I've framed it this way:** I've deliberately centered the explanation on the "sharp edges make rich harmonics" idea, because it's the single mental model that ties together why direct conversion works at all (short pulse → big, brief derivative → many harmonics), why longer-term stability and locking matter separately from that (timing traceability is a different axis from pulse-shape quality), and why multisoliton reshaping is possible without extra hardware (interference between two already-harmonic-rich sources). I've also deliberately separated the "referenced" Allan-deviation stability claim from a hypothetical "absolute" stability claim, because the paper is careful about this distinction itself and it's an easy point for a reader to over-read.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the metrological locking-chain engineering in the Methods section (the electro-optic-modulator sideband scheme, the specific 8.168 GHz modulation frequency, and exactly how the beat notes at each targeted harmonic — 50, 100, 150 GHz — are down-converted into the accessible RF counter band for Fig. 6) is the most jargon-dense part of the paper, and my plain-language description of it is a compressed simplification. I believe I've captured the *purpose* of the scheme (referencing the repetition rate and its harmonics to a common GPS-disciplined clock) correctly, but there may be real subtlety in exactly how the frequency-counter/heterodyne readout chain avoids introducing its own systematic offsets that I haven't fully unpacked here.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **Sharp pulses are secretly broadband radio sources.** Feed an extremely short optical pulse straight into a simple antenna, with no amplifier and no synthesizer, and you don't get one radio tone out — you get a whole family of them, because a fast-changing signal is inherently rich in harmonics. This paper shows that trick works even with commercial, off-the-shelf hardware never designed for it.

2. **This is a floor, not a ceiling.** The antennas used here were built for lasers running 500x slower than this microcomb. The paper's own analysis says a purpose-built antenna could realistically boost the output by orders of magnitude — meaning today's "good enough to work" demonstration likely understates what this approach can eventually do.

3. **A "problem" turned into a feature.** Multiple solitons circulating together in the same laser cavity are usually treated as noise to avoid in comb engineering. Here, their interference becomes a free dial: change how many solitons there are and how far apart they sit, and you reshape which radio harmonics come out loud — no external filters or extra electronics required.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Millimetre-wave radio sources with very low noise are becoming essential for next-generation (6G) wireless, sensing, and timing systems. Photonic "frequency combs" — laser sources that emit many perfectly evenly-spaced frequencies at once — have become the best way to generate these signals, but past work mostly used them to make one very clean radio tone, via an indirect conversion process.

This paper does something more direct. The researchers take a compact, chip-based optical "microcomb" — a laser source whose pulses circulate through a tiny ring resonator and repeat about 49 billion times per second — and feed its raw output straight into an ordinary, commercially available radio antenna designed for lab spectroscopy, with no extra amplification. Instead of getting one radio tone out, they get a whole broadband family of them, spanning roughly 600 to 1,000 gigahertz. The reason is intuitive: the optical pulses are so short and sharp that, once converted to an electrical signal, they naturally contain many harmonics at once — the same reason a very fast digital clock signal radiates interference across many multiples of its base frequency.

The team also shows the source stays coherent over unusually long delays (equivalent to over a thousand pulse repetitions), which relaxes how tightly a receiving system needs to be synchronized to it; that locking the pulse repetition rate to a GPS-referenced clock improves timing precision roughly 40-fold without changing the pulse shape itself; and, most strikingly, that when two pulses circulate in the laser cavity instead of one, their interference reshapes which radio harmonics come out strongest — a way to sculpt the output spectrum using nothing but the laser's own internal dynamics, no external filters needed.

The honest caveat: today's antennas were built for much slower lasers, so the actual power efficiency here is well below what's achievable — the authors estimate a purpose-built antenna could improve things by orders of magnitude. This is a single research group's demonstration, not yet independently replicated, and its precision-timing numbers describe how well the system tracks its own external reference clock rather than an independent absolute stability rating. Still, it's a genuinely new and simple route from "a chip-scale optical pulse source" to "a broadband radio comb," with real implications for compact 6G-era hardware and terahertz measurement systems.
