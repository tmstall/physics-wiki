## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**"Whose Page Curve Is It Anyway? Rotating Black Holes, Swallow-Tails, and the Information That Comes Back in Fits and Starts"**

This paper takes the now-standard "islands fix the information paradox" toy calculation and runs it on a *spinning*, AdS-embedded black hole for the first time in this combination — then shows that if the hole also happens to sit near a genuine thermodynamic phase transition, the smooth textbook Page curve you're used to seeing can develop a sharp kink or jump instead.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Since 2019–2020, the "island" program (Almheiri, Engelhardt, Marolf, Maxfield; Penington; Almheiri-Hartman-Maldacena-Shaghoulian-Tajdini — refs [10]–[14] here) resolved a 45-year-old headache: Hawking's original calculation says the entanglement entropy of the radiation leaving an evaporating black hole grows forever, but unitarity (information can't actually be destroyed) says it has to turn over and come back down to zero once the hole is gone — that turnover is the Page curve, predicted by Don Page in 1993 on pure statistics grounds, decades before anyone could derive it from first principles. The island fix says: when you compute entanglement entropy holographically, you have to let the "region" whose entropy you're extremizing include not just the escaped radiation but, at late times, a chunk of the black hole's interior too (the "island"). Once you allow that, the curve turns over exactly where Page said it should.

The overwhelming majority of that literature — and this is worth sitting with for a second — has been done on *non-rotating*, often asymptotically *flat* toy black holes: Schwarzschild, RN, dilaton black holes in 1+1 or 2+1 dimensions. Spin gets you superradiance, a much messier near-horizon geometry, and a genuinely richer thermodynamic phase diagram (Kerr-AdS black holes, unlike Schwarzschild-AdS, support *first-order* phase transitions between distinct black hole branches purely as a function of the spin parameter — this has been known since Caldarelli, Cognola & Klemm's foundational 1999–2000 work, [Thermodynamics of Kerr-Newman-AdS black holes and conformal field theories](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/9908022), and has spawned a large "black-hole-chemistry" literature since). This paper's specific move is to bolt those two threads together: take the island machinery (previously applied to *charged* AdS black holes with a first-order transition by Lin, Yu, Ge & Tian, [Entanglement Entropy, Phase Transition, and Island Rule for Reissner-Nordström-AdS Black Holes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.06873), *Phys. Rev. D* 110, 046008 (2024) — explicitly cited as ref [67] and clearly the direct template) and apply it instead to a *rotating* AdS black hole, where the phase transition is driven by spin rather than charge.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is a theoretical/formal quantum-gravity paper — a semiclassical holographic calculation, not an experimental or observational one. Nothing here is testable against real astrophysical black holes; the stakes are entirely about whether the island-rule story for information recovery is *robust* — does it still reproduce a sensible Page curve when you add realistic complications (rotation, a genuine first-order phase transition), or does something break?

**Prior Belief Check:** The headline finding — islands reproduce the Page curve for Kerr-AdS, saturating at twice the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy — is not surprising to experts; it's exactly what the framework is built to do, and by this point (dozens of island papers across nearly every black hole background you can name, several cited in this paper's own introduction: refs [26]–[66]) a *failure* to reproduce Page's curve would be the shocking result. The genuinely novel-feeling claim — that a first-order phase transition leaves a visible discontinuity in the dynamical Page curve — would raise eyebrows among specialists, not because it's implausible, but because the mechanism connecting a static, equilibrium concept (which thermodynamic branch has lower free energy) to a dynamical, non-equilibrium process (the black hole evaporating in real time) is asserted here more than it's derived, which I'll come back to in Sections 5–6. Net assessment: incremental in method, moderately interesting in its specific conclusion, contingent on an assumption that isn't fully justified.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** Single-group result, no independent confirmation. It was published as a Regular Article in *The European Physical Journal C* (86, 611, June 2026) — so it has cleared ordinary peer review — but the specific claim "first-order transitions imprint discontinuities on Kerr-AdS Page curves" hasn't yet been checked by anyone outside this group. Independent confirmation would look like: another group redoing the island calculation with a genuinely dynamical (not quasi-static/linear) evaporation model, or extending the charged-black-hole precedent (ref [67]) to check whether *its* phase-transition-driven discontinuity survives a similarly careful dynamical treatment.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Concept 1 — The island as a "recovery becomes cheaper elsewhere" event.**

Ordinary holographic entanglement entropy (the Ryu-Takayanagi prescription) says: to find the entropy of a region of the boundary CFT (here, the radiation that's escaped the black hole), find the minimal bulk surface anchored on that region's boundary, and its area (divided by 4G) is the entropy. Early on, the only candidate surface sits right near where the radiation was collected, and its area — and hence the entropy — just keeps growing as more radiation piles up. The island rule adds a second candidate surface, one that pokes into the black hole interior, and says: compute the *generalized entropy* (area of this surface, plus ordinary field-theory entanglement entropy of everything inside it) for every candidate, extremize, and take the smallest. At late times, the interior candidate wins.

**Analogy:** Think of this like a distributed storage system with erasure coding across three servers, where a file's actual bytes physically live on Server A (the black hole interior), but a network monitor deciding "how much information is accessible to a client watching only Servers B and C (the radiation)" doesn't have to physically read Server A's disk once enough correlated parity data has accumulated on B and C. Early on, the monitor's honest answer is "growing, unbounded, since I haven't seen enough parity yet." Past a threshold, the *cheaper, correct* description switches to "the reconstructible content is fixed and capped," even though nothing physically moved off Server A.

**Breaks when:** you push this to ask what physically changes at the switchover. In real erasure coding, two independently existing copies (or parity shards) sit on different disks the whole time, and it's just your bookkeeping that catches up. In the island story, there's only ever one quantum state — the "surface inside the horizon" isn't a separate stored copy, it's a re-description of *the same* degrees of freedom in a different variable (entanglement wedge reconstruction). If you interpret the island as literally "a backup copy of the interior sitting somewhere else," you've violated no-cloning and have the wrong picture.

**Concept 2 — Why you can turn a 4D rotating black hole into a stack of independent 1D problems.**

Directly solving a wave equation on the full 4D Kerr-AdS geometry is intractable in closed form. The trick (imported from ref [25], Jiang & Wu) is: expand the scalar field in the basis of angular eigenfunctions $Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)$ — this is exactly analogous to diagonalizing a big, coupled linear system into its eigenbasis. Once you do that, the angular ($\theta,\phi$) dependence completely decouples, and each $(l,m)$ mode becomes its own independent, decoupled (1+1)-dimensional field theory living on the radial-time plane near the horizon. You've traded one intractable 4D coupled problem for infinitely many *independent, tractable* 2D problems that can each be solved on their own and never talk to each other again.

**Analogy:** This is the same move as diagonalizing a large correlated dataset into its principal components (eigenvectors) so that instead of one enormous coupled optimization, you get N independent, embarrassingly-parallel 1D sub-problems — like spinning off N single-threaded jobs instead of wrestling with one giant multi-threaded, lock-contended job.

**Breaks when:** you ask what happens to the *coupling terms* you dropped. The reduction is only clean near the horizon (the "near-horizon dimensional reduction"), and the paper explicitly restricts to the s-wave ($l=0$) sector and to the small-spin regime $a \ll M$ where Hawking emission dominates over superradiance. Push the analogy to imagine this decomposition is exact and lossless everywhere, for all $l$, at any spin — and it breaks; higher partial waves and superradiant mixing are being thrown away by hand, not shown to be negligible from first principles.

**Concept 3 — First-order phase transitions and the swallow-tail.**

An AdS black hole in the canonical ensemble (fixed temperature, coupled to a thermal bath) has a free energy $F(T)$. For Schwarzschild-AdS this famously has a single "Hawking-Page" transition to a thermal-AdS state. For a *charged or rotating* AdS black hole, something richer can happen: below a critical value of the charge or spin, $F(T)$ becomes triple-valued over a range of temperatures — plotting it produces a self-intersecting "swallow-tail" shape — corresponding to three distinct black hole solutions (small, intermediate, large) all existing at the same temperature. The crossing point of the swallow-tail marks a genuine first-order phase transition between the small and large branches; the middle (intermediate) branch has higher free energy and negative specific heat, so it's thermodynamically unstable and never physically preferred. Above the critical value, the three branches merge and the swallow-tail vanishes — this is a full "black-hole-chemistry" analog of the liquid-gas critical point, well-established for RN-AdS and (per Caldarelli-Cognola-Klemm) for Kerr-AdS since the late 1990s.

**Analogy:** Picture a CPU power-management scheme where, for a given target thermal-design-power target, there are actually *two* stable (frequency, voltage) operating points that dissipate the same power — a low-frequency/high-voltage-efficiency point and a high-frequency/lower-efficiency point — plus one *unstable* intermediate point that a real controller would never settle into (any perturbation pushes it toward one of the two stable points). The system picks whichever stable point has lower total energy cost, and if you sweep the target power slowly, at some point the "cheaper" choice flips discontinuously from one branch to the other — a discontinuous jump in observed clock speed, even though the target power was swept continuously.

**Breaks when:** you ask what governs *which* point gets chosen. A real power controller uses an engineered heuristic (a lookup table, a control loop) — there's no underlying variational/free-energy principle forcing the choice, and the "unstable" operating point isn't a real mathematically-consistent state of the chip in the way the intermediate black hole branch is a genuine (if never realized) classical solution of Einstein's equations.

**Concept 4 — Scrambling, briefly.** The paper also computes the *scrambling time* — how fast information dropped into the hole gets mixed into degrees of freedom accessible from outside — and finds it scales as $\sim \frac{1}{\kappa}\log S_{BH}$ (surface gravity times log of the entropy). This matches the well-known "fastest scrambler" conjecture (Sekino-Susskind, Hayden-Preskill): a good cryptographic mixing function achieves full avalanche diffusion after only $O(\log N)$ rounds for $N$ bits, not $O(N)$ — black holes are conjectured to saturate that same logarithmic bound, and this calculation reproduces the expected scaling rather than deriving anything new about it.

**Central analogy for this paper:** *information recovery as a switched, cheapest-available encoding.*

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**Step 1 — Get to an effective 2D theory.** Starting from the Kerr-AdS metric (Eq. 2) and a massless scalar field, the authors perform the partial-wave decomposition described above (Concept 2), landing on an effective 2D metric near the horizon (Eq. 8) coupled to a dilaton field $\psi$ and a $U(1)$ gauge field $A_\mu$ that encode the leftover angular-momentum information. Crucially, they then work in the tortoise coordinate and Kruskal coordinates to get a *conformally flat* metric, $ds^2 = -\Omega^2(r)\,dU\,dV$ — this matters because entanglement entropy formulas for a 2D CFT are known in closed form only for this kind of geometry.

**Step 2 — Without an island: the paradox reappears exactly as advertised.** Treating the radiation region as everything far from the horizon and computing its entropy via the standard 2D CFT formula gives, at late times,

$$
S(R) \simeq \frac{c}{3}\,\kappa\, t_b
$$

**Symbol definitions:** $c$ : central charge of the effective 2D CFT (a proxy for the number of field degrees of freedom) · $\kappa$ : surface gravity of the black hole (sets the Hawking temperature, $T = \kappa/2\pi$) · $t_b$ : time at the radiation-collection boundary.

**What this actually means:** the entanglement entropy of the escaped radiation grows *linearly forever*, with no cap. That's exactly Hawking's 1975 result, reproduced here for the rotating case — like a log file that just keeps appending lines with no maximum size, eventually exceeding the disk quota (the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy bound) that a bounded system is never supposed to exceed.

**Step 3 — With one island: the cap appears.** Adding the island contribution and re-extremizing (Eqs. 21–31), at early times ($t_a, t_b \to 0$) no island exists — the extremization returns a trivial/sub-Planckian island location, so early-time behavior is unchanged from Step 2. At late times, extremizing over the island's position and time coordinate forces $t_a = t_b$, and the entropy becomes

$$
S = \frac{2\pi(r_+^2+a^2)}{G\,\Xi} + \mathcal{O}(cG) = 2\,S_{BH}
$$

**Symbol definitions:** $r_+$ : outer horizon radius · $a$ : rotation (spin) parameter · $\Xi \equiv 1 - a^2 l^{-2}$ : a rotation/AdS-radius correction factor · $S_{BH}$ : ordinary Bekenstein-Hawking entropy of the black hole.

**What this actually means:** once the island is included, the entropy of the radiation stops growing and locks onto a fixed value — twice the black hole's own thermodynamic entropy — which is exactly the ceiling an eternal (non-evaporating, two-sided) black hole is allowed to have. It's the equivalent of the monitoring system in the storage analogy above finally noticing that the "recoverable content" estimate has saturated: past a certain point, checking more of the radiation tells you nothing new, because the cheapest correct description now routes through the interior surface instead of growing without bound.

This gives the Page time $t_{\text{Page}} = \frac{3}{c\pi T(r_+)}S_{BH}$ (Eq. 37) — the crossing point between the two curves in Fig. 3 — and the scrambling time discussed in Concept 4 above.

**Step 4 — Map the known Kerr-AdS phase diagram.** Independently of the island calculation, the authors compute the standard canonical-ensemble free energy $F = M - TS$ using the exact Kerr-AdS mass, temperature, and entropy (Eqs. 40–42). Plotting $F$ against $T$ (Fig. 4a) reproduces the textbook swallow-tail below a critical spin $a_c \approx 0.12455$ (AdS radius set to 1): three branches — small black hole (SBH), thermodynamically-unstable intermediate black hole (IBH), and large black hole (LBH) — merging into one smooth branch above $a_c$. This part is a faithful, correctly-executed reproduction of Kerr-AdS thermodynamics that's been known since Caldarelli, Cognola & Klemm (1999–2000); it is not new physics, but it's the necessary setup for what comes next.

**Step 5 — Force the two calculations to talk to each other.** This is the paper's actual novel move. They assume the horizon radius shrinks *linearly in time* during evaporation ($dr_+/dt = -x$, imported wholesale from refs [76]–[77], not derived from their own entanglement calculation), and then ask: as $r_+(t)$ sweeps down through the small/intermediate/large branch structure from Step 4, what does the resulting Page curve, $S = \min\left[\frac{2}{3}\pi T(r_+)\,t,\ 2S_{BH}\right]$ (Eq. 44), look like?

For a large black hole starting point (Fig. 6a), the curve rises, peaks past roughly the halfway point of the assumed evaporation time, and then exhibits a **sharp discontinuity** before dropping to zero — attributed to the imprint of the underlying Hawking-Page-like transition. For an intermediate-branch starting point (Fig. 6b), the peak is lower, the Page time occurs earlier, and the discontinuity is sharper — consistent with the IBH branch being the thermodynamically unstable one. For a small-black-hole starting point (Fig. 6c), where only a single branch exists at low temperature, the curve is smooth throughout, with no discontinuity — exactly as expected once there's no phase transition to imprint anything.

**Step 6 — Repeat with a new ensemble.** They then define a new thermodynamic variable $\zeta \equiv \frac{dM}{da}$ conjugate to the spin parameter, Legendre-transform to a new ensemble ($\tilde M = M - \zeta a$), and redo Steps 4–5. In this ensemble only *two* branches ever appear (Fig. 7a) — no swallow-tail, no first-order transition, just an ordinary (Hawking-Page-type) transition — and correspondingly, the Page curves computed in this ensemble (Fig. 9) are smooth and continuous everywhere, even for the small-black-hole case. The headline conclusion: whether the Page curve shows a discontinuity depends on your choice of statistical ensemble, not just on the black hole itself.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** A reader might assume this calculation captures the full physics of a rotating black hole's evaporation, including the fact that rotating black holes preferentially shed angular momentum via superradiant emission. The paper actually restricts everywhere to the regime $a \ll M$ where ordinary Hawking radiation dominates and superradiance is explicitly neglected (stated plainly in §II and again in the conclusion, which points to ref [70] as the only place a rotating case with superradiance has been worked through, for BTZ specifically). This is honestly flagged by the authors, not hidden — but it does mean the paper's spin-dependence results describe a "slow rotator" limit, not a generic Kerr-AdS black hole.

> **Watch:** A reader might assume the "sharp discontinuity" in Figs. 6a–6b is a genuine dynamical consequence the authors derived from their own island calculation. It's actually produced almost entirely by Step 5's imported *linear* horizon-shrinkage ansatz ($dr_+/dt = -x$, from refs [76]–[77]) being swept mechanically through the *equilibrium* branch structure from Step 4 — including through the intermediate branch, which the paper itself states is thermodynamically unstable (§IV.1). Nothing in the paper derives why (or whether) an evaporating black hole should traverse an unstable equilibrium branch quasi-statically at all; the two calculations (dynamical evaporation, static phase structure) are stitched together by assumption rather than jointly derived.

> **Watch:** A reader familiar with black hole thermodynamics might assume $\zeta$, defined as $dM/da$, is a known or standard quantity — the natural conjugate to angular momentum in the ordinary first law is the horizon angular velocity $\Omega_H$, not this. The paper doesn't cite or engage with the pre-existing "restricted phase space thermodynamics" literature for Kerr-AdS black holes (e.g., [Wei & Liu, 2112.08672](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08672), and follow-ups through 2024–2025) that explores alternative conjugate-variable constructions for rotating AdS thermodynamics, nor does it assign $\zeta$ any independent physical or boundary-CFT meaning — it's introduced purely as a formal Legendre-transform device to get a different ensemble.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Two things stand out, and it's worth being precise about which parts are actually new versus imported wholesale.

**New to the field (as far as I can verify):** applying the island-rule + first-order-phase-transition combination — previously demonstrated only for a *charged*, non-rotating black hole (ref [67], the RN-AdS paper) — to a *rotating* black hole, where the phase transition is spin-driven rather than charge-driven. A companion Kerr paper exists ([Entanglement island and Page curve of Hawking radiation in rotating Kerr black holes, 2406.13949](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13949v1)), but it addresses asymptotically flat Kerr and doesn't fold in a phase transition at all — so the specific combination here (rotating + AdS + phase-transition-aware Page curve) does look like a genuine gap-fill, structurally analogous to how a dozen prior papers each redid the island calculation for one more black hole background (RN, dilaton, charged, warped AdS, accelerating — all cited in this paper's own introduction, refs [26]–[66]).

**New to the reader, and possibly new to the field with an important caveat:** the $\zeta$-ensemble, and the finding that the phase-transition/discontinuity connection is ensemble-dependent. This is a conceptually interesting question (does the *shape* of information recovery know about your choice of statistical ensemble, or only about the black hole's intrinsic geometry?) — but as flagged in the Assumption Audit, I could not confirm this specific construction is unprecedented, since a "restricted phase space" literature already explores alternative rotating-AdS ensembles that this paper neither cites nor distinguishes itself from.

**Predictive Content Check.**

*Falsifiable handle:* This paper relabels/reorganizes existing formalism for a new background rather than making a prediction with an observable and a scale. There is no proposed astrophysical signature, no analog-gravity experiment, nothing checkable against data — it's a self-consistency exercise within semiclassical holography. The nearest thing to a "falsifiable" claim is internal to the formalism itself: a more careful, genuinely dynamical treatment of the evaporation (rather than the imported linear-shrinkage ansatz) could contradict the specific claim that phase transitions produce sharp Page-curve discontinuities — that's the concrete, checkable target for a future paper, not an experiment.

*Formalism load:* Partially load-bearing, unevenly. The island/RT/dimensional-reduction machinery (Steps 1–3) is doing real derivational work — it produces a genuinely non-trivial, checkable result (saturation at $2S_{BH}$) that could have come out wrong. But the headline "phase transitions imprint discontinuities" claim (Step 5) leans on formal apparatus — the equilibrium thermodynamic branch structure — that is *asserted* to apply to a non-equilibrium evaporation trajectory without independent justification. The mathematical machinery of Steps 1–4 is solid; the *bridge* connecting it to the dynamical Page curve is closer to a plausible narrative dressed in real equations than a first-principles derivation.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> Superradiance is entirely neglected; the analysis holds only in the slow-rotation limit $a \ll M$. **(A) Consensus** — this is explicitly stated as a scope-limiting choice by the authors themselves, both at the outset and again in the conclusion as an explicit future-work item. **(paper §II, §V)**

> The near-horizon dimensional reduction to an effective 2D theory, and the restriction to the s-wave sector, is a standard simplification across the entire island literature (needed because computing 4D entanglement entropy directly is intractable), but for a *rotating* geometry specifically, higher partial waves and greybody/transmission effects are generally expected to matter more than for Schwarzschild, and no error estimate is given for dropping them. **(B) Contested** — most of the island literature treats this approximation as an acceptable and even necessary simplification rather than a real defect, but no one has quantified how much of the physics a rotating case specifically loses by it, so reasonable specialists could disagree on how serious this is. **(broader literature)**

No argument is given for why a real evaporation process should move quasi-statically along an unstable equilibrium sequence, and the "linear shrinkage" law itself is imported from generic evaporation literature rather than derived from this paper's own island/entropy calculation. **(C) Speculative** — this is my own inference from reading the derivation chain; the authors don't address the equilibrium/non-equilibrium tension directly, and a specialist in black hole evaporation dynamics may have a standard resolution I'm not aware of. **(analyst inference)**

> Everything here is single-group, unreplicated work in a subfield where cross-checking (other groups redoing the same island calculation for the same background) is the normal path to confidence, not experiment. **(A) Consensus** — this is simply the epistemic status of essentially all "island rule applied to background X" papers, acknowledged as such across the field. **(broader literature)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

The paper sets out to check whether the island-rule resolution of the black hole information paradox — by now demonstrated across dozens of non-rotating backgrounds — survives contact with a rotating, AdS-embedded black hole, and whether a genuine thermodynamic phase transition (which Kerr-AdS black holes are known to have, and non-rotating AdS black holes without charge are not) leaves any trace on the resulting Page curve.

The first half of the paper is essentially reassuring, in the expected way. Starting from the Kerr-AdS metric, the authors perform a near-horizon dimensional reduction — decomposing the field into independent angular modes, the way you'd diagonalize a coupled system into independent eigenmodes — to get a tractable effective 2D theory. Computing the entanglement entropy of the escaped radiation without allowing for an island reproduces Hawking's original problem exactly: the entropy grows without bound and eventually exceeds the black hole's own thermodynamic entropy, violating unitarity. Adding a single island — extremizing the generalized entropy over a candidate surface behind the horizon and picking the smaller of the two competing answers — fixes this: at late times the entropy locks onto twice the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy, reproducing the expected Page curve, complete with a Page time and a scrambling time that match known general expectations (the latter saturating the conjectured "fastest scrambler" logarithmic bound).

The second half is where the paper tries to say something new. Kerr-AdS black holes, like their charged cousins, have a genuine first-order phase transition in the canonical ensemble below a critical spin value — three coexisting solution branches (small, unstable-intermediate, large) that merge above the critical point, exactly analogous to a liquid-gas critical point. This part of the physics is well-established (dating to Caldarelli, Cognola & Klemm around 1999–2000) and the paper reproduces it correctly but doesn't claim it as new. What *is* new is asking what happens to the Page curve when you let an evaporating black hole's horizon radius sweep, over time, through this branch structure: the paper finds that a sharp discontinuity appears in the Page curve exactly when a first-order transition exists in the underlying thermodynamics, and no discontinuity appears when it doesn't (either because the spin is above the critical value, or, more interestingly, because a newly-introduced alternative statistical ensemble — conjugate to the spin rather than treating it as fixed — doesn't support a first-order transition at all). The conceptual punchline the authors want you to take away is that a first-order phase transition leaves a "clear imprint" on the dynamics of information recovery.

This summary is framed to be honest about what's solid versus what's asserted: the island-rule reproduction (Sections II–III of the paper) is careful, technically sound, and unsurprising given the current state of the field. The phase-transition-imprint claim (Section IV) is the interesting, novelty-bearing part, but it rests on gluing together a static equilibrium calculation and a dynamical evaporation ansatz without deriving why that gluing is physically justified — particularly the fact that the "unstable" intermediate branch gets treated as something the black hole passes through in real time.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the physical justification (or lack thereof) for treating the thermodynamically unstable intermediate black hole branch as a quasi-static waypoint in a real-time evaporation trajectory. This is exactly the kind of subtlety where a specialist in black hole evaporation dynamics (as opposed to equilibrium thermodynamics) might have a standard resolution — some existing prescription for how a system moves through an unstable branch during a genuinely time-dependent process — that I'm simply not aware of, and the paper itself doesn't address the tension explicitly enough for me to resolve it from the text alone.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. Even for a spinning black hole embedded in anti-de Sitter space, the "island rule" trick — letting the entanglement calculation reach into the black hole's interior at late times — still fixes Hawking's information paradox exactly the way it has for every other black hole background tried so far: the radiation's entropy stops growing forever and caps out at twice the black hole's own thermodynamic entropy.

2. Kerr-AdS black holes have a genuine liquid-gas-style phase transition (known for 25 years) where, below a critical spin, three distinct black hole solutions can exist at the same temperature — and this paper's real contribution is showing that when a black hole evaporates through that phase structure, the usual smooth Page curve can develop a sharp jump instead, essentially fingerprinting the phase transition onto the information-recovery process.

3. That fingerprint isn't universal — it shows up in the ordinary "fixed spin" ensemble but disappears in a newly-proposed alternative ensemble where a different variable is held fixed instead, meaning whether information seems to come back smoothly or in a jump can depend on a choice about how you're doing the bookkeeping, not just on the black hole itself — though how that stitching between static thermodynamics and dynamical evaporation is actually justified is the part of this paper's argument I'd want to see spelled out more carefully.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Physicists have spent the last several years showing that a once-mysterious fix — letting entanglement calculations "see into" a black hole's interior at late times (the island rule) — successfully resolves Hawking's famous information paradox for one black hole background after another. This paper applies that fix, for the first time in this specific combination, to a *rotating* black hole embedded in anti-de Sitter space (a mathematically convenient universe with a reflecting boundary), rather than the non-rotating cases most prior work has focused on. As expected, the trick works: the entanglement entropy of the escaping radiation, which naively grows forever and violates basic thermodynamic bounds, instead levels off once the interior region is properly accounted for — reproducing the expected "Page curve," the rise-then-plateau shape that any information-preserving evaporation process should follow.

The more interesting part concerns a separate, well-established fact about rotating black holes in this kind of universe: below a critical rotation speed, they can undergo an abrupt, first-order phase transition, similar to how water abruptly boils into steam rather than gradually thinning out. The paper asks what happens to the information-recovery curve if you let an evaporating black hole sweep through that phase transition over time, and finds that the smooth curve develops a sharp jump exactly when the transition is present, and stays smooth when it isn't. It also introduces an alternative way of doing the underlying thermodynamic bookkeeping in which the same black hole shows no phase transition at all — and, correspondingly, no jump in its information-recovery curve.

The honest caveat is that connecting "the black hole passed through a phase transition" (a statement about which configuration is favored in equilibrium) to "the information-recovery curve jumped" (a statement about a real-time, non-equilibrium process) is asserted here more than it's derived — the paper borrows a generic assumption about how fast the black hole shrinks from other work, rather than computing that from its own results, and doesn't fully address why an admittedly *unstable* intermediate configuration should be treated as something the black hole actually passes through. The core information-paradox result stands on solid, well-trodden ground; the phase-transition twist is the genuinely new and more speculative part.

### A. Concept 3 unpacked — why $T(r_+)$ has a hump, and where the swallow-tail actually comes from

The compressed background-section version says "$F(T)$ becomes triple-valued below a critical spin." Rebuilding this from the paper's own equations (Eqs. 40–42), for the worked example $a=0.08$, $l=1$ (matching the paper's own Fig. 4b branch-boundary numbers):

**Why $T(r_+)$ is non-monotonic.** Splitting the temperature formula into three additive, physically distinct pieces —

$$
T(r_+) = \underbrace{\frac{r_+(1+a^2)}{4\pi(a^2+r_+^2)}}_{\text{Term A: ordinary Hawking piece}} + \underbrace{\frac{3r_+^3}{4\pi(a^2+r_+^2)}}_{\text{Term B: AdS confinement}} - \underbrace{\frac{a^2}{4\pi r_+(a^2+r_+^2)}}_{\text{Term C: rotation penalty}}
$$

— and evaluating all three terms numerically across $r_+=0.1$ to $1.0$ (verified by hand to reproduce every $T(r_+)$ value from the direct formula) shows three distinct regimes: near the extremal radius ($r_+\approx0.079$ for this $a$), Term C's steep $-1/r_+$ divergence collapsing toward zero drives a sharp rise in $T$ (the small-black-hole, SBH branch); once Term C has become negligible ($r_+\approx0.18$–$0.53$), a straight tug-of-war between declining Term A and still-small Term B lets $T$ *dip* (the unstable intermediate branch, IBH); past $r_+\approx0.53$, Term B's cubic growth permanently overtakes Term A's $1/r_+$ decline and $T$ climbs for good (the large-black-hole branch, LBH). The two turning points found this way — $r_+=0.1775$ (local max) and $r_+=0.535$ (local min) — landed exactly on the branch boundaries the paper itself states for $a=0.08$, confirming the mechanism.

**Which branch wins — free energy.** Computing $F(r_+)=M-TS$ at the same radii (using Eqs. 40, 42) gives $F_{\text{LBH}} < F_{\text{SBH}} < F_{\text{IBH}}$ near $T\approx0.30$, but $F_{\text{SBH}} < F_{\text{LBH}}$ near $T\approx0.27$ — interpolating the sign flip between these locates the actual first-order transition temperature at $T_c\approx0.29$ for this $a$, the exact analog of the Maxwell-construction crossing in a van der Waals gas. Plotting the sequence of $(T,F)$ pairs in order of increasing $r_+$ traces a path that reverses direction in $T$ twice (rise–fall–rise) while $F$ changes differently — a curve that reverses in one coordinate while still evolving in the other necessarily self-intersects, which *is* the swallow-tail, not merely something that looks like one.

**Stability criterion, concretely.** On the IBH stretch, $r_+$ (hence $M$) is rising while $T$ is falling, so $C=dM/dT<0$ — negative specific heat, i.e. losing energy makes it hotter, a runaway rather than an equilibrium, which is the literal content of "thermodynamically unstable" rather than a judgment call.

**Open boundary of this derivation:** the exact critical value $a_c=0.12455$ (where the dip disappears entirely) requires solving the paper's Eq. 43 — $dT/dr=0$ and $d^2T/dr^2=0$ simultaneously, a genuine cusp/inflection condition — which was not independently re-derived; only the qualitative mechanism (why a critical $a_c$ should exist at all) was reconstructed.

### B. Step 1 unpacked — from the 4D wave equation to a conformally flat 2D metric

**First pass (didn't land):** framed the emergence of a $U(1)$ gauge field from angular-momentum decomposition, and the Kruskal exponential map's regularization of the horizon, primarily through analogies (a per-thread "clock offset" picture for the gauge-field piece; general assertions of divergence-healing for Kruskal). Feedback: didn't work, and specifically the analogy didn't help.

**Second pass (the version worth keeping):** dropped analogy for exact algebra and numbers.

*Honesty flag added:* the paper's Eq. 6 (the mode equation with the emergent gauge-coupling term) is imported from ref. [25] (Jiang & Wu) rather than derived step-by-step in the paper itself or independently re-derived in this discussion — going from Eq. 3 to Eq. 6 also requires a near-horizon approximation on Eq. 3's $\theta$-dependent coefficients that neither source spells out in full. What follows takes Eq. 6 as given and shows *its* structure is recognizable exactly, not approximately.

*The gauge-field identification is an exact algebraic match, not a resemblance.* The textbook equation for a charge-$q$ scalar minimally coupled to a background potential $A_t$ in a static 2D spacetime is

$$
-\frac{1}{f(r)}(\partial_t - iqA_t)^2\Phi + \partial_r(f(r)\partial_r\Phi) = 0
$$

The paper's Eq. 6 bracket is

$$
-\frac{1}{f(r)}\left(\partial_t + i\,\frac{\Xi am}{r^2+a^2}\right)^2\varphi_{lm} + \partial_r(f(r)\partial_r\varphi_{lm}) = 0
$$

These are the identical differential equation with $q\leftrightarrow m$ and $A_t = -\Xi a/(r^2+a^2)$ (matching Eq. 7 exactly) — erasing the physics labels leaves two indistinguishable equations. The azimuthal quantum number $m$ literally plays the role of electric charge once the frame-dragging cross-term in Eq. 3 (present because $Y_{lm}\propto e^{im\phi}$ turns every $\partial_\phi$ into a factor of $im$) is repackaged this way.

*The dilaton, same treatment.* The $(r^2+a^2)/\Xi$ prefactor in Eq. 6 is exactly what survives the angular integration once $\int|Y_{lm}|^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi=1$ eats the angular dependence — calling it $\psi(r)$ and "dilaton" is a naming convention from 2D-gravity (a field multiplying the whole action satisfies that definition by construction), not an analogy.

*Tortoise coordinate, then Kruskal — two different jobs, shown with numbers.* $dr_*=dr/f(r)$ makes the metric manifestly conformally flat, $ds^2=f(r)(dr_*^2-dt^2)$, but $f(r)\to0$ at the horizon makes $r_*\to-\infty$ there — a toy calculation with $\kappa=1$, $r_+=1$ shows $r_*=\tfrac12\ln(r-r_+)$ running to $-\infty$ as $r\to1$ ($r_*=-2.303,\,-3.454,\,-4.605$ at $r=1.01,\,1.001,\,1.0001$), confining this patch to one exterior wedge. Exponentiating, $U=-e^{-\kappa u}$, converts that same divergence into an ordinary approach to zero ($U=-0.100,\,-0.0316,\,-0.0100$ at the same three $r$ values) — the horizon becomes a regular point instead of an unreachable edge, which is what lets $U,V$ range over all four wedges of the Penrose diagram. This global reach is not optional dressing: the entanglement-entropy distance formula (Eq. 13) needs points from *different* wedges (island points behind the horizon, radiation points in the exterior on both sides), and is only well-defined once $U,V$ are genuinely global coordinates — which the tortoise-only patch does not provide.
