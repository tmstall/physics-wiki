## 1. Sort first, then shred: how Enceladus pre-sorts its ocean salts

**Hook:** Cassini's dust detector found that salty ice grains from Enceladus mostly carry *one* salt each, not a pinch of everything. The best explanation is that big ocean droplets freeze slowly deep in the vents, separating their salts the way a freezing brine does, and are then smashed into small pieces against the vent walls on the way out. If that is right, the moon is doing sample preparation for future missions.

---

## 2. Big-picture context

Enceladus, a 500-km moon of Saturn, sprays water vapour and ice grains from fractures near its south pole ("tiger stripes"), fed by a salty ocean under the ice. A few percent of the grains escape and feed Saturn's faint E ring. From 2004 to 2017 Cassini's Cosmic Dust Analyzer (CDA) recorded a mass spectrum for each grain that hit it: a grain-by-grain chemical record of ocean material, with far better statistics in the E ring than in the rare plume passes.

Earlier CDA work sorted grains into three families: Type 1, almost pure ice; Type 2, carrying organics; and Type 3, salt-rich at around 1% salt by mass. The accepted reading since 2009–2011 (largely from this same group) has been that Type 3 grains are flash-frozen droplets of seawater flung up by bursting bubbles. Each would be a tiny sample of the whole ocean, and averaging them gives the ocean's recipe: mostly sodium chloride, then carbonate and bicarbonate, a little potassium, and (since 2023) phosphate.

This paper looks at about 1,000 Type 3 grains individually and finds that averaging hid something: the salts don't travel together. A grain shows chloride *or* carbonate *or* phosphate, rarely two. The paper asks what could sort the salts, tests it with freezing experiments and equilibrium chemistry, and works out what the vents must be like.

**Paper type and stakes:** this is a combined data-reanalysis, laboratory and modelling paper in planetary science. It revises how Type 3 grains form (slow freezing plus fragmentation, not flash freezing), and that changes how future missions should sample the plume: grain by grain, not in bulk.

**Prior Belief Check.** The paper complicates the working picture rather than overturning it. The ocean-spray origin stays; what changes is droplet size (tens to hundreds of micrometres, not a few), freezing speed (slow), and when the small grains form (afterwards, by breakage). Wall collisions themselves were proposed by a co-author in 2008. The surprise for specialists is the implied environment: the plume looks violent from outside, yet the model needs calm lower vents where droplets drift up at about 10 metres per second and cool a few degrees per minute. The authors are revising their own 2009–2011 flash-freezing picture, to their credit.

**Replication & Convergence Note.** Single-team result: the reanalysis, experiments and interpretation come from one collaboration (Free University Berlin and Tokyo, with partners elsewhere). Two inputs are independent: the vent model (Pankine 2023) and the ice-fragmentation thresholds (other groups). Confirmation would mean another team re-classifying the public CDA spectra, a second lab reproducing the segregation with free-floating droplets at low pressure, and eventually a plume mission seeing one salt per grain.

## 3. Background crash-course

### Origins & Big Picture: how a small moon ends up spraying its ocean into space

Enceladus should be a frozen, dead ball. It is too small to have kept the heat from its formation. What keeps it active is Saturn's tide. The moon's orbit is slightly oval, held that way by a gravitational resonance with the larger moon Dione, so each 33-hour orbit Saturn squeezes and relaxes it. That flexing heats the interior through friction, mostly in a porous rocky core and in the ice shell itself. The heat keeps a global ocean liquid under the ice: about 20–30 km of ice on average, thinning to a few kilometres at the south pole, where the heat flow is concentrated.

Water circulating through the warm rocky core reacts with the rock, much like hydrothermal systems on Earth's sea floor. Cassini found the fingerprints: tiny silica grains that only form in hot water (around 90 °C), molecular hydrogen in the plume (a product of water reacting with iron-rich rock), and a high pH. That rock-water chemistry sets the ocean's salt recipe: sodium chloride, a lot of carbonate and bicarbonate, a little potassium, and phosphate at concentrations far above Earth's oceans.

At the south pole the shell is cracked along the tiger stripes. Ocean water rises into these cracks. Because ice floats, water fills a crack to roughly nine-tenths of the way up, so the water surface (the "water table") sits a few hundred metres to perhaps 1.5 km below the ground. At that surface the water sits at about 0 °C at very low pressure, close to the point where liquid, ice and vapour coexist. It evaporates, and gas bubbles rise through it and burst, throwing droplets into the vapour above, the same way breaking waves throw sea spray into Earth's air.

The vapour flows up the crack towards the vacuum above. As it rises it expands, speeds up and cools. Some of it re-condenses on the crack walls, which warms the ice around the vent and keeps the whole system near a steady state. Near the surface the gas reaches the speed of sound and then goes supersonic; Cassini measured jet speeds above 1,000 m/s. The gas carries the droplets and grains with it, but they lag behind the gas and keep bumping into the walls. About 200 kg of vapour per second leaves the moon. Ice grains leave more slowly than the gas, and those faster than the escape speed (about 240 m/s) stay in orbit around Saturn and form the E ring.

Where this paper fits: everything Cassini learned about the ocean's salts came from grains that went through this whole journey. The paper asks what the journey does to the chemistry. The answer is that it acts like a sorter. Slow cooling at the bottom of the vent lets each salt crystallize in its own place inside a big droplet, and violent collisions near the top break the droplet into one-salt pieces. That bears directly on how to read Cassini's data, and on how the next Enceladus missions (an ESA Enceladus mission under study for the 2040s, and NASA's Orbilander concept) should design their dust instruments.

> **Analogy:** The vent works like a two-stage factory line. The lower stage is a slow conveyor through a gently cooling oven; the upper stage is a high-speed chute that ends in a crusher.
>
> **Breaks when:** you think of it as designed or fixed. A real vent changes shape over time (walls erode near the water and narrow near the top as vapour freezes on them), the tide raises and lowers the water by tens of metres each orbit, and the "conveyor speed" depends on that geometry. The paper treats the vent as steady, which is an approximation.

### How the dust detector reads a single grain

The CDA works by impact ionization. A grain hits a metal target at several kilometres per second (here 4 to 16 km/s), vaporizes, and part of the vapour becomes ions. An electric field pulls the positive ions down a 23-cm flight tube; lighter ions arrive first, so arrival time gives mass. The result is one mass spectrum per grain.

Two quirks matter. First, the detector sees only positive ions, so a salt's negative part (chloride, carbonate, phosphate) shows up only inside a positive cluster, such as a sodium ion stuck to a whole sodium chloride molecule. Each salt is identified by its cluster. Second, faster impacts break fragile clusters: the carbonate cluster fades about sixfold from the slowest to the fastest impacts, while the chloride cluster holds up.

> **Analogy:** It is like profiling a program by sampling only the return values that come back with a specific tag attached. You never see the untagged values directly, only the tagged ones, and the sampling rate (impact speed) changes which tags survive.
>
> **Breaks when:** you assume the absence of a tag means the value was absent. In the detector, a missing cluster can mean the salt wasn't there, or that it was there below the noise, or that the impact broke it apart. The paper handles this with thresholds (a peak must be twice the local noise) and speed cuts, but it is a real source of ambiguity.

### Freezing a salty droplet: brine rejection and the precipitation sequence

You know the core chemistry: when salt water freezes, the ice that forms is nearly pure water, and the salt is pushed into the remaining liquid ("brine rejection"). The brine gets saltier and its freezing point drops, until each salt hits its solubility limit and crystallizes out. The new point here is the order.

The authors ran a chemical-equilibrium calculation (with the standard low-temperature brine package) for their ocean analog solution. As it cools, sodium phosphate crystallizes first, at about −1 °C. Sodium carbonate follows from about −4 °C, and sodium bicarbonate from about −20 °C. Last, potassium chloride and then sodium chloride drop out together between about −22.5 and −24 °C, when the last of the liquid freezes. About 90% of the water has already turned to ice by −5 °C.

The key idea: different temperatures mean different *times* during cooling. Cool slowly, and each salt crystallizes, gets walled in by new ice, and the next one crystallizes elsewhere in the shrinking brine. Cool very fast, and there's no time to sort; the droplet can even freeze into a mixed glass.

> **Concept:** In a slowly cooled droplet, each salt crystallizes during its own temperature window, so salts with well-separated windows end up in different places. The degree of separation depends on how much time passes between windows (set by the cooling rate) and on how much room there is (set by droplet size). Salts whose windows overlap (here, potassium and sodium chloride, about 1.5 °C apart) separate poorly. Too-fast cooling produces a uniform mix or a glass.
>
> **Vehicle:** A build pipeline where each stage writes its output to a fresh directory. If stages run one after another with gaps between them, every directory holds one stage's output. If you run all stages at once on a fast clock, everything lands in one shared directory, interleaved. Two stages that start a fraction of a second apart share many files even with a slow clock.
>
> **Analogy:** Temperature window ↔ pipeline stage. Cooling rate ↔ clock speed. Droplet volume ↔ disk space for separate directories. Salt crystal patch ↔ a stage's output directory. Glass formation in flash freezing ↔ all stages dumping into one directory at once. Potassium and sodium chloride ↔ two stages that start nearly together.
>
> **Breaks when:** you treat separation as complete and deterministic. Crystals in a droplet nucleate at random places and grow along ice-crystal boundaries, so the "directories" are patchy, three-dimensional and partly overlapping, and even a slow freeze leaves some mixed regions. The pipeline picture also has no slot for side reactions: the hydroxide the authors see (below) is not in the equilibrium sequence at all.

### Breaking ice grains against a wall

Lab and engineering studies have measured when an ice particle hitting a wall bounces, chips or shatters. The larger the particle, the lower the speed needed. From the paper's Fig. 12: at 100 m/s, particles above roughly 6–9 micrometres start to break and above about 45–50 micrometres they shatter completely. At 300 m/s, near the vent exit, breakage starts below 1 micrometre. Those speeds are normal in the upper vent, so a frozen droplet the size of a fine hair (100 micrometres) would be ground down to the micrometre grains Cassini actually measured.

> **Analogy:** Splitting a large file into fixed-size network packets: any file bigger than the packet size gets chunked, while small files pass unchanged.
>
> **Breaks when:** you assume the cuts are random. Real fractures prefer weak boundaries, and the authors argue the ice-salt boundaries are exactly such weak points, so breakage may follow the salt patches. That would make sorting better, but it has not been measured; the thresholds above are for pure ice.

Read it top to bottom: uniform ocean water becomes sorted only because the slow stage comes before the crushing stage. Reverse their order (crush first, then freeze) and every small grain would freeze as a mixed sample of the ocean, which is the old picture.

**Central analogy for this paper:** sort on a slow ingest, then shard into pages.

## 4. Core technical explanation

**The central analogy in one paragraph.** Think of a database that receives a stream of mixed records. If it ingests them slowly, it has time to build a clustered index, physically grouping records by key on disk. If it then splits the table into small pages, nearly every page holds records with a single key. A query that reads one page at a time sees "only chloride" or "only carbonate". A query that scans the whole table and averages sees the original mix. Slow freezing is the clustered ingest; wall collisions are the paging; the CDA reads one page per grain. **Breaks when:** a database index is complete and exact, while freezing segregation is partial and patchy (some pages always hold two keys), and nothing in a database corresponds to the hydroxide that turns up in almost every grain.

### Step 1: sorting about a thousand salty grains into subtypes

**The failure it prevents:** one averaged spectrum gives the ocean's mean recipe but erases grain-to-grain differences. **What the authors do:** from 7,353 E-ring spectra taken in 2004–2008 (before plume crossings contaminated the detector), they pick 961 salty ones and classify each by which salt clusters rise above twice the local noise, first by potassium, then by anion. **What it buys:** a census of which salts travel together.

The result, trimmed from the paper's Table 1:

| Subtype | What the grain shows | Number | Share of all 961 |
|:--|:--|--:|--:|
| Sodium chloride | chloride (plus hydroxide) | 222 | 23% |
| Sodium carbonate | carbonate (plus hydroxide, sometimes oxide) | 224 | 23% |
| Hydroxide only | sodium hydroxide, no other salt | 160 | 17% |
| Uncertain | hydroxide only, but too noisy to tell | 231 | 24% |
| Chloride + carbonate mixed | both | 12 | 1.2% |
| Sodium phosphate | phosphate (plus hydroxide) | 9 | 0.9% |
| Potassium chloride, no sodium | potassium chloride and hydroxide | 20 | 2.1% |
| Potassium + sodium mixed | both alkali metals | 42 | 4.4% |
| Irregular | distorted spectra | 41 | 4.3% |

*Table 1 (from the paper), trimmed.* **What to look at:** chloride and carbonate grains are equally common (about 23% each), yet only 12 grains carry both. The potassium group is the one place where mixing is common: two-thirds of potassium-rich grains also carry sodium.

Two details matter later. Almost every grain shows sodium or potassium hydroxide alongside its main salt; exactly one grain in the whole set lacks it. And potassium only ever appears as a chloride, never as a carbonate or phosphate, while sodium appears with all four anions.

### Step 2: separating composition from impact speed

**The failure it prevents:** faster impacts break fragile clusters, so "chloride" and "carbonate" grains might be one material seen at different speeds. **What the authors do:** they estimate each grain's speed (from Cassini's orbit, plus a bare hydrogen peak that only appears above about 10 km/s), bin by speed, and track peak ratios and subtype shares. **What it buys:** confidence the subtypes are compositional. The carbonate cluster fades about sixfold from slowest to fastest, the chloride cluster holds, and subtype shares stay mostly flat below 10 km/s. Above that, many "uncertain" grains become "hydroxide only", as stronger signals lift them over the noise threshold.

### Step 3: is the separation real or chance?

**The failure it prevents:** with chloride and carbonate each in roughly a third of the classifiable sodium grains, some overlap would happen by chance, and a small overlap could be luck. **What the authors do:** they build a 2-by-2 table (chloride yes/no against carbonate yes/no) over the 627 sodium grains that can be classified, leaving out the noisy and irregular ones, and test it. **What it buys:** if the two salts were independent, about 88 grains should carry both; 12 do. Carbonate is about 25 times less likely in a grain that has chloride. The chance of this split arising from independent salts is far below one in ten billion. Phosphate is even stricter: none of its 9 grains shows chloride or carbonate, where about 7 would be expected by chance.

### Step 4: ruling out separate water sources

**The failure it prevents:** the simplest alternative is that different vents tap different pockets of water with different salts. **What the authors do:** they argue from geochemistry. Sodium and potassium both stay dissolved during water-rock reactions, and sodium is far more abundant than potassium in the solar system, so a potassium-rich, sodium-poor water body cannot form. Partial freezing of water inside cracks shifts ion ratios but never makes a brine dominated by carbonate or by potassium; earlier freezing simulations of Enceladus water never produced one. **What it buys:** the sorting must happen inside individual droplets, after they leave a fairly uniform ocean.

### Step 5: freezing droplets in the lab

**The failure it prevents:** "slow freezing separates salts" must be shown at the scale of Cassini's 1–2 micrometre grains. **What the authors do:** they freeze droplets of an ocean analog (concentrations estimated for Enceladus, phosphate doubled to show up in maps), 15, 200 and 1,000 micrometres across, on a cold steel plate at 1, 5, 10 and 20 K per minute and by plunging into liquid nitrogen. They freeze-dry them, leaving the salt skeleton, and map elements with an electron microprobe at 0.4-micrometre resolution. **What it buys:** a direct picture of where each salt ends up.

![Figure 6](figures/enceladus-aee7256_fig6.jpg)

*Figure 6 (from the paper): element maps of freeze-dried 200-micrometre droplets, one row per cooling rate (1, 5, 10, 20 K/min and flash freezing); columns show sodium, potassium, carbon (carbonate), chlorine, oxygen and phosphorus; scale bars 50 micrometres.* **What to look at:** the chlorine column. At 5 and 10 K/min chlorine sits in a few sharp hot spots that match the potassium spots, while carbon fills the space between them. At 20 K/min and flash freezing, chlorine is spread evenly across the droplet. Slow cooling sorts; fast cooling doesn't.

The authors quantify this with a histogram of the carbonate-to-chloride ratio in every sodium-rich pixel.

![Figure 7](figures/enceladus-aee7256_fig7.jpg)

*Figure 7 (from the paper): fraction of sodium-rich pixels at each carbonate-to-chloride ratio (logarithmic axis; zero means equal amounts, as in the starting solution), for 200-micrometre droplets at five cooling rates.* **What to look at:** flash freezing (grey) is a single tall peak near zero: almost every pixel holds both salts in the starting proportions. At 1 and 5 K/min (pink, purple) the middle sags and both ends rise: pixels are either carbonate-rich or chloride-rich. The 10 K/min curve (green) sits in between and is only weakly two-humped, so the real threshold looks closer to 5–10 K/min than a clean 10.

Size matters too: 15-micrometre droplets barely separate even at the slowest rate, for lack of room. The droplets flattened 4:1 on the plate, so a 200-micrometre droplet holds the volume of a sphere about 125 micrometres across.

### Step 6: potassium and hydroxide set further limits

**The failure it prevents:** slower isn't automatically better, since Cassini sees potassium only as a chloride. **What the authors do:** they track potassium. At 5 and 10 K/min it sits in the chloride patches; at 1 K/min it moves into the carbonate patches (potassium carbonate, never seen by Cassini), and for millimetre droplets the switch starts at 10 K/min. **What it buys:** a window: slower than about 20 K/min to sort, faster than about 1 K/min to keep potassium out of the carbonate.

Hydroxide is the awkward ingredient: the equilibrium calculation never makes it. In slowly frozen droplets, though, sodium-and-oxygen spots appear around voids the authors read as escaped carbon dioxide bubbles; losing carbon dioxide makes brine more alkaline. Flash-frozen droplets show none. This is a lead, not a demonstration, and hydroxide also forms away from the voids.

### Step 7: the equilibrium precipitation sequence

**The failure it prevents:** lab maps show where salts went but not why in that pattern. **What the authors do:** they compute the equilibrium salt assemblage for the same solution while cooling in small steps. **What it buys:** a timing chart to match against Cassini's subtypes.

![Figure 10](figures/enceladus-aee7256_fig10.jpg)

*Figure 10 (from the paper): equilibrium freezing of the analog solution from +5 to −24 °C. Top: total amount of each solid (log scale). Second: how fast each solid forms per degree. Third: pH of the remaining liquid. Bottom: percentage of water still liquid.* **What to look at:** in the second panel, the red phosphate spike near −1 °C, the cyan carbonate spike near −4 °C, then nothing new until the potassium (purple) and sodium chloride (green) spikes crowd together at the far right, around −23 °C. Phosphate comes out alone, carbonate comes out with only a trickle of phosphate, and the two chlorides come out together. That maps onto Cassini: pure phosphate grains, carbonate grains without visible phosphate, and potassium that often comes with sodium chloride. Potassium chloride starting slightly first leaves room for the 20 potassium-only grains.

### Step 8: can a vent cool droplets that slowly?

**The failure it prevents:** a droplet in a fast gas flow would normally freeze in seconds, far too fast to sort. **What the authors do:** they show a droplet can't drift from its surroundings' temperature: a 200-micrometre droplet one degree too warm sheds that degree by evaporation in a fraction of a second (radiation would take a minute). The droplet is locked to the gas, the gas to the wall, so its cooling rate is set by how fast it travels through the vent's temperature gradient. **What it buys:** a way to compute the cooling rate from the vent's structure.

This is the one equation the argument rests on:

$$\frac{dT}{dt} \;=\; \frac{dT}{dz}\,\times\,v$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\frac{dT}{dt}$ : cooling rate the droplet experiences (K per second)
- $\frac{dT}{dz}$ : how much colder the vent wall gets per metre of height (K per metre)
- $v$ : how fast the droplet rises (metres per second)

**What this actually means:** the droplet is like a data packet passing through a chain of routers, each with its own clock offset. The packet's timestamp error grows at the rate (offset change per hop) times (hops per second). Slow travel through a shallow gradient means slow drift; fast travel through a steep gradient means fast drift. **Breaks when:** the droplet's own latent heat or internal conduction can't keep up. Then it lags the gas instead of tracking it; the authors' estimate assumes perfect tracking.

They then take a published steady-state vent model (Pankine 2023) for a 1,500-metre crack, where wall temperature follows a balance of vapour condensing on the walls, conduction through the ice and radiation from the surface.

![Figure 11](figures/enceladus-aee7256_fig11.jpg)

The hatched band marks cooling below 20 K/min.* **What to look at:** the orange curve stays inside the hatched band from the bottom up to roughly 350–400 m depth, about the lowest three-quarters of the vent, while the gas creeps along at about 10 m/s and the temperature falls only a few degrees. Then, in the top couple of hundred metres, speed and cooling rate shoot up together.

The droplets don't have to finish freezing slowly, the authors note: they only need to cool slowly until phosphate and carbonate are out, after which the leftover chloride brine can freeze fast. A sudden pressure drop at that stage would drive carbon dioxide out of the brine and raise its pH just as the chlorides crystallize, which could be why hydroxide rides with chloride.

### Step 9: smashing frozen droplets into Cassini-sized pieces

**The failure it prevents:** sorted droplets are tens to hundreds of micrometres across, but Cassini measured 1–2 micrometre grains, and the plume has almost none above 10. **What the authors do:** they compare upper-vent gas speeds (hundreds of m/s) with measured ice-fragmentation thresholds; grains lag the gas in a crooked crack, so each hits the walls several times. **What it buys:** a way to make small one-salt grains that also explains the plume's size cutoff and non-spherical grains.

![Figure 12](figures/enceladus-aee7256_fig12.jpg)

*Figure 12 (from the paper): impact speed needed to fragment pure crystalline ice particles against their diameter (both log scales). Red line: onset of partial breakage. Blue line: complete shattering. Solid parts are measured; dashed parts are extrapolated. Crosses are independent sub-micrometre measurements. Grey and black boxes mark 100 and 300 m/s.* **What to look at:** follow the 300 m/s black line: breakage starts below about 1 micrometre. Anything the size of the parent droplets (tens of micrometres and up) is far above both lines and would be shattered.

Salt-ice boundaries should be weak points, so real breakage may need less speed. Subtypes have similar sizes, which fits random breakage; the mixed potassium-sodium group leans larger, as expected if bigger fragments more often hold two salt patches.

### Step 10: what it means for reading the plume

**The failure it prevents:** sorted grains might invalidate ocean-composition estimates from Type 3 grains. **What the authors do:** they argue the *ensemble* still carries the ocean's salt ratios, while absolute concentrations become less certain (fragments may not keep the parent's salt fraction, and all pick up condensed ice). **What it buys:** rare ingredients (rarer than phosphate, in 9 of over 7,300 grains) should appear in very few grains at high concentration, so future missions need single-grain instruments; bulk melting would undo the sorting. Organics could be sorted the same way.

### Claim check

Checks done in Python (scipy for the contingency tests; pixel-colour digitization of Fig. 11 from the downscaled image), plus hand arithmetic for the droplet volume.

**Chloride–carbonate exclusivity: Reproduced.** From the supplement's table, independence predicts 88 mixed grains against 12 observed, carbonate is 24.5 times less likely when chloride is present, and the exact test's p-value is far below the paper's "less than 10 to the minus 10". (The "88" uses the 627 classifiable grains; over all 858 regular grains it would be 64, still five times the observed count.)

**Subtype counts and shares: Reproduced.** The Table 1 rows sum to 899 sodium-rich, 858 regular, 62 potassium-rich and 961 in total, and the pie-chart percentages in Fig. 5 match the counts.

**Slow-cooling window in the vent model: Partly reproduced.** From the digitized Fig. 11, cooling stays below 20 K/min up to about 365 m depth (76% of the vent, matching "lowermost 75%"), for about 105 seconds (matching "about 1 minute" minimum). But the text says slow cooling "is maintained until the gas temperature drops below 255 K". The curves end the slow zone near 262 K (about −11 °C); the gas reaches 255 K only around 160 m depth, where cooling already exceeds 40 K/min. Slow cooling therefore covers phosphate and most carbonate, not the bicarbonate step near −20 °C. The paper's fallback (only phosphate and carbonate must precipitate slowly) keeps the mechanism alive; the "until 255 K" match is overstated by about 7 K.

**Droplet-size equivalence: Reproduced.** A 200-micrometre droplet flattened 4:1 has the volume of a sphere about 126 micrometres across, matching the paper's "roughly 120" and "about 40% smaller".

**Sensitivity check — vent length.** The 1,500-m vent is the most influential input, and the supplement itself says buoyancy alone puts the water table only about 500 m down, with 1,500 m possible if the ice props itself up. Squeeze the same profile into a vent one-third as long and every cooling rate triples: the slow zone ends near −5 °C, with phosphate out but only about half the carbonate. The conclusion survives for water tables deeper than roughly 1 km and turns marginal near 500 m, unless the vent bottom is wider and slower than modelled (as the authors argue tidal erosion would make it, without modelling it).

**Robustness test — alternative explanations for exclusivity.** *Impact-speed artefacts* → would show as subtype shares drifting with speed → they mostly don't below 10 km/s, and chloride is the speed-stable cluster, so speed can't turn carbonate grains into chloride grains. *Noise thresholds* → mixed grains hiding among noisy ones → the noisy grains are excluded from the test. *Ionization suppression* → one salt hiding the other's cluster, making mixed grains look pure → the 12 mixed grains show both clusters *can* appear together, so suppression isn't total; whether a minor salt at a tenth of the main one would still show is untested. This could inflate the exclusivity somewhat but not create it.

**Internal consistency — significant findings.** (1) *The "until 255 K" claim* overstates the vent-model match by about 7 K (above), which changes which salts fall inside the slow window. (2) *The sodium–potassium significance test is not meaningful as run.* Its table (supplement Table S5) has a cell that is zero by construction, since every Type 3 grain has sodium or potassium, and "sodium present" is really a peak-ratio threshold. The quoted p-value is an artefact of that setup. The separation is visible in the counts, but should rest on the distribution of sodium-to-potassium ratios. Neither finding touches the chloride–carbonate result. ### Assumption audit

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes each subtype is one salt mineral sitting in the grain. **The paper actually says** the detector sees only positive clusters, hydroxide shows up in essentially every grain partly because alkaline salts make hydroxide clusters on impact, and the second-largest "single-salt" class, hydroxide-only (17% of grains), is not produced by the equilibrium chemistry at all. Another quarter of the grains can't be classified. The clean story covers the chloride, carbonate, phosphate and potassium groups, about half the data.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the lab droplets reproduce what Cassini saw. **The paper actually says** the match is qualitative. In the experiments, more than 10% of pixels hold both chloride and carbonate even at the best cooling rate, against under 3% of Cassini's classifiable grains. The authors argue their maps underestimate separation (two-dimensional view of flattened droplets frozen from below, pixel size larger than the salt patches in a real grain), and a glass-plate test does improve separation, but the gap has not been closed. "Improved experiments are already in progress."

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the vent model shows Enceladus' vents cool droplets slowly. **The paper actually says** it borrows a one-dimensional steady-state model for a 1,500-m vent that its own supplement calls an upper estimate. It shows such a vent *can* provide the needed window, not that real vents do; vent length, width and flow structure are unmeasured.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes sorted grains make earlier ocean-composition estimates wrong. **The paper actually says** salt *ratios* from averaging many grains should still be fine; it is the absolute concentrations that become less certain, because fragments may not keep the parent's salt-to-water ratio and they pick up condensed ice.

## 5. What's genuinely new or clever

**1. Reading grains one at a time instead of averaging them.** The spectra aren't new (the 2023 phosphate paper used them). The move is to ask what travels with what, the same shift as going from a mean latency to asking which requests share a slow path. It also recovered the potassium-rich grains, which earlier work hadn't recognized as salty. New to the field, not just to the reader.

**2. Using the vent's own thermal structure as the clock.** A droplet can't set its own cooling rate: evaporation locks it to the gas, the gas to the wall, so it cools exactly as fast as it travels through the wall's temperature gradient. That turns an impossible-looking requirement (a calm, slow freeze inside an erupting geyser) into a question about the vent's temperature profile, and a slow-bottom, fast-top vent reconciles slow freezing with the violent jets. Wall collisions were proposed in 2008; pairing them with segregation inside large parent droplets is new.

A smaller point: the paper turns a chemistry result into an instrument requirement. If nature has already separated the ingredients, a single-grain analyser sees each one concentrated, and a bulk sampler throws that away.

### Predictive content check

**Falsifiable handle.** The paper is partly retrodictive: the subtype split was observed first and the mechanism was built to explain it. It does make several forward predictions that could fail:

- A future single-grain instrument at Enceladus should find the same split: chloride, carbonate and phosphate grains mostly separate, chloride and carbonate each around a quarter of salty grains, mixed grains only a few percent.
- Mixing should rise with grain size: larger fragments are more likely to hold two salt patches. The potassium-sodium group already leans this way.
- Any rare ingredient (below phosphate's abundance) should show up in a tiny fraction of grains but at high concentration, not as a faint trace in many.
- Free-floating, spherical droplets of a few hundred micrometres, frozen at 5–20 K/min at low pressure, should push the mixed fraction from the current lab value of over 10% down towards Cassini's under 3%. If improved experiments stall at 10%, the mechanism is in trouble or something else (such as detection effects) is contributing.
- Plume grains should be irregular fragments, not spheres.

**Formalism load:** not triggered; the paper's few equations (impact chemistry, a grain-drag estimate, the vent model's heat balance) all feed specific numbers.

## 6. Limitations and open questions

**Lab geometry differs from the vent.** Droplets were flattened on a steel plate, frozen mostly from below, in air at 1 atmosphere, and mapped only from above; Enceladus droplets are free-floating spheres freezing inward in near-vacuum vapour. **(A) Consensus** — the paper lists these differences and calls its segregation a lower limit, standard for first-generation analog work. **(paper, Experimental limitations section)**

**The lab still mixes salts more than Cassini sees.** Over 10% of pixels hold both chloride and carbonate at the best rate, against under 3% of Cassini's grains. **(B) Contested** — the authors' explanation (two-dimensional maps, limited resolution) is plausible and the glass-plate test supports it, but kinetics or detection effects could also fill the gap, and it is untested. **(paper, supplement S2.1; analyst inference)**

**The slow-cooling window rests on one borrowed vent model at its long end.** The 1,500-m vent is the upper end of the supplement's own range (buoyancy alone gives about 500 m). My digitization ends the slow zone near −11 °C rather than the implied −18 °C; a vent one-third as long would end it near −5 °C, with about half the carbonate out. **(C) Speculative** — from digitizing and rescaling Fig. 11, not from my own vent model; a wider, slower vent bottom could restore the window. **(analyst inference; paper supplement S3.1)**

**Hydroxide is unexplained.** It is in essentially every grain and forms a stand-alone class, yet equilibrium chemistry never makes it; the carbon-dioxide-escape idea rests on spots near voids, and the missing potassium-hydroxide class is "very poorly understood". **(A) Consensus** — stated plainly by the authors. **(paper, Discussion item 4; supplement Fig. S8)**

**Detection limits for mixed grains are untested.** No lab analog shows whether a minor salt at a tenth of the main one would still give a visible cluster; if not, some "pure" grains are mixed. **(C) Speculative** — the 12 mixed grains show suppression isn't total; the size of the effect is my concern, not the paper's. **(analyst inference)**

**The sodium–potassium significance test is set up incorrectly.** Its table has a cell that is zero by construction, so the p-value means little; the separation shows in the counts but isn't established statistically. **(C) Speculative** — my reading of supplement Table S5; it doesn't touch the chloride–carbonate result. **(analyst inference)**

**The chemistry model is simplified.** No kinetics, contact with Earth-like air, phosphate at twice the Enceladus upper estimate, and carbonate timing very sensitive to pH and the carbonate-to-chloride ratio. **(B) Contested** — the authors flag kinetics and ratio sensitivity; the air-contact point (a vent's own carbon dioxide would differ) is mine. **(paper, Thermodynamic calculations section; analyst inference)**

**Big parent droplets and weak salt-ice boundaries are inferred, not measured.** The droplet size rests on another group's scaling estimates; easier breakage at salt-ice boundaries is argued, not shown. **(B) Contested** — both reasonable, but each could shift the size requirements a lot. **(paper, Discussion; broader literature)**

**One team, one early slice of data.** Spectra from 2004–2008 only, classified by one team; experiments from one lab. **(A) Consensus** — the standard status of a first result. **(paper, Methods; analyst inference)**

**Next 12–24 months:** levitated-droplet experiments at low pressure; lab spectra of mixed-salt grains to calibrate detection limits; an independent reclassification of the public CDA spectra; a vent model with realistic geometry and condensation; and the same test for organics.

## 7. Detailed summary and explanation

**What the paper found:**

- Among 961 salty E-ring grains, salts mostly travel separately. Chloride and carbonate grains are equally common (about a quarter each), but only 12 carry both, where chance would give about 88. Phosphate grains never carry either. Potassium appears only as a chloride, usually alongside sodium chloride. Hydroxide accompanies nearly every grain, and one grain in six carries hydroxide alone.
- Impact speed and separate water sources don't explain this; the sorting happens inside droplets of a uniform ocean.
- Lab droplets a few hundred micrometres across sort their salts when cooled at about 5–10 K per minute, stay mixed at 20 K per minute or faster, and misplace potassium below about 1 K per minute. Tiny droplets barely sort at all.
- Equilibrium chemistry gives an order (phosphate, carbonate, then both chlorides together at the end) that matches the grain types.
- In a model vent, evaporation locks droplets to the wall temperature, so they cool slowly for the first one to two minutes, then very fast; wall collisions near the top shatter them into one-salt pieces.
- Salt ratios from averaging many grains still hold; absolute concentrations are less certain, and rare ingredients will be concentrated in few grains.

**Why the summary is framed this way.** The three threads have very different strength, and I kept them apart on purpose. The spacecraft result is solid: I reproduced its statistics, and the alternatives I tested don't explain it away. The lab result is real but qualitative, cleaner in space than on the bench. The vent stage is a plausibility argument on a borrowed model at its favourable end, and its fine print is weaker than the text says. So I read the mechanism as "shown possible and consistent", not "shown to happen". The central analogy, sorting on a slow ingest and then sharding into pages, carries the logic: the order of the stages is what matters. Shard first and freeze afterwards, and every grain would be a mixed miniature of the ocean, which is the old picture. The takeaway is a shift in how to read Enceladus data: a salty grain is a fragment of the ocean, not a sample of it.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** my two "significant findings" rest on my own reading rather than the paper's statements. The 7-K gap in the slow-cooling window comes from digitizing curves in a downscaled image (about ±1–2 K of reading error, and I may be misreading which temperature the authors meant by "until 255 K", gas or droplet, though the text says gas). The critique of the sodium–potassium test is a statistical judgement about a supplementary table. I also did not view the coadded spectra (Figs. 1–4) or the supplementary figures as images, so I am trusting the captions on what each cluster looks like.

**Revision notes:** the referee pass added the speed-artefact and detection-threshold cases to the robustness test (including that the 12 mixed grains rule out total suppression), weakened the 10 K/min threshold to "5–10" after re-reading Fig. 7, added the vent-length sensitivity from the supplement's own 500-m estimate, added a Breaks-when line to the equation analogy, and cut about 1,200 words to get back under the length ceiling.

## 8. Three crystallized takeaways

1. **Enceladus' salty ice grains mostly carry one salt each.** Chloride grains and carbonate grains are equally common, but almost never the same grain, so each grain is a fragment of the ocean, not a miniature copy of it.

2. **The vents may freeze ocean spray slowly, then smash it.** Droplets drift up through a gently cooling crack, sorting their salts as they freeze over a minute or two, before a supersonic upper section shatters them into one-salt pieces. That this is how real vents work is shown to be possible, not yet demonstrated.

3. **Nature is doing sample preparation for the next mission.** Rare ocean ingredients should be concentrated in a few grains rather than diluted in all of them, so a probe that analyses grains one at a time has a big advantage over one that collects and melts them in bulk.

## 9. Shorter summary

Saturn's moon Enceladus sprays material from its underground ocean into space, and some of it ends up in Saturn's E ring as tiny ice grains. Cassini's dust detector measured the chemistry of grains one at a time. The salt-rich ones were long thought to be flash-frozen droplets of seawater, each a miniature sample of the ocean.

This paper looked at about a thousand of those salty grains individually and found that their salts mostly don't mix. Sodium chloride and sodium carbonate grains are equally common, yet only about 1% of grains contain both, where chance would give roughly seven times as many. Phosphate grains contain neither, and potassium appears only as a chloride.

To explain this, the authors froze droplets of ocean-like brine in the lab. Droplets a few hundred micrometres across, cooled slowly (around 5 to 10 degrees per minute), separate their salts into small patches, because each salt crystallizes at a different temperature. Fast freezing leaves them mixed. A model of the moon's vents suggests droplets near the water surface drift up slowly and cool at just this kind of rate for a minute or two. Near the top, the gas speeds up to hundreds of metres per second, and collisions with the walls would shatter the frozen droplets into micrometre pieces, each mostly carrying one salt.

The spacecraft statistics are solid. The lab sorting is real but less complete than in space, and the vent stage relies on a borrowed model at its favourable end. It shows the mechanism is possible, not yet that it happens.

If correct, it changes how to sample Enceladus. Averages over many grains still give the ocean's salt ratios, but rare ingredients, possibly including organics, will be concentrated in a few grains. Future missions should analyse grains one by one rather than melting them together.
