# 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**When AI Populations Invent Their Own Language — And Rig the Vote**

Put a crowd of language models in a room with no leader, no ballot, and no shared vocabulary, and within about fifteen rounds of paired conversation they'll spontaneously agree on a common word — but the word they land on is quietly biased in ways none of them show individually, and a coordinated sliver as small as 2% of the population can flip everyone else's answer.

---

# 2. Big-Picture Context

Multi-agent LLM systems are moving from novelty to infrastructure fast — negotiation bots, customer-service swarms, simulated-society research tools, and autonomous agents that talk mostly to *each other* rather than to a human. Almost all AI-safety evaluation, though, still happens at the level of a single model answering a single prompt. This paper asks a different question: when you stop testing one agent and start watching what a whole *population* of interacting agents does, does anything show up that individual testing would never catch?

The experimental paradigm isn't new — it's borrowed close to unmodified from statistical-physics and complex-systems research on human language and convention formation. The "naming game" (Baronchelli and collaborators, mid-2000s) models how a population agrees on an arbitrary shared label through nothing but repeated, local, pairwise interactions — no central authority, no global visibility, just win-stay/lose-shift feedback accumulating over many small conversations. A companion strand of that same literature — "critical mass" or "committed minority" dynamics — asks how stable an established consensus really is: insert a small subgroup that stubbornly refuses to go along with the majority, and past some threshold size the whole population can tip to the minority's answer (this is the same family of ideas behind Centola et al.'s 2018 human-subject finding that roughly 25% committed dissenters can flip an established social convention in a real social network). This paper's move is to swap the human participants in that decades-old experimental design for LLM agents and see whether the physics still holds — and whether anything LLM-specific shows up.

The closest relative in this pipeline's own reading is De Marzo, Castellano & Garcia's *Science Advances* paper on LLM-agent majority-following (analyzed 2026-08-18), which maps a different multi-agent LLM phenomenon onto a different physics model — a Curie-Weiss mean-field ferromagnet, asking whether and at what population size N a group of agents following a majority-influence rule locks into consensus. That paper's question is essentially "can they reach consensus, and how does the threshold scale with N?" This paper's question is "once a population *does* converge, what exactly did it converge to, is that outcome fair, and how fragile is it?" — consensus formation is the starting assumption here, not the finding being tested. Both papers use the same journal, the same "borrow a statistical-physics model, run LLMs through it" move, and the same underlying worry (safety properties of populations aren't the same as safety properties of individuals) — but they are asking genuinely different questions of genuinely different game structures (majority-influence cascade vs. pairwise naming-game coordination), so this is not independent replication of the same result; it's converging use of the same research strategy from two different angles.

**Paper Type & Stakes.** This is a peer-reviewed, experimental/simulation-based *Science Advances* Research Article applying an established statistical-physics experimental paradigm to populations of LLM agents; the stakes are squarely AI-safety-relevant — it demonstrates a category of group-level behavior (bias that appears only in aggregate, and consensus that a small hostile minority can hijack) that no amount of single-model red-teaming would surface.

**Prior Belief Check.** Mixed. Spontaneous convergence to a shared convention is the *expected* result if the naming-game literature transfers to LLMs at all — it would be more surprising if it *didn't* converge, given how strongly next-token prediction rewards locally coherent, pattern-continuing behavior. The genuinely surprising piece, flagged as counterintuitive even to specialists in this space, is the dissociation between individual and collective bias: agents that show no statistically detectable preference when queried in isolation can still produce a population that lands overwhelmingly on one answer. That result directly undercuts a common implicit assumption in AI evaluation — that testing a model's outputs one prompt at a time is sufficient to characterize its fairness properties. The critical-mass finding replicates the qualitative shape of the human result (small minorities can tip majorities) but the reported range is startling on the low end: 2% is an order of magnitude below the ~25% threshold found in the comparable human-subject experiment, which is worth flagging as a genuinely unexpected quantitative divergence from the human benchmark this paradigm was built to mirror.

**Replication & Convergence Note.** This is a single-group result (Ashery, Aiello & Baronchelli — City St George's, University of London / IT University of Copenhagen). Testing four different LLM families within the same paper (Llama-2-70b-Chat, Llama-3-70B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, Claude-3.5-Sonnet) functions as a partial internal robustness check — and the fact that results vary enormously across those four models is itself informative — but there is no independent replication yet by an outside lab, and none of the current frontier model generations (GPT-4/5-class, Gemini, newer Claude or Llama releases) were tested. Independent confirmation would look like a different research group re-running the identical protocol on the same model families plus additional ones and reporting comparable convergence timing, bias direction, and critical-mass thresholds; a specific hazard is that any of these numbers could be an artifact of the particular prompt template used, which the paper itself flags as a bounding parameter (see Section 6).

# 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

## The naming game

Two agents are picked at random from a population and each independently proposes a "name" — some arbitrary label — for a shared object neither has a prior convention for. If their names match, both are rewarded; if not, both are penalized and move on to the next random pairing. Repeat this thousands of times across the whole population and, remarkably, the group as a whole converges on a single dominant name — even though no agent ever sees the population's overall state, only its own private history of past pairings.

**Analogy:** this is structurally identical to *eventually-consistent gossip protocols* in distributed systems — a set of nodes, each with only its own local interaction log, periodically compares state pairwise with a random peer and reconciles, with no coordinator and no node ever seeing the global picture. Enough rounds of pairwise gossip and the whole system converges on one shared value, purely from local anti-entropy exchanges.

**Breaks when:** you ask *which* value wins. Real gossip protocols are engineered with an explicit, deterministic tiebreaker — last-writer-wins timestamps, vector clocks, a designated authoritative source — so the converged value is knowable in advance from the protocol's rules. The naming game has no such built-in tiebreaker. Which name the population lands on is genuinely path-dependent, decided by the accumulated luck of early random pairings, not by any rule you could read off the protocol beforehand. Push the gossip analogy toward "predict the outcome from the spec" and it stops working — that's precisely the mechanism this paper is exploiting to show *bias* creeping in through the back door.

## Individual bias vs. collective (emergent) bias

An agent asked once, "which of these two options do you prefer," might show no statistically detectable preference at all. But when a whole population of such agents interacts repeatedly, and small early asymmetries get reinforced round after round, the population's *aggregate* outcome can be wildly lopsided — even though the ingredient that produced it (individual preference) tested clean.

**Analogy:** cache/CDN traffic warm-up. The very first requests to a set of otherwise-identical cache replicas are, for practical purposes, randomly distributed across them. Tiny, untraceable timing differences mean one replica happens to get slightly more hits early on; more hits keep it "warm" (faster, more likely to be chosen by a load balancer favoring low latency), which drives still more traffic its way — a positive-feedback loop that ends with one replica dominating, even though no single request was ever routed there "because" of the eventual outcome.

**Breaks when:** in a real cache system, you can usually trace the seed asymmetry back to something measurable — a latency spike, an unevenly weighted load balancer, a physically closer data center. In this paper's isolation test, round-one preference is statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip for at least some of the model/convention pairs tested — so unlike a traceable cache hotspot, the seed of the eventual lopsided outcome isn't visible in the quantity being measured at all. It emerges from the *accumulation dynamics themselves*, not from a locatable individual-level asymmetry — which is the paper's central, more unsettling point.

## Critical mass / committed minority

A majority consensus, once established, isn't infinitely stable. Insert a small subgroup of "committed" agents who ignore the majority entirely and always push a fixed alternative, no matter what happens to them in any given interaction — and past some threshold fraction of the population, the *whole group* can flip to the minority's answer, even though the minority never becomes a numerical majority itself.

**Analogy:** a Byzantine-fault / poisoning attack on a leaderless gossip-consensus network — a small number of nodes that always broadcast a fixed, non-adaptive value regardless of what they hear from peers. Below some fraction of the network they simply get out-voted and averaged away; above a threshold fraction, they can drag the entire network's eventual-consistency convergence point to their value.

**Breaks when:** classical Byzantine fault-tolerant systems are *engineered* around a formally provable threshold (e.g., you need more than one-third malicious nodes to break consensus in textbook BFT) — a single clean safety margin you can design around. This paper's thresholds are not designed constants; they're empirically measured and vary enormously — by more than 30-fold across the models tested — depending on which LLM is running the population and how entrenched the existing convention already is. There's no single number to engineer a safety margin around, unlike a BFT protocol's guaranteed fraction.

**Central analogy for this paper:** a leaderless gossip protocol with a hijackable, arbitrary tiebreaker.

# 4. Core Technical Explanation

**Setup.** A population of N=24 LLM agents (one of four models tested per run: Llama-2-70b-Chat, Llama-3-70B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, or Claude-3.5-Sonnet) is seeded with no shared convention. Each round, two agents are drawn at random and simultaneously — without seeing each other's live answer — pick a name from a shared pool of W symbols (W=10 for the main experiments; W=26, the full alphabet, and W=2, a minimal two-option pool, are used for specific robustness and isolation tests; N=200 is used to check the result holds at larger scale).

**Payoff.** This is a pure coordination game, not a game with a "correct" answer: if the two agents' names match, both get +100 points ("success"); if they don't match, both get −50 points ("failure"). The only incentive is to agree with whoever you're paired with next — there's nothing intrinsically better about any one name.

**Memory.** Each agent's prompt carries a rolling window of only its own H=5 most recent interactions — partner's choice, its own choice, whether it succeeded, and its running score. Crucially, this is *private, local* memory, not global population state — exactly like a real gossip node that only ever sees its own interaction log, never the network's aggregate picture. On its very first interaction, with an empty memory, an agent picks essentially at random.

**Decision rule.** The prompt asks the agent to predict what a partner is likely to do, based on its own H-round history, and choose accordingly — an explicitly local optimization. The output format is forced to be `{'value': <name>; 'reason': <explanation>}` — answer first, justification second — specifically to stop the model's own reasoning process from smuggling extra bias into the final choice.

**What happens.** Without any agent ever seeing the population-wide state, a single dominant convention is established by roughly population round 15 for three of the four models (Llama-2-70b-Chat is the exception — the least capable model tested, it doesn't reliably reach full consensus in the same window). The mechanism the paper isolates behind this is a strikingly simple behavioral reflex: after a success, an agent repeats its previous choice on the next interaction 99.4% of the time; after a failure, it switches to a different name 97.3% of the time. Nobody explicitly programmed this "win-stay, lose-shift" rule into the agents — it fell directly out of next-token prediction conditioned on a memory-laden prompt, and it's exactly the reinforcement mechanism that decades-old physics naming-game models *assume by construction* to produce convergence. The paper is essentially confirming that LLM agents reproduce, behaviorally, the microscopic rule the theoretical model always had to assume.

**Worked micro-example — why win-stay/lose-shift snowballs.** Take a toy population of just 4 agents and a 2-name pool {A, B}, all starting with empty memory (illustrative arithmetic below, not a reproduction of the paper's own simulation, to show the mechanism in miniature):

| Round | Pair        | Agent 1 picks                             | Agent 2 picks               | Outcome                               |
| ----- | ----------- | ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Alice–Bob   | A (random)                                | B (random)                  | Fail — both switch strategy next time |
| 2     | Alice–Carol | B (switched after fail)                   | A (random, no memory yet)   | Fail — both switch again              |
| 3     | Bob–Dana    | A (switched after fail)                   | A (random)                  | **Success** — both repeat A next time |
| 4     | Alice–Dana  | A (switched again — now happens to align) | A (repeating after success) | **Success** — reinforces A further    |

By round 4, two of the four agents have just been rewarded for A and are ~99% likely to repeat it; a third has independently drifted toward A through its own switch-after-failure. One lucky early success (round 3) starts a chain that only needs a couple more rounds to visibly tip the small population toward A — and with only two options, every failure is also, automatically, a nudge toward whichever name is not currently failing. Scale this same win-stay/lose-shift reflex up to thousands of pairwise rounds across 24 (or 200) agents, and the same snowball — amplified by sheer repetition — is what produces population-wide consensus in about 15 rounds, without any agent ever being told what the rest of the population is doing.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** the reader likely assumes "collective bias" here just means the population's chosen name reflects whatever individual agents secretly preferred all along — like a poll where the majority opinion wins. The paper's W=2 isolation test shows the opposite for the pairs it examined: round-one, no-memory preference between the two names is statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip, yet the same underlying model still produces a population that locks onto one name in a heavily lopsided way across independent runs. The bias is a property of the *interaction dynamics accumulating over rounds*, not a straightforward readout of latent individual preference.

> **Watch:** the reader likely assumes the "committed minority" agents in the critical-mass experiments are just ordinary agents that happen to start out favoring the alternative name. They are structurally different: committed agents ignore their own interaction history entirely and output the fixed alternative convention on *every single round*, win or lose — a non-adaptive strategy no other agent in the population follows, not a variant initial condition.

> **Watch:** the reader likely assumes "an LLM agent" is a roughly interchangeable ingredient across this kind of experiment — that model choice is an implementation detail. It is one of the paper's largest experimental variables: critical-mass thresholds swing by more than 30-fold across the four models tested, and one model (Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct) spontaneously abandons its weaker convention with *zero* committed adversaries at all, a qualitatively different behavior none of the other three models show. Model identity is closer to an independent variable in this design than a constant.

# 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**Reusing a fully characterized paradigm as a behavioral assay.** Rather than inventing a bespoke LLM benchmark from scratch, the authors repurpose a decades-old physics/complex-systems experimental design — one whose baseline behavior is already known from both human-subject experiments and a "theoretical minimal" agent-based null model with no cognition at all (the dashed black comparison curve in Fig. 1A). That gives LLM behavior two independent yardsticks to be measured against simultaneously — a real cognitive baseline (humans) and a zero-cognition mechanical baseline (the minimal model) — rather than floating free with no reference point, which is new to the field as applied to multi-agent LLM populations specifically, even though the paradigm itself is old.

**The round-1 isolation test as a clean individual-vs-collective wedge.** The W=2, no-memory-yet measurement is a genuinely clever design move: it isolates a statistical test of individual-level bias (a single prompt, single agent, no interaction history) and directly compares it against a group-level outcome distribution generated by the *same underlying model*. That's what lets the paper make its most counterintuitive claim rigorously — "bias absent when tested alone, bias present when tested as a crowd" — rather than just asserting it. This specific dissociation (not just "bias exists," but "bias can be undetectable at the unit level and only visible at the aggregate level") is new to the field, not just new to the reader, and is the paper's sharpest contribution to the AI-safety conversation about what "testing for bias" should even mean for systems built out of interacting agents.

**Predictive Content Check**

**Falsifiable handle (always):** the paper makes concrete, independently checkable claims — specific critical-mass percentages for particular model/convention pairs that any lab with API access to the same model families could attempt to reproduce, plus a testable *ordinal* prediction that generalizes beyond any single point estimate: whichever convention is empirically identified as "stronger" (deeper basin of attraction) should always require a *larger* committed minority to overturn than a convention identified as "weaker," for a given model. If a replication attempt found that ordering reversed — weak conventions consistently harder to flip than strong ones — that would falsify the dynamical-systems mechanism (basin depth) the paper proposes to explain the asymmetry, independent of whether any specific percentage reproduces exactly.

*(Formalism-load test skipped — this paper doesn't lean on new mathematical structure to generate its claims; the "theoretical minimal naming game" curve used for comparison is an existing baseline model, not new machinery, and the statistics deployed (chi-square tests on choice distributions) are doing ordinary, undecorated hypothesis-testing work.)*

# 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> The specific numbers reported — convergence speed, which name wins, exact critical-mass thresholds — are bounded to the particular LLMs, prompt template, and toy conventions tested; the authors themselves frame the results as reliant on "the LLM model, the prompt, and specific conventions" and call for testing across "different controlled experimental settings." **(A) Consensus** — this is the paper's own explicitly stated scope limitation, not an external critique. **(paper, stated limitations)**

> The population is "unstructured" — any two agents are equally likely to be paired, with no underlying social-network topology (no small-world clustering, no scale-free hubs, no organizational structure). Real deployed multi-agent systems typically *do* have structured communication patterns, and the naming-game physics literature this paradigm descends from has repeatedly shown that network topology materially changes both convergence speed and which outcomes are reachable. **(A) Consensus** — explicitly flagged by the authors as a stated direction for future work ("embedding the population in more realistic social networks"). **(paper, stated limitations)**

> The "conventions" tested are toy labels — single letters or short arbitrary strings from a small pool — not the kind of socially loaded categories (gender, race, and other social norms) where a real-world instance of this mechanism would actually matter. This paper demonstrates the *mechanism*, not yet a real-world harm case; the authors say as much themselves, calling for a move toward "more realistic conventions such as moving from alphabet letters to sensitive human norms." **(A) Consensus** — explicit, stated future work in the paper's own discussion. **(paper, stated limitations)**

> The exact agent-count-to-percentage mapping behind the headline critical-mass figures (e.g., precisely how many committed agents, out of what total population, correspond to the "as small as 2%" and "as large as 67%" claims for each model) did not fully reconcile between two independent AI-mediated extraction passes of this paper's figures and tables during this analysis — one pass implied a larger population denominator than the N=24 baseline stated as the default population size elsewhere in the same paper. **(C) Speculative** — this is a retrieval-fidelity gap on the analyst's side, not a claim about the paper's own internal rigor; the qualitative shape of the finding (huge, model-dependent range; strong-vs-weak asymmetry) is corroborated across both extraction passes, but the precise headcount arithmetic should be checked against the paper's own Table S3 and Fig. 3B before being cited as exact. **(analyst inference / access-limited)**

# 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

Twenty-four (or, in a robustness check, two hundred) LLM agents are dropped into a population with no shared vocabulary and asked to play a purely local coordination game: get paired at random with another agent, each pick a name from a shared pool, get rewarded if you match and penalized if you don't, remember only your own last five interactions, and try again. No agent ever sees what the rest of the population is doing. And yet, within roughly fifteen rounds of this blind, decentralized shuffling, the whole population settles on one dominant name — for three of the four LLM families tested (the fourth, the least capable model, converges more slowly or not at all in the same window). The mechanism behind this is almost embarrassingly simple: agents repeat whatever just worked (about 99% of the time) and abandon whatever just failed (about 97% of the time) — a "win-stay, lose-shift" reflex that nobody programmed in explicitly, but that next-token prediction over a memory-conditioned prompt produces anyway, and that happens to be exactly the ingredient decades-old physics models of human convention formation always assumed was doing the work.

The more surprising finding sits one layer deeper. When the same models are tested on a single, memory-free choice between two names, their individual-level preference is statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip — no detectable bias in isolation. But run those same models through the full repeated-interaction game, and the population as a whole can land on one of the two names in a heavily lopsided way across independent runs — for some models, dramatically so. The bias isn't hiding somewhere in the individual agents waiting to be measured better; it emerges from the accumulation dynamics of repeated interaction itself, which means testing a single model's output for fairness, one prompt at a time, would never catch it.

The paper's third finding stress-tests how stable that emergent consensus actually is. Seed a small subgroup of agents that ignore their own history and always push a fixed alternative name, and past some threshold size, the whole population's consensus can be dragged over to the minority's choice — even though the minority is never a majority itself. How small that threshold needs to be varies enormously by model, from as little as roughly 2% of the population up to as much as roughly two-thirds, and — consistent with a basin-of-attraction picture borrowed from dynamical systems — whichever convention is more deeply entrenched requires a proportionally larger committed minority to overturn.

This summary is organized around the same three-finding structure the paper itself uses — emergence, then bias, then critical mass — deliberately, because the ordering is causal rather than arbitrary: you need a stable population-wide consensus to exist before you can ask whether what it converged to is biased, and you need a description of *how* biased (and how strongly) before "how easily can it be tipped over" is even a well-posed question. Framed that way, the paper reads less as three separate results and more as one escalating argument about what "safety" means for a *population* of interacting AI agents rather than any single one of them.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the precise numeric mapping underlying the critical-mass percentages — exactly how many committed agents, out of what total population, produce the "as small as 2%" and "as large as 67%" figures for each model. This paper was retrieved through AI-mediated extraction of the arXiv companion (the *Science Advances* publisher page itself blocked automated fetching), and two independent extraction passes returned figures for the underlying agent counts that didn't fully reconcile with each other or with the stated N=24 baseline population used elsewhere. The qualitative shape of the finding — a huge, strongly model-dependent range, a consistent strong-vs-weak asymmetry, and a spontaneous flip for one model with zero adversaries — is corroborated across both passes and the paper's abstract framing, so confidence in the *shape* of the result is reasonably high; confidence in any single exact percentage-to-headcount figure quoted above is not high enough to treat as citation-ready without checking the paper's own Table S3 and Fig. 3B directly.

# 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **A crowd of AI agents with no leader, no ballot, and no shared vocabulary will spontaneously agree on a common answer within about fifteen rounds of one-on-one conversation** — the same way decades-old physics models of human language convention formation predicted, driven by nothing more than a simple, unprogrammed "keep doing what just worked, abandon what just failed" reflex.

2. **None of the individual agents show any measurable bias when tested alone — but the crowd, as a whole, can land on one answer almost unanimously.** Bias here is a property of the group's interaction dynamics, not a readout of any single member's leanings, which means checking one AI model for fairness in isolation doesn't tell you whether a population of them, talking to each other, will behave fairly.

3. **A small, stubborn minority — in the most extreme case tested, as little as roughly 2% of the population — can flip an entire group's consensus to whatever answer it insists on,** and exactly how small that minority needs to be depends heavily on which AI model is running the population and how entrenched the existing answer already is — a genuine, model-dependent vulnerability to coordinated manipulation, not a fixed, engineerable safety margin.

# 9. Shorter Summary

Twenty-four AI language-model agents, with no leader and no shared vocabulary, are set loose in a simple game: pair up at random, each pick a name from a shared list, get a reward if you match your partner and a penalty if you don't, remember only your last few interactions, repeat. Within about fifteen rounds, the whole group — with nobody able to see the big picture — settles on one dominant name. The mechanism is a simple habit that emerged on its own: agents keep doing what just worked and abandon what just failed, and that alone is enough to snowball into group-wide agreement.

The more unsettling finding is what that agreement can hide. Tested one at a time, these models show no detectable preference between two options — a coin flip. But run them through the full group game, and the population as a whole can land overwhelmingly on one option anyway, for some models very lopsidedly across many independent runs. The bias isn't sitting inside any individual model waiting to be caught by ordinary testing; it emerges only from repeated group interaction, which means testing single AI outputs for fairness — the standard practice today — can miss it entirely.

The paper's third experiment asks how fragile a group's shared answer really is. Plant a small number of agents that always push a different, fixed alternative no matter what happens to them, and once that group crosses some size threshold, the *entire population* can flip to their answer — even though the plants are never a majority. That threshold varies enormously depending on which AI model is involved, from as little as around 2% of the population up to roughly two-thirds, and answers that are more firmly entrenched are harder to dislodge, as you'd expect.

Together, these three results make a single point: safety properties that look fine for one AI model tested by itself can behave completely differently once a population of copies starts talking to each other — both in what biases show up and in how easily a small, coordinated minority can hijack the outcome. As AI agents increasingly interact with other AI agents rather than only with humans, that's a genuinely different risk surface than anything single-model evaluation was built to catch, and this paper is among the first to make it concretely measurable.
