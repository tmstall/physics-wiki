## 1. Spinning Clusters, Seen in the Afterglow

**One-sentence hook:** By lining up 25 nearby galaxy clusters along the direction their galaxies seem to swirl, and averaging the Big Bang's afterglow behind them, two Columbia physicists found a faint warm-side/cool-side pattern — the first convincing sign that the hot gas filling clusters is moving in an organized, rotating-or-sloshing way, although the signal turns out to be larger than gentle rotation alone can easily explain.

---

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Galaxy clusters are the largest objects in the Universe held together by their own gravity: a few hundred to a few thousand galaxies, embedded in an enormous cloud of dark matter, with most of the ordinary matter not in the galaxies at all but in a diffuse gas heated to tens of millions of degrees. That gas — the intracluster medium — is where most of what we know about clusters comes from. X-ray telescopes see it glow. Astronomers then weigh the cluster by assuming the gas is in balance: its pressure pushing outward exactly holds off gravity pulling inward. If the gas is also moving in bulk — rotating, or still sloshing after a collision — some of the support against gravity comes from motion, not pressure, and the mass estimates come out too low. That bias matters, because counting clusters by mass is one of the sharpest tests of cosmology. So "is the gas moving, and how?" is not a curiosity; it feeds directly into cosmological parameters.

Measuring gas motion is hard. X-ray spectroscopy can, in principle, measure Doppler shifts of emission lines, but only with very fine energy resolution (the Hitomi and XRISM satellites have done this for a handful of clusters). The alternative used here is indirect but elegant: the gas leaves a tiny imprint on the cosmic microwave background (CMB), the radiation left over from the early Universe that shines through every cluster from behind. Electrons moving toward us nudge the CMB photons they scatter to slightly higher temperature; electrons moving away nudge them lower. This is the kinematic Sunyaev–Zel'dovich (kSZ) effect. A rotating cluster should therefore show one warm side and one cool side — a dipole — on the CMB sky.

The problem is size. The predicted dipole is roughly 10 to 100 millionths of a degree, while the ordinary CMB fluctuations on the same patch of sky are many times larger. No single cluster can show it. The only route is to average many clusters, and averaging only works if every cluster's dipole is lined up the same way first. A 2019 attempt (Baxter, Sherwin and Raghunathan) did exactly this with 13 clusters, orienting each one using the redshifts of its galaxies, and found a hint at roughly 2σ — about a 1-in-25 chance of being noise. This paper repeats that idea with twice the clusters and a cleaner map, and reaches 3.6σ.

**Paper type and stakes:** this is an observational Letter reporting the first statistically convincing detection of a predicted but never-confirmed signal — the rotational kSZ effect — plus a set of map-cleaning techniques that should carry over to future kSZ work. The stakes are a new, independent way to see how gas moves inside clusters.

**Prior Belief Check.** The existence of the effect surprises no one: if cluster gas moves coherently, the dipole must be there, and simulations predict it. What was uncertain was whether today's data could see it, and how strongly the gas actually rotates. On "can it be seen," the paper is a confident yes. On "how fast does the gas rotate," the result is less settled than the v1 title suggests: as the Claim Check below shows, the measured signal is several times larger than what the galaxies' own swirl would produce if the gas co-rotated with them at the template's density, which points toward non-equilibrium flows (mergers) contributing heavily. The published version's retitling ("cluster rotation *and intrahalo gas motions*") reads as the authors and referees acknowledging exactly this. So: confirmatory for the existence of an internal-motion kSZ dipole, mildly surprising in its amplitude, and not yet a clean measurement of rotation.

**Replication and Convergence Note.** This is a single-group result on public data, but it has partial independent support: Yang et al. (arXiv 2603.14494, March 2026) used a different, optically selected sample of 134 galaxy groups and a different pipeline and found a similar dipole at about 2.3σ. That support is weaker than it looks, because both analyses use the same Planck maps over the same SDSS region of sky and probably share some of the same nearby clusters, so their noise is partly correlated. Truly independent confirmation would come from a different CMB instrument (ACT, SPT, or the Simons Observatory) and a different spectroscopic survey (such as DESI) on a disjoint cluster sample.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

### The microwave background as a backlight

The CMB is the light released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, when the Universe cooled enough for atoms to form and light to travel freely. Today it is a nearly perfect blackbody at about 2.7 kelvin, the same in every direction to about one part in 100,000. Those tiny primordial variations — the "primary CMB" — are the main noise source for this paper: on the angular scales of a nearby cluster (tens of arcminutes, about the size of the full Moon), they are hundreds of times bigger than the signal being hunted.

The key fact for what follows: anything that scatters CMB photons without changing their blackbody *shape* is spectrally indistinguishable from the primary CMB. You cannot separate it by color. You can only separate it by knowing *where* and *in which orientation* to look.

> **Analogy:** The CMB is a backlit frosted panel with a fixed, random mottling baked into the glass. A cluster is a faint smudge on the panel. If the smudge has the same color as the glass, you can only find it by knowing exactly where it is and what shape to expect.
>
> **Breaks when:** you treat the mottling as fixed noise you could calibrate out. The primary CMB is a single sky realization — there is no second panel to compare against — so its pattern behind each cluster is genuinely unknown and has to be averaged away statistically.

### Two ways cluster gas imprints itself: hot electrons and moving electrons

When CMB photons pass through the cluster, about one in a few hundred to a thousand scatters off a free electron. Two things can happen.

**Thermal SZ (tSZ):** the electrons are very hot, so scattering gives photons a small energy kick on average. This *changes the spectrum*: the CMB looks dimmer at low frequencies and brighter at high frequencies, with a crossover near 217 GHz. Because it has its own color signature, it can be measured — and removed — using multi-frequency maps.

**Kinematic SZ (kSZ):** if the gas as a whole moves along the line of sight, the scattered photons pick up a Doppler shift. Toward us: slightly warmer. Away: slightly cooler. Crucially, the shift *preserves the blackbody shape*. That is what makes kSZ both clean (it depends only on gas momentum, not temperature) and hard (it hides inside the primary CMB).

The whole paper rests on one relation:

$$
\frac{\Delta T}{T_{\rm CMB}} = -\frac{\sigma_T}{c}\int n_e \, v_{\rm los}\, dl
$$

**Symbol definitions:**
- $\Delta T$ : the change in CMB temperature in one direction on the sky (kelvin)
- $T_{\rm CMB}$ : the average CMB temperature, 2.7255 K
- $\sigma_T$ : the Thomson cross-section, the effective target area one electron presents to a photon (square meters)
- $c$ : the speed of light
- $n_e$ : the number of free electrons per unit volume at each point along the sightline
- $v_{\rm los}$ : the gas velocity along the line of sight, positive when moving away
- $dl$ : a small step along the sightline; the integral adds up every electron the photon passes

**What this actually means:** the temperature shift is "how many electrons the photon passes" times "how fast they're moving toward or away from you," with a minus sign so that approaching gas makes it warmer. It's like a sum over a memory-mapped array where each element is density times velocity — a dot product along the line of sight. A rotating cluster makes that dot product positive on one side and negative on the other.

### Why rotation makes a dipole, and why you must line clusters up

A cluster drifting as a whole produces a uniform warm or cool spot (a "monopole"). A cluster spinning about an axis that is not pointed at us produces a warm half and a cool half — a dipole aligned with the spin. Stack many clusters at random orientations and those dipoles cancel to zero. So the method needs an outside estimate of each cluster's spin direction, and here that comes from its galaxies: split the galaxies into two halves with a line through the center, compare the average redshift on each side, and rotate the dividing line until the difference is largest. The side with lower redshift is coming toward us.

> **Concept:** For each cluster, the authors estimate a rotation direction from galaxy redshifts and rotate the CMB cutout so that every cluster's estimated approaching side points the same way. Averaging the aligned cutouts reinforces any dipole that genuinely follows the galaxy-inferred direction and cancels the random primary-CMB pattern. The galaxy-inferred direction is itself noisy, and simulations show that galaxies, dark matter, and gas in a cluster need not share the same spin axis, so the alignment is only partial.
>
> **Vehicle:** A performance engineer chasing a tiny, systematic latency bump in a service whose traces are dominated by jitter. She cannot see the bump in any single trace. But a separate, noisy event log tells her roughly when a particular internal event (say, a lock handoff) occurred in each request. She shifts each trace so the logged event sits at time zero, then averages thousands of traces. The jitter averages away; the bump that is truly locked to the event survives. If the event log is sometimes wrong by a few microseconds, the averaged bump comes out smeared and smaller than the real one.
>
> **Analogy:** Each cluster's CMB cutout ↔ one request's latency trace. Primary CMB fluctuations ↔ jitter. The galaxy-inferred rotation direction ↔ the noisy event log. Rotating each cutout to a common orientation ↔ shifting each trace to align on the logged event. Misalignment between gas and galaxy spin ↔ log timestamps that are off by a random amount. The stacked dipole ↔ the averaged latency bump.
>
> **Breaks when:** you ask what the aligned bump *means*. In the profiler, anything event-locked in the average is caused by that event, because the log is generated by the same code path. Here, the "event log" — the galaxy redshift split — responds to *any* organized motion that makes one side of the cluster approach and the other recede: true rotation, or two subclusters falling through each other. The averaged dipole proves the gas moves coherently with the galaxies' velocity pattern; it cannot, by itself, say whether that pattern is spin or collision. The vehicle has no category for "trigger fires on two physically different causes."

### Cleaning the map: color separation and the polarization trick

Planck observed the sky at nine frequencies. Each foreground — dust in our galaxy, radio sources, the thermal SZ — has a different color signature, while the CMB and kSZ share one signature. The authors combine the frequency maps with weights chosen so that anything with the CMB's color passes through at full strength and everything else is suppressed as much as possible. They also force the weights to give *exactly zero* response to the thermal-SZ color. The weights are recomputed separately for different angular scales and sky regions, because galactic dust varies across the sky while extragalactic signals vary mostly with scale. (The general technique is called "internal linear combination"; the scale-and-position variant uses spherical wavelets called needlets.)

The genuinely new trick is the second step. The primary CMB is also faintly *polarized*, and one polarization pattern (called the "E-mode") is partly correlated with the temperature pattern, because both come from the same early-Universe sound waves. The kSZ signal from a nearby cluster has essentially no polarized counterpart. So part of the primary-CMB temperature noise can be *predicted* from the polarization map and subtracted, while the kSZ signal is untouched.

> **Analogy:** Think of a noisy cycle counter whose jitter is partly driven by cache misses. You have a second counter for cache misses. Regress the cycle counter on the miss counter, subtract the predicted part, and the jitter shrinks — while any effect you care about that is unrelated to cache misses stays put.
>
> **Breaks when:** the second counter is itself noisy. Planck's polarization map is dominated by instrument noise on these scales, so the subtraction removes only about a tenth to a fifth of the primary-CMB noise. A cleaner polarization map (from ACT, SPT, or the Simons Observatory) could remove much more — the ceiling set by the physics is roughly the square of a correlation that can approach 40 percent.

*Analyst-built illustration (not from the paper):* how "predict-and-subtract" shrinks noise without touching the signal.

```
        temperature map         polarization map       cleaned map
        (signal + CMB)          (CMB-correlated part)  (signal + less CMB)

  cool  ..::##   ##::..   warm   ..::#    #::..          . ::#  #:: .
        ..::##   ##::..    -     ..::#    #::..    =     . ::#  #:: .
```

*What to look at:* the polarization map knows nothing about the cluster's gas motion, so subtracting its prediction can only remove primary-CMB noise. With Planck's noisy polarization data the noise drops by about 6 percent in amplitude for this measurement; the signal is unchanged.

### Origins and big picture: how clusters form, heat their gas, and acquire spin

Galaxy clusters are the latest and largest products of hierarchical structure formation. The early Universe was nearly smooth, with tiny density ripples — the same ones imprinted in the CMB. Gravity amplified them. Dark matter, which feels only gravity, collapsed first into small clumps; those merged into bigger ones; and over about 10 billion years, the rarest high peaks grew into clusters of a hundred trillion to a few quadrillion solar masses. Clusters are still forming today: the nearby ones in this paper are mostly a few billion years past their last major merger, and some (Abell 1750 in this sample) are visibly colliding right now.

The gas follows the dark matter into the deepening wells. As it falls in at a thousand kilometers per second or more, it slams into gas already there and shocks, converting infall speed into heat. That is why cluster gas sits at 10 to 100 million degrees — hot enough that it is fully ionized and glows in X-rays. Left alone, the gas settles into near-balance, with its pressure supporting it against gravity. The densest gas in the core radiates fast enough that, naively, it should cool and flow inward in a "cooling flow" of hundreds of solar masses per year; observations show far less cooling than that, and the standard explanation is heating by the central black hole's jets. The paper notes that rotation could also help hold the core up.

Where would rotation come from? The classic answer is *tidal torques*. While a proto-cluster is still a loose, irregular overdensity, the lumpy matter around it pulls unevenly on it, spinning it up slightly before it collapses. Once it collapses, it stops gaining much spin this way. The result is modest: simulations show clusters are supported mainly by random motions, with rotation contributing a few percent to perhaps ten percent. The second source is mergers. When two subclusters collide off-center, their orbital angular momentum gets deposited into the merged system. Right after such an event, the gas carries large coherent flows — sloshing, shear, partial rotation — that decay over a few billion years.

A further subtlety matters directly for this paper: the gas, the dark matter, and the galaxies need not spin about the same axis. Galaxies orbit almost without friction and keep memories of the subclusters they arrived in; gas collides, shocks, and mixes. Simulations of very massive clusters (the MACSIS set) find the spin axes of the three components are often misaligned by tens of degrees. Since this paper orients each cluster using its galaxies, any misalignment smears the gas dipole and makes it look weaker than it is.

Why does this matter beyond clusters? Because cluster masses are estimated by assuming the gas is supported by pressure alone. Any bulk motion — rotation or merger flows — means pressure is doing only part of the work, so the inferred mass is too low. This "hydrostatic bias," estimated at roughly 10 to 30 percent, is one of the largest uncertainties when clusters are used to measure how fast cosmic structure grows. A direct measurement of gas motion is a direct handle on that bias.

> **Analogy:** A server farm's load balancer assumes every node's CPU load comes from requests (pressure). If some nodes are also running a background migration job (bulk motion), you'll underestimate the true request capacity you need — the background job is "hidden support."
>
> **Breaks when:** you imagine the background job is steady. Merger-driven flows are transient and decay over billions of years, while tidal-torque spin is a long-lived property; the CMB dipole sees both and cannot tell them apart.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### Step 1 — A map where cluster gas cannot pretend to be a dipole

**The failure it prevents:** hot cluster gas makes a big thermal-SZ cold spot at every cluster center, which would add noise exactly where you are looking.

**What they do:** the authors build their own CMB-colored map from Planck's newest data release, weighting the frequency channels so the thermal-SZ signature is removed exactly. They inpaint the brightest point sources and the galactic plane before computing weights so those regions don't distort the weights. The result is a full-sky temperature map containing only things with the CMB's color: primary CMB, kSZ, and noise.

**What it buys:** the thermal SZ is round, so it would not *create* a false dipole, but it adds noise at the cluster positions. The supplement confirms the expectation: without removing it, the stack still shows the dipole, but a clear cold spot from residual thermal SZ appears in the difference image, centered right on the clusters (which also confirms the cluster centers are well placed).

### Step 2 — Shrinking the biggest noise source

**The failure it prevents:** primary CMB fluctuations dominate the noise by a large factor.

**What they do:** two moves. First, subtract the part of the temperature map predictable from Planck's polarization map (the E-mode trick above), across a wide range of angular scales. Second, filter out the very largest scales (bigger than about two degrees), where the primary CMB is overwhelmingly strong and the clusters contribute nothing, and the very smallest (under a few arcminutes), where instrument noise dominates.

**What it buys:** together these raise the significance by about half to one σ. The authors are candid about one piece: dropping the polarization subtraction lowers the result from 3.6σ to 3.0σ, but only about 0.2 to 0.3σ of that comes from genuinely lower noise. The rest comes from a statistically insignificant (1.4σ) dipole in the polarization-predicted map that happens to point *opposite* the expected signal, so subtracting it adds to the signal — in their words, a fortunate fluctuation. To my knowledge this is the first time the polarization-subtraction idea, proposed in 2008, has been applied to real data.

### Step 3 — Which clusters, and which way are they turning?

**The failure it prevents:** optically found "clusters" can be projections of unrelated groups, and a poorly determined spin direction just adds noise to the stack.

**What they do:** they start from a catalog of about 89,000 galaxy groups found in SDSS by linking galaxies close in position and redshift, a catalog that deliberately splits merging systems into separate substructures. They keep only groups that match an X-ray-detected cluster (within 10 arcminutes and a small redshift difference), have at least 50 member galaxies, and are mostly unmasked: 54 clusters. For each, they find the line through the X-ray center that maximizes the redshift difference between the two halves, scanning in 5-degree steps. For a truly rotating system, that difference should trace a clean sine wave as the line turns; they keep only clusters whose curve correlates with a sine at 0.8 or better. Final sample: 25 clusters, at redshifts 0.02 to 0.09 (about 300 million to 1.2 billion light-years away), with masses from about 30 trillion to 400 trillion Suns.

**What it buys:** X-ray matching gives reliable centers and independent masses; the sine-shape cut throws out clusters with messy, undecidable velocity patterns. A redshift-difference stack of the galaxies themselves, oriented the same way, shows a strong dipole — confirming the orientation pipeline works.

### Step 4 — Rotate, rescale, weight, average

**The failure it prevents:** clusters of different sizes and distances would blur together, and a small, noisy cluster would count as much as Coma.

**What they do:** each cutout is rotated so the estimated spin points up, with the approaching side on the right, then rescaled so every cluster spans the same size in units of its own reference radius (R500, the radius inside which the average density is 500 times the cosmic critical density; roughly 0.5 to 1.1 megaparsecs, or about 1.5 to 3.5 million light-years, for this sample). Each cluster gets a weight proportional to its mass times its galaxy redshift split times its galaxy count, divided by how noisy the CMB is for a cutout of that size. The dipole is measured as half the temperature difference between the right and left halves, using the region between one-tenth and three-quarters of R500.

**What it buys:** a weighted average in which large, fast-looking, well-sampled clusters dominate — which is what a matched filter should do, provided the weights aren't tuned on the outcome. The weighting turns out to matter a lot (see the Claim Check).

### Step 5 — How likely is chance?

**The failure it prevents:** an apparent dipole in 25 averaged cutouts could be a random alignment of primary-CMB patterns.

**What they do:** repeat the whole oriented, weighted stack 30,000 times at random sky positions within the same survey area, and see how often noise alone produces a dipole as big as the real one.

**What it buys:** a direct, assumption-light false-alarm rate. The real dipole beats 99.98 percent of the random stacks.

### Step 6 — Does the shape look like rotating gas?

**The failure it prevents:** a single dipole number could come from one hot pixel or an odd shape; the radial profile checks the signal looks spread like cluster gas.

**What they do:** average temperature in six radial bins on each side, and compare with a template: every cluster rotating like a solid body (speed rising in proportion to distance from the axis), with gas density from a simulation-calibrated profile and Planck's 5-arcminute blur applied. They scale the template by one free amplitude.

**What it buys:** the best-fit amplitude is 1.05 with an uncertainty of 0.32, a 3.3σ preference for a nonzero signal, and the shape fits well. The authors stress that the amplitude being near one is *not* a measurement of rotation speed: they chose the template's speed to reproduce about 25 millionths of a degree, matching a simulation prediction.

### Ruling out impostors

The paper checks three other ways to get a rotation-aligned dipole. Dust in cluster galaxies, Doppler-boosted by their motion, could mimic the effect — but the 545 GHz dust-dominated channel stacked the same way shows nothing, and removing that channel from the map changes nothing. Gravitational lensing of the CMB by the cluster makes a dipole, but along the local CMB gradient, which has no reason to follow galaxy spin. The "moving lens" effect from a cluster's sideways motion is expected to be ten times weaker and aligned with that motion, not with spin. All three average away. These tests are well designed and convincing: the signal is a kSZ dipole from internal gas motion.

### Claim check

Done with a short Python script (`claimcheck.py`) using the paper's quoted figures and Table I.

1. **Detection significance — Reproduced.** "Beats 99.98 percent of randoms" corresponds to 3.5σ one-sided or 3.7σ two-sided, so 3.6σ is a fair middle. One caveat in plain words: with 30,000 randoms, 99.98 percent means only about six random stacks beat the real one; that small count alone makes the significance uncertain by about ±0.1σ. The headline holds.

2. **Profile amplitude — Reproduced.** 1.05 divided by its 0.32 uncertainty gives 3.3σ, as stated, consistent with the dipole result.

3. **Cluster weights — Reproduced.** The 25 weights in Table I sum to 100 percent, and the per-cluster noise implied by the weight formula rises smoothly toward the nearest clusters, as it should (bigger cutouts collect more large-scale CMB). One thing the table makes visible: the top five clusters carry 57 percent of the weight, so the effective number of independent clusters is about 12, not 25.

**Sensitivity check — the weighting and a few favorable steps.** The paper's own supplement shows the result depends strongly on how clusters are weighted: equal weights give 1.5σ (not significant), mass alone 3.1σ, the fiducial choice 3.6σ, and expected-signal weighting 3.7σ. Removing Coma (15 percent of the weight) gives 2.9σ. Dropping the polarization step gives 3.0σ, part of whose gain the authors attribute to luck. A deliberately conservative test that randomizes each real cluster's orientation also gives 2.9σ. The weights are physically motivated rather than tuned, and mass-weighting makes sense, so the fair summary is: **the conclusion survives as roughly 3σ evidence across reasonable choices, but it survives only with mass-informed weighting, and the 3.6σ figure sits at the favorable end of that range.**

**Robustness test — alternative explanations (observational).** Dust: a contaminating dust dipole would show up at 545 GHz — none does. Thermal SZ: a leakage problem would show a centered cold spot and a dipole in the thermal-SZ-only map — the cold spot appears only in the unprotected map and the thermal-SZ map has no dipole. Lensing and moving-lens effects: they would align with the CMB gradient or the cluster's sideways motion, which are unrelated to galaxy spin, so they average out. The detection of a kSZ dipole is robust.

**Significant finding — the amplitude is too large for gentle co-rotation, and the simulation comparison is mass-mismatched.** This does not touch the detection; it touches the physical reading in the v1 title ("cluster rotation"). Three numbers from the paper's own inputs:

- To reach about 25 millionths of a degree, the authors' template needs gas rotating at 1,500 km/s at one-fifth of R500, rising to about 3,750 km/s at half of R500 (solid-body rotation). A standard dark-matter profile at this sample's typical mass gives a circular orbital speed of only about 950 to 1,100 km/s at those radii. Gas rotating faster than the circular speed cannot stay bound in equilibrium.
- The galaxies' own redshift splits in Table I imply rotation of only about 150 to 720 km/s, weighted average about 400 km/s — roughly four times slower than the template at the inner radius. And misalignment between galaxy and gas spin should make the observed dipole *smaller* than the true one, not larger.
- The "consistent with simulations" comparison is to the MACSIS clusters, which are about 10 to 100 times more massive (median roughly a quadrillion solar masses) than this sample's weighted average of about 250 trillion. Scaling the simulated amplitude down self-similarly predicts a signal about two to three times weaker than observed.

What this means: either (a) the dipole is dominated by non-equilibrium flows — merger shear and sloshing, which can reach 1,000 to 2,000 km/s; (b) the true signal is smaller and this first detection caught an upward fluctuation (a normal selection effect for a just-over-threshold discovery — the earlier 13-cluster analysis measured about half this amplitude in the same convention); (c) the gas density profile in these particular clusters is higher than assumed; or some mix. The amplitude uncertainty (about 30 percent) does not rescue a pure-rotation reading. The paper partly anticipates this — a footnote defines its effect "broadly" to include merger flows, and it flags known mergers like Abell 1750 — but the abstract and title of v1 present "galaxy cluster rotation" as the finding. The published version's added phrase "and intrahalo gas motions" is the right correction.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the measured dipole tells us how fast clusters rotate. The paper actually says its fitted amplitude is "not a constraint on the physical rkSZ amplitude": the template speed was chosen to match a simulation, and misalignment and projection losses were not modeled. What is measured is that a dipole *exists* and lines up with the galaxy velocity pattern.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "rotation" means steady, long-lived spin. The paper's own definition (footnote 1) covers any dipolar gas motion, including merger-driven flows, and it states the distinction "is not always clear." The galaxy redshift split cannot distinguish a spinning cluster from two colliding subclusters, and the amplitude analysis above favors substantial merger contributions.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes 25 clusters means 25 independent votes. The weighting concentrates more than half the statistical power in five clusters, Coma alone carries 15 percent, and equal weighting yields no significant signal. The result is really "a dozen massive clusters, weighted by mass and spin estimate."

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes the noise model is complete. The 30,000 random stacks capture primary CMB and instrument noise but not "cluster-correlated" noise — signals that exist only because a cluster is there (lensing, residual radio sources, kSZ from neighbors). The authors argue these are small and offer the randomized-orientation test (2.9σ) as a rough upper bound on their effect; they state that future higher-resolution analyses will need a dedicated treatment.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**First, polarization-based cleaning of temperature maps, on real data.** Subtracting the part of the primary-CMB temperature that is predictable from the E-mode polarization is an old idea (Frommert and Ensslin, 2008) that nobody had applied to data. Here it works modestly — Planck's polarization map is noisy, so it removes only about 10 to 20 percent of the primary-CMB power — but the authors show clearly where the ceiling is: with signal-dominated polarization maps from ACT, SPT, or the Simons Observatory, the technique could remove a much larger fraction of the dominant noise for *every* small-signal CMB secondary measurement, not just this one. This is new to the field, and arguably the paper's most transferable contribution.

**Second, a better-curated orientation sample.** Combining an SDSS group catalog that splits substructures, X-ray cross-matching for centers and masses, and a "does the redshift split look like a clean sine wave?" quality cut turns a noisy orientation step into a usable filter. Each piece existed; assembling them and showing that mass-informed weighting is what makes the stack work (equal weights fail) is new to this specific problem. The clever, honest touch is reproducing the 2019 result first with their own pipeline (they get 1.8σ versus the published 96 percent confidence), so the improvement is traceable to data and method, not a different definition.

What is *not* new: the oriented-stacking approach (Baxter et al. 2019) and the idea of the effect (Cooray and Chen, and Chluba and Mannheim, 2002).

### Predictive Content Check

**Falsifiable handle:** the paper makes a clear, near-term, testable prediction: the dipole should grow in significance roughly in proportion to the square root of the number of properly weighted massive clusters, and should persist in an independent CMB dataset. With the Simons Observatory and DESI spectroscopy on ten to a hundred times more clusters, a real effect should reach well beyond 5σ. If a larger, independent sample shows a dipole consistent with zero, or one much smaller than about 25 millionths of a degree for comparable masses, this result was a fluctuation or a Planck-specific artifact. A second, sharper handle follows from the Claim Check: if the signal is mostly merger-driven, it should be concentrated in visibly disturbed clusters and largely vanish in relaxed ones; if it is mostly rotation, it should survive in relaxed clusters at a level consistent with their galaxy kinematics. The authors name this split as future work.

Formalism-load check: skipped — the math here (a line-of-sight integral and a template fit) is plainly doing its job.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

**The physical reading — rotation versus merger flows — is undetermined.** The galaxy-based orientation responds to any coherent bipolar motion; the measured dipole cannot separate spin from collision. **(A) Consensus** — the paper says so explicitly in its footnote defining the effect and in its conclusions, and the published retitling reflects it. **(paper, footnote 1 and Conclusions)**

**The signal is several times larger than galaxy-tracked co-rotation in equilibrium can produce.** The authors' own template requires gas speeds above the circular orbital speed, and the galaxy splits suggest speeds about four times lower. **(C) Speculative** — this comes from my recomputation from the paper's template parameters, Table I galaxy redshift splits, and a standard dark-matter profile; a specialist could argue for higher central gas densities or different velocity structure that softens the tension. **(analyst inference)**

**The simulation comparison is mass-mismatched.** "Qualitatively consistent with state-of-the-art simulations" compares to clusters 10 to 100 times more massive than this sample. **(B) Contested** — the authors present the comparison as qualitative and the amplitude as unconstrained, so reasonable people can differ on how much weight the sentence carries; the mass mismatch itself is a fact from the MACSIS paper. **(analyst inference, from broader literature)**

**Significance depends on weighting and a few favorable steps.** Equal weights give 1.5σ; without Coma, 2.9σ; without polarization cleaning, 3.0σ (partly due to a lucky fluctuation, per the authors). **(A) Consensus** — all of these numbers are reported in the paper's own supplement. **(paper, Supplement B.3, B.4, A.3)**

**Small effective sample.** About 12 effective clusters, five of which carry most of the weight. **(A) Consensus** — this follows directly from Table I weights and the Coma test the authors themselves ran. **(paper, Table I; analyst inference for the effective number)**

**Cluster-correlated noise is not in the noise model.** The randoms capture primary CMB and instrument noise but not cluster-specific contaminants. **(A) Consensus** — acknowledged by the authors, who give the randomized-orientation 2.9σ as a rough check and call for dedicated treatment in future work. **(paper, Supplement B.6, footnote 11)**

**Galaxy–gas spin misalignment and redshift-space tilt are not modeled.** Simulations show misalignment can halve the signal; the independent Yang et al. study finds that tilted galaxy distributions can bias galaxy-inferred rotation by tens of percent. **(A) Consensus** — both effects are documented in the simulation and follow-up literature; the paper acknowledges misalignment. **(paper, Theory section; broader literature)**

**Selection on a sine-like redshift pattern may preferentially pick merging systems.** A clean two-sided velocity split is exactly what two infalling subclusters produce. **(C) Speculative** — this is my reading of how the cut interacts with merger geometry; the substructure-splitting group catalog was chosen partly to mitigate it, and its effectiveness is not quantified. **(analyst inference)**

**Open questions for the next 12–24 months:** Does the dipole persist in ACT or SPT data on an overlapping sample? Does it split cleanly between relaxed and disturbed clusters? Can a DESI-based sample with a few hundred clusters measure the amplitude well enough to compare with galaxy kinematics directly? And can the polarization-cleaning step be shown to improve other kSZ analyses by the amount the authors project?

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

**Summary.** Goldstein and Hill report the first statistically convincing detection of the "rotational" kinematic Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect: a warm-side/cool-side pattern in the cosmic microwave background behind galaxy clusters, caused by hot cluster gas moving toward us on one side and away on the other. The signal is far too small to see in any one cluster, so they average 25 nearby, massive, X-ray-confirmed clusters after rotating each one to line up with the direction its galaxies appear to swirl, estimated from galaxy redshifts. Before averaging, they clean Planck's maps aggressively: a color-based combination of frequency channels that passes CMB-colored signals and exactly removes the thermal signature of hot gas; a new step that subtracts the part of the temperature noise predictable from the polarization map; and a filter removing the largest and smallest angular scales. The averaged map shows a dipole of about 25 millionths of a degree, beating 99.98 percent of 30,000 random stacks (3.6σ). A radial profile fit confirms the shape at 3.3σ. Tests at the dust-dominated frequency, without thermal-SZ removal, and against lensing-type explanations all support a genuine kSZ origin. The authors present the amplitude as qualitatively consistent with simulations and envision a new tool for probing gas dynamics, hydrostatic mass bias, and cooling flows.

**Explanation of the framing.** I've framed this as a solid detection of *coherent internal gas motion* rather than of *rotation*, for two reasons. First, the method's orienting key — the galaxies' redshift split — fires for collisions as readily as for spin, and the paper's own definition concedes this. Second, the amplitude is the wrong size for gentle co-rotation: reproducing it with the authors' template requires gas faster than the cluster's orbital speed, roughly four times faster than the galaxies indicate, while the "simulation-consistent" amplitude comes from clusters much more massive than these. The published version's title change suggests referees pushed in the same direction. I've also tempered the 3.6σ: it is real, but it sits at the favorable end of a roughly 3 to 3.6σ range that the authors' own robustness tests span, and it rests on about a dozen effective clusters. None of this diminishes the paper's two durable contributions: a detection that moves the effect from "hinted" to "evidenced," and a polarization-cleaning technique that should pay off much more with the next generation of CMB polarization maps. The takeaway is: the CMB can now see gas moving inside clusters; whether that motion is mostly spin or mostly collision is the next question, and the data needed to answer it is arriving.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the amplitude argument in the Claim Check — it rests on the authors' specific gas-density template, a simple solid-body velocity shape, a standard dark-matter profile for the circular speed, and a hemisphere-average conversion of galaxy redshift splits to speed. Each is reasonable, but a cluster specialist could plausibly argue for denser cores, nonuniform rotation curves, or galaxy kinematics that underestimate gas speeds, and I could not view the figures directly to check whether the profile's radial extent supports one reading over another.

**Revision notes:** the referee pass added the effective-sample-size point, reframed the sensitivity check around the paper's own robustness numbers, tightened the amplitude finding to rely on paper inputs plus one standard profile, and replaced figure-number references to supplementary plots with section references.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **Cluster gas has been caught moving in the CMB's shadow.** Averaging 25 aligned clusters reveals a warm/cool dipole at 3.6σ — the first convincing detection of the long-predicted rotational kSZ effect.

2. **"Rotation" is the hopeful word; "organized motion" is the safe one.** The signal is too strong for gas gently co-rotating with the galaxies, and the method can't tell spin from collision — mergers are likely a big part of it.

3. **The lasting trick is the polarization cleaning.** Using the CMB's polarization to predict and subtract part of its temperature noise worked modestly with Planck and should work much better with the next generation of telescopes, for many faint signals, not just this one.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Galaxy clusters are filled with gas at tens of millions of degrees. Whether that gas spins or sloshes matters, because astronomers weigh clusters by assuming the gas is held up by pressure alone; hidden motion makes them underestimate cluster masses, which in turn feeds into cosmology.

One way to see gas motion is through the cosmic microwave background, the faint radiation from the early Universe that shines through every cluster. Gas moving toward us makes the background slightly warmer; gas moving away makes it slightly cooler. A rotating cluster should therefore show a warm side and a cool side. The effect is tiny — about 25 millionths of a degree — and buried under much larger natural ripples in the background.

Samuel Goldstein and Colin Hill used Planck satellite maps and galaxy redshifts from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey. For 25 nearby, X-ray-confirmed clusters, they estimated each cluster's spin direction from which side's galaxies were approaching, rotated each map patch to match, and averaged them. They also cleaned the maps more carefully than before, including a new trick: using the background's polarization to predict and subtract part of its temperature noise. The result was a clear warm/cool pattern, stronger than 99.98 percent of random trials — about 3.6σ. Tests ruled out dust, lensing, and leftover heat signatures.

Two cautions. The strength depends on giving massive clusters more weight, and it is carried mostly by about a dozen of them. And the signal is larger than gentle rotation alone could produce: it would require gas moving faster than the cluster's own orbital speed. Collisions between subclusters, which the method can't distinguish from spin, probably contribute heavily — and the published version of the paper now says "rotation and intrahalo gas motions."

The detection is solid; the interpretation awaits larger samples from upcoming telescopes and spectroscopic surveys.
