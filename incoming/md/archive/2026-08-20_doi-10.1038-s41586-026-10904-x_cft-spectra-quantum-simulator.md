**Listening to the Universal Music of a Phase Transition, One Atom at a Time**

# 2. Big-Picture Context

Conformal field theories (CFTs) are a special class of quantum field theory that show up in a surprising range of places: string theory and the AdS/CFT correspondence in high-energy physics, statistical mechanics near critical points, and condensed-matter systems tuned exactly to continuous quantum phase transitions. At such a transition, correlations extend across arbitrarily large distances and the system loses sensitivity to its microscopic details — what survives is a universal, "hardware-independent" effective description, and for a huge and important class of 1+1-dimensional transitions, that description is a CFT.

CFTs come with unusually rich, precise predictions. Put the system in a finite box (a finite-length chain, say) and CFT theory predicts a specific discrete tower of low-energy excitation levels, whose *ratios* to each other are universal numbers — the same for any microscopic system that flows to the same CFT, regardless of what atoms or spins or electrons it's actually made of. John Cardy worked this out theoretically in the 1980s: the pattern of finite-size energy levels directly encodes which fields (operators) the CFT contains and their scaling dimensions, and boundary conditions act as a control knob that changes which operators are allowed to appear, and hence the observable spectrum. Despite four decades of theory, this rich structure — the actual discrete spectrum, not just a bulk scaling exponent — had never been directly measured in either a real solid-state material or a synthetic quantum simulator. Prior experimental probes (inelastic neutron scattering on magnetic insulators, quantum Hall edge-state transport) could see power-law correlations consistent with CFT physics, but resolving individual excitation energies and their exact ratios was out of reach.

This paper closes that gap using a programmable neutral-atom (Rydberg) quantum simulator — a physical platform built from individually trapped, laser-controlled atoms arranged in a tunable array — combined with a newly developed measurement technique the authors call modulation spectroscopy.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** this is a combined experimental-and-theoretical condensed-matter/quantum-simulation physics paper. The experimental contribution is a new spectroscopic technique for resolving many-body excitation spectra in an analog quantum simulator; the payoff is the first direct observation of CFT excitation spectra (for two different universality classes) in any physical system, plus a demonstration that the same technique can access the dynamical structure factor — a step toward a general diagnostic tool for identifying unknown universality classes in future quantum-simulation experiments where classical simulation isn't feasible.

**Prior Belief Check.** This result is consistent with, not a challenge to, established CFT theory — nobody doubted that Ising and tricritical Ising CFTs should describe these transitions, and the specific universal ratios (2:4:6:8, 4/3, 5/3, 10/3, and so on) were calculated decades ago. What makes this genuinely informative to specialists is less "a surprising new fact about physics" and more "a hard experimental target finally hit": individually resolving many-body excitation energies cleanly enough, with small enough finite-size and non-universal corrections, to actually see exact CFT-predicted ratios — rather than just qualitative scaling behavior — is a real technical achievement that many in the quantum-simulation community would have expected to remain out of reach for a while longer, especially at a genuinely interacting (non-free-fermion) multicritical point like tricritical Ising.

**Replication & Convergence Note.** This is a single lab's result (Caltech, with theory collaborators at Paris-Saclay, the Technical University of Munich, and the Munich Center for Quantum Science and Technology), using one specific hardware platform (an array of individually trapped $^{88}$Sr atoms). It has not been independently replicated on a different quantum-simulation platform (for example, trapped ions or superconducting qubits) or with a different atomic species. Genuine independent confirmation would look like another group reproducing the same universal energy ratios — ideally via a microscopically different realization of the same Ising or tricritical-Ising universality class — as a real test of universality, not just of this specific apparatus.

# 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

You already have real footing here from the AdS/CFT and quantum-gravity curriculum — conformal field theory itself isn't a cold start, just a different application of it (condensed-matter criticality rather than a gravitational dual). What's genuinely new is the experimental platform and technique, so that's where the ground-up treatment goes.

**Quantum criticality and universality classes.**

**Analogy:** At a continuous phase transition, a system becomes exactly self-similar across scales — the way a well-designed cache hierarchy or a fractal data structure looks statistically the same whether you zoom into 10 elements or 10,000. Different microscopic systems (different specific interactions, different hardware) can flow to the *same* effective long-distance theory, the same way different sorting algorithms with different constant-factor overheads can share the same asymptotic complexity class. That shared effective theory is the "universality class," and for the 1+1D transitions studied here, it's a CFT.

**Breaks when:** pushed on what exactly becomes scale-invariant — it's specifically the long-distance, low-energy physics (correlation functions, the *ratios* of excitation energies) that's universal; short-distance, high-energy details (the exact atomic spacing, the exact Rabi frequency used to drive the experiment) remain completely non-universal and platform-specific. The universal/non-universal split is not "everything becomes the same," it's "one specific layer becomes the same."

**Finite-size CFT spectra and universal ratios.**

**Analogy:** Put a CFT in a finite box (an open chain of length $L$) and its otherwise-continuous spectrum discretizes into a specific finite tower of allowed energy levels. The *absolute* energies depend on non-universal microscopic details (like a benchmark's raw clock cycles, which depend on the specific machine), but the *ratios* between levels are universal fingerprints of the CFT — like normalizing a benchmark suite's results to a baseline run, which cancels out the machine-specific clock speed and reveals only the underlying architecture class.

**Breaks when:** pushed on "finite box" — for an open chain, the boundary conditions at the two ends aren't incidental, they're a genuine control knob (see below) that changes *which* levels are even present in the tower, not just their absolute scale.

**Majorana fermions and the free-fermion Ising CFT.**

**Analogy:** The Ising CFT's excitation spectrum is built from "chiral Majorana fermions" — a way of re-expressing the many interacting spin (or CDW-order) degrees of freedom as free, non-interacting fermionic quasiparticles. This is a change of basis that diagonalizes an otherwise-coupled system into independent modes, the same way a Fourier transform decomposes a complicated, internally-coupled signal into independent frequency components that don't talk to each other.

**Breaks when:** pushed to ask whether this free-fermion description survives at the tricritical point too — it doesn't. The tricritical Ising (TCI) CFT is a genuinely *interacting* CFT (no free-fermion rewrite exists), which is exactly why it's the more stringent test of the method: any agreement there can't be attributed to secretly reducing to free-particle counting.

**Boundary conditions as a control knob.**

**Analogy:** The same bulk CFT can support several distinct *stable* boundary conditions — think of it like the same underlying protocol running with different "edge policies" for how a distributed system handles its boundary nodes (pinned to a fixed value versus left free to fluctuate). Changing only the edge behavior — without touching the bulk physics at all — can select a genuinely different discrete spectrum from the same underlying CFT, the way changing the boundary conditions of a recurrence relation selects a different specific solution from the same general family of solutions.

**Breaks when:** pushed on "just an edge effect" — for the TCI CFT specifically, different boundary conditions change the *universal energy ratios themselves* (4/3 for free, 2 for fixed, 10/3 for an unstable intermediate condition), not just some local edge energy offset. The boundary choice reaches into the bulk-visible spectrum.

**Rydberg atom arrays and modulation spectroscopy.**

**Analogy:** The experimental platform is a fully programmable hardware testbed: individually trapped, laser-controlled atoms held in a tunable optical-tweezer array, coupled to each other through van der Waals interactions, acting as a physical analog computer built to realize a specific target Hamiltonian. Modulation spectroscopy is the measurement trick: periodically "shake" the system at a tunable frequency and watch for a resonant response — like sweeping a chirp signal through a circuit and recording where it "rings." A resonance appears exactly when the drive frequency matches an internal energy gap between the ground state and some excited state, and the *strength* of that resonance reveals the transition's matrix element (how strongly connected those two states are) — together, resonance frequency plus strength map out the spectrum.

**Breaks when:** pushed on "sweeping a chirp" — the actual protocol interleaves modulation with a careful adiabatic ramp before and after (to prepare the ground state and to read out the result in a basis where the excited-state population can actually be measured), so it isn't a passive continuous sweep the way an electrical frequency-response measurement typically is; it's closer to prepare → briefly perturb → measure in a rotated basis.

**Central analogy for this paper:** *A finite chain resonating in CFT's universal harmonics.*

# 4. Core Technical Explanation

**The platform and the phase diagram.** The experiment uses a 1D chain of up to $L=35$ individually trapped $^{88}$Sr atoms, each a qubit with ground state $|0\rangle$ and Rydberg state $|1\rangle$, driven by a global Rabi frequency $\Omega$ and detuning $\Delta$, with van der Waals interactions $V_{ij} \propto |r_i - r_j|^{-6}$ between atoms in $|1\rangle$. In the strong-blockade limit (nearest-neighbor interaction $V_1 \to \infty$, forbidding adjacent Rydberg excitations) plus an engineered next-nearest-neighbor interaction $V_2$, the effective Hamiltonian reduces to the Fendley–Sengupta–Sachdev (FSS) model, whose phase diagram (Fig. 1a) has a disordered phase and a $\mathbb{Z}_2$-ordered charge-density-wave (CDW) phase, separated by a second-order transition line governed by the Ising CFT that terminates at a tricritical point governed by the TCI CFT.

**The measurement sequence.** The team adiabatically prepares the ground state close to a target critical point, then applies a brief, weak, coherent sinusoidal modulation of either the global detuning or a spatially-patterned local detuning, then adiabatically ramps into the fully ordered or disordered phase and reads out the change in Rydberg-state population, $\delta n$. Population transfer appears as a peak in $\delta n$ whenever the modulation frequency $f$ matches a ground-to-excited-state energy gap; the peak height is proportional to the squared transition matrix element between those states — essentially Fermi's golden rule applied to a coherent many-body drive.

**Symmetry selection.** Because the ground state and a spatially uniform (global) modulation are both even under reflection, global modulation alone only ever populates even-parity excited states. To reach odd-parity states, the team applies a spatially patterned local-detuning modulation with an odd reflection symmetry, which selectively couples to the odd sector instead — combining both modulation types maps out the complete spectrum.

**Ising CFT spectrum.** Tuned to the Ising critical detuning on a 19-atom chain, global modulation reveals four clean population-transfer peaks whose center frequencies match numerically predicted excited-state energies, with peak heights tracking the predicted transition strengths. The measured ratio between these four peaks is **2:4:6:8** — exactly the CFT-predicted ratio for the even-parity sector.

**Worked equation box — where the ratio comes from.**

$$k_n = \frac{\pi}{L}\left(n + \tfrac{1}{2}\right), \qquad E_n = \hbar v\, k_n$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$k_n$ : the quantized momentum of the $n$-th single-particle (Majorana fermion) mode in an open chain of length $L$
$v$ : a non-universal "light-cone" velocity, set by the microscopic couplings (not itself universal)
$E_n$ : the energy of that single-particle mode

**What this actually means:** individual fermion modes come in a half-integer-spaced ladder ($n+\tfrac12$), so their energies land at $\tfrac12, \tfrac32, \tfrac52, \dots$ times a common base unit. Physical excited states require an *even number* of these fermions (Table 1), and combining specific pairs of modes reproduces exactly the observed 2:4:6:8 ladder — a direct, checkable consequence of the half-integer quantization rule above, not a coincidence of the fit.

**Finite-size scaling.** Repeating this measurement at chain lengths $L = 7$ to $35$ and rescaling the modulation frequency by $L$ collapses all the spectra onto the same universal rescaled energies (Fig. 2) — the direct experimental signature of CFT finite-size scaling (energies shrink as $1/L$). A chi-square test confirms the data match the Ising CFT prediction within uncertainty for $L \geq 19$; smaller chains show sizable non-universal corrections (see Limitations). Odd-parity spectroscopy via local modulation reveals peaks interleaved between the even ones, in the ratio **3:5:7**, matching the CFT's odd-sector prediction.

**Momentum resolution and the light cone.** Applying modulation at nonzero wavevector reveals a linear dispersion, $E = \hbar v k_n$ — the emergent "light cone" of a relativistic-like 1+1D field theory — and lets the team extract the non-universal velocity $v$ directly from where the response turns on, in agreement with numerics. This confirms linear dispersion itself, not just the level *counting*, as a genuine feature of the emergent field theory.

**The tricritical point.** The TCI CFT sits at the end of the second-order line and is a genuinely interacting theory (no free-fermion rewrite), with richer boundary physics: it supports two distinct *stable* boundary conditions, conventionally "fixed" (edges strongly promote CDW order) and "free" (edges suppress it), each giving different universal energy ratios. The team locates the TCI point numerically (specific $V_2$ and critical detuning where the low-lying spectrum matches TCI predictions), then measures a boundary CDW order parameter $\sigma_\text{edge}$ as a function of interaction strength approaching the TCI point — it drops toward near-zero right at TCI, suggestive of the system naturally landing near a "free"-boundary-like configuration. Spectroscopically, the second-to-first excited-state energy ratio $E_2/E_1$ shifts from the Ising value of 2 (confirmed experimentally at $2.08(4)$ away from TCI) to $1.45(25)$ at the TCI point — consistent with the TCI free-boundary prediction of $4/3$.

**Deliberately tuning the boundary condition.** By adding engineered local detunings at the two chain edges (emulating extra "ancilla" atoms pinned to the Rydberg state), the team continuously tunes a dimensionless boundary-strength parameter $\eta \in [0,1]$ from free ($\eta=0$) to fixed ($\eta=1$). The measured $E_2/E_1$ ratio traces out a non-monotonic curve, peaking near an *unstable* intermediate fixed point at $\eta_c \approx 0.39$ where the measured ratio, $2.5(4)$, is close to the TCI prediction of $10/3$ for that unstable boundary condition — direct experimental evidence that TCI supports (at least) three distinct boundary universality classes, not just two.

**The dynamical structure factor.** A second protocol (modulation without an intervening ramp) accesses, in the short-modulation-time limit, the dynamical structure factor $S(k,\omega)$ — the Fourier transform of the system's space-time correlations. The Ising CFT predicts this should be frequency-independent at $k=0$ and low frequency for the energy-density field $\varepsilon$ (whose scaling dimension is exactly 1, making $S \propto \omega^{2\Delta_\varepsilon - 1} = \omega^0$, a constant). A 35-atom chain shows an approximately constant low-frequency response, consistent with a tensor-network (TDVP) numerical simulation of the same protocol — an encouraging first access point to genuine dynamical (not just static-spectrum) CFT correlations.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** Reader might assume "observing CFT spectra" here means the same kind of thing prior neutron-scattering or quantum-Hall experiments already showed (a bulk power-law exponent). The paper's actual achievement is finer-grained and harder to fake: resolving *individual discrete many-body excitation energies* in a finite system and matching their precise ratios (2:4:6:8, 3:5:7, 4/3, 2, 10/3) directly against CFT operator-content predictions.

> **Watch:** Reader might assume the Rydberg chain is a stand-in or proxy for some specific real solid-state material undergoing this transition. It's closer to the opposite — the Rydberg array is a genuine, microscopically-known physical system in its own right (the FSS spin model), used to directly probe universal CFT physics that, by definition, doesn't care what the microscopic realization is.

> **Watch:** Reader might assume the Ising and tricritical Ising measurements required fundamentally different experimental techniques. They didn't — the exact same modulation-spectroscopy toolkit is used for both; only the tuned point in parameter space, and which theoretical spectrum the results are compared against, differs.

Modulation spectroscopy as a new quantum-simulator technique.** The core methodological contribution is a coherent, resonant, weak sinusoidal-drive protocol (with careful ramp-in/ramp-out for state preparation and readout) purpose-built to resolve individual many-body excitation energies and their transition matrix elements in an analog quantum simulator — genuinely new to this platform, not a repurposing of an existing standard tool. # 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> Small system sizes show sizable non-universal corrections — the chi-square test confirms agreement with the Ising CFT prediction only for $L \geq 19$; a 7-atom chain, while useful for demonstrating the technique (Rabi oscillations, coherence), does not itself cleanly show the universal ratio. **(A) Consensus** — explicitly quantified in the paper's own statistical analysis (§"Analysis of measured spectra"); finite-size corrections at small $L$ are a standard, expected feature of any finite-size-scaling measurement, not a controversial point. **(paper, Methods)**

> The quench-dynamics measurement at the TCI point (Extended Data Fig. 6) shows fast-decaying, weakly coherent oscillations, attributed to laser intensity/frequency noise and $V_2$ calibration uncertainty, and is explicitly described by the authors as only a qualitative proof-of-principle rather than a precision cross-check of the modulation-spectroscopy result. **(A) Consensus** — the paper states this limitation directly rather than overselling the agreement. **(paper, Methods, "Quench dynamics at the TCI point")**

> The dynamical-structure-factor result (constant low-frequency response of the $\varepsilon$ field) is framed as "an encouraging first indication" rather than a fully quantitative confirmation of the predicted universal scaling exponent, and a harder companion measurement (the $\sigma$-field correlator, with its more distinctive $\Delta_\sigma = 1/8$ scaling) is left to future work requiring an additional tweezer wavelength. **(B) Contested** — the paper's own framing is appropriately cautious, but a specialist could reasonably view the demonstrated flat-response result, backed by matching tensor-network numerics, as fairly convincing evidence already; how much weight to place on a qualitative-regime result before the harder companion measurement exists is a judgment call rather than a settled point. **(paper, Discussion and outlook)**

# 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

Conformal field theories make unusually precise predictions about how a system behaves right at a continuous phase transition — not just a scaling exponent, but a full discrete ladder of excitation energies (in a finite system) whose ratios are universal numbers, independent of microscopic detail. These predictions have existed since the 1980s, but no one had ever directly measured that discrete spectrum in a real physical system, solid-state or otherwise — the individual energy levels of an interacting many-body system are simply very hard to resolve.

This paper does it using a programmable array of individually-trapped Rydberg atoms as a quantum simulator, tuned precisely to two different quantum phase transitions: an ordinary Ising transition, and a rarer tricritical Ising (TCI) multicritical point. The key new tool is modulation spectroscopy — briefly and coherently "shaking" the system at a tunable frequency near its critical point, then reading out which excited states got populated. At the Ising transition, this reveals four excitation energies in the exact ratio 2:4:6:8, and a companion measurement using a spatially patterned drive reveals the "hidden" odd-parity levels in the ratio 3:5:7 — both are the CFT's predicted signatures, arising from the transition's underlying description in terms of free, linearly-dispersing Majorana fermions. Repeating the measurement at several chain lengths and rescaling by system size collapses all the data onto the same universal curve, directly confirming the CFT's characteristic finite-size scaling.

At the harder TCI point — a genuinely interacting CFT with no free-particle shortcut — the team shows that different boundary conditions (how the two ends of the chain are treated) select entirely different universal spectra from the same underlying bulk physics. Left alone, the system naturally sits near a "free" boundary condition (ratio 4/3); adding engineered pinning fields at the edges can push it smoothly toward a "fixed" boundary condition (ratio 2), and in between, the data pick out a third, unstable boundary condition (ratio close to 10/3) that theory predicts should exist but is delicate to actually realize. A separate variant of the modulation technique also picks up a first signal of the dynamical structure factor — a genuinely time-dependent correlation function, rather than just a static spectrum — showing the flat, frequency-independent low-frequency response the CFT predicts for one of its fields.

I've framed this summary around the specific, checkable numbers (2:4:6:8, 3:5:7, 4/3, 2, 10/3) rather than walking through the CFT formalism in detail, because those numbers are exactly what makes this a falsifiable, reproducible experimental claim rather than a qualitative "we saw criticality" result — and they're the part any future replication attempt, on this or another platform, would actually be checking.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the finer mathematical structure connecting specific pairs of occupied fermion modes to the exact observed energy ratios (Table 1's combinatorics), and the detailed boundary-CFT fusion-rule arguments in the Methods (which primaries appear under which boundary condition, and why), are dense enough that my plain-language description captures the *result* accurately but likely loses some of the derivation's internal logic — if you want to push on exactly *why* a given pair of fermion modes produces a given energy ratio, or exactly how the Cardy-state fusion rules pick out which TCI fields appear at which boundary, that's the part worth unpacking further rather than taking my summary as the full derivation.

# 9. Shorter Summary

At a continuous phase transition, physical systems become governed by a conformal field theory (CFT) — a mathematical framework that makes unusually precise predictions, including an exact discrete ladder of excitation energies whose *ratios* are universal, the same for any system in the same category regardless of its microscopic makeup. These predictions have existed since the 1980s but had never been directly measured in a real system, because individually resolving many-body excitation energies is extremely hard.

This paper does it, using a fully programmable array of individually trapped and laser-controlled atoms as a quantum simulator. The team developed a new measurement technique — briefly and coherently "shaking" the atom chain at a tunable frequency while it's tuned exactly to a phase transition, then reading out which excited states light up. At an ordinary (Ising-type) transition, this reveals four excitation energies in the ratio 2:4:6:8, exactly as theory predicts, and the pattern holds up correctly as the chain length is varied — the hallmark signature of universal, scale-free physics.

They then pushed the technique further, to a rarer and genuinely harder-to-analyze transition point (called tricritical Ising) where the physics can no longer be reduced to independent, non-interacting particles. There, they showed something CFT theory predicts but nobody had shown experimentally before: how the two ends of the atom chain are treated — pinned versus free — can select an entirely different universal spectrum from the exact same underlying bulk physics, with the ratios shifting between predicted values as that boundary condition is smoothly tuned. They even found direct evidence of an unstable "in-between" boundary condition, a delicate theoretical prediction realized experimentally for the first time.

Beyond confirming decades-old theory, this establishes a technique other researchers can use going forward: a way to directly probe the emergent, universal field theory at any quantum phase transition realized in a programmable quantum simulator — including transitions whose underlying universality class isn't known in advance and can't easily be worked out by classical computer simulation. It's a single lab's result on one specific hardware platform so far, with the harder, more distinctive parts of the measurement (like the finer dynamical correlation function) explicitly flagged by the authors as preliminary, promising first steps rather than finished, fully quantitative confirmations.

**Q: explain emergent conformal field theory**

An emergent conformal field theory (CFT) describes a phenomenon where a microscopic quantum or statistical system — governed by discrete lattice interactions or non-relativistic physics at short distances (UV) — flows in the long-distance, low-energy limit (IR) to a scale- and conformally invariant fixed point. Conformal symmetry is not present in the fundamental microscopic Hamiltonian; it emerges dynamically as irrelevant microscopic details wash out under coarse-graining.

**The Mechanism: Renormalization Group (RG) Flow**

*Vanishing Beta Functions:* Under the RG flow, coupling constants $g_i$ evolve according to their beta functions. At a continuous phase transition or quantum critical point, the system hits a fixed point where $\beta(g_i) = \partial g_i/\partial\ln\mu = 0$.

*Diverging Correlation Length:* At criticality, the correlation length diverges ($\xi\to\infty$). The microscopic lattice spacing $a$ becomes effectively infinitesimal ($a/\xi\to0$), wiping out microscopic anisotropies and restoring continuous translational and rotational symmetry.

*Symmetry Enhancement:* In unitary, local relativistic field theories (and many non-relativistic systems with dynamic critical exponent $z=1$), scale invariance automatically enhances to full conformal invariance: $x^\mu\to x'^\mu$, $g_{\mu\nu}(x)\to\Omega^2(x)g_{\mu\nu}(x)$.

**Dimensional Behavior and Classifications**

*Two Dimensions (d=2):* The conformal group is infinite-dimensional, generated by two commuting copies of the Virasoro algebra with central charge $c$: $[L_m,L_n]=(m-n)L_{m+n}+\tfrac{c}{12}m(m^2-1)\delta_{m+n,0}$. This rigid algebraic structure completely fixes critical exponents, correlation functions, and Operator Product Expansions (OPEs) for minimal models.

*Higher Dimensions (d≥3):* The conformal group is finite-dimensional, isomorphic to $SO(d+1,1)$ in Euclidean space. Critical phenomena are classified non-perturbatively via the modern conformal bootstrap, which extracts universal scaling dimensions from crossing symmetry and unitarity bounds.

**Key Physical Realizations**

- *Lattice Spin Systems:* The classical 2D Ising model on a square lattice has only discrete $\mathbb{Z}_2$ spin-flip and 4-fold rotational symmetry. At $T=T_c$, it flows to the $c=1/2$ free Majorana fermion CFT.
- *1D Quantum Criticality:* The 1D antiferromagnetic spin-1/2 Heisenberg chain flows in the IR to the $SU(2)_1$ Wess-Zumino-Witten (WZW) model or Luttinger liquid ($c=1$ compactified boson).
- *Fractional Quantum Hall Edges:* The 1D chiral gapless edge states of fractional quantum Hall liquids emerge as chiral CFTs (e.g., Kac-Moody current algebras).
- *AdS/CFT Correspondence:* Emergent boundary CFTs provide the exact microscopic holographic definition of quantum gravity in higher-dimensional Anti-de Sitter bulk geometries.

## Appendix — Deep-Dive Discussion Log (2026-08-22)

*The following exchange took place in a Claude deep-dive chat for this paper.*

**Q: explain: emergent conformal field theory**

"Emergent" here is doing real work, not just decorative language — it's pointing at a specific, slightly counterintuitive fact about where the CFT description comes from.

The microscopic system in this paper — the FSS model on a chain of Rydberg atoms — has none of a CFT's defining symmetries built in. It's a lattice of discrete atoms, sitting at discrete positions, interacting through van der Waals forces that fall off as $1/r^6$. There's no continuous spacetime, no Lorentz invariance, no scale invariance, and definitely no conformal invariance in the microscopic Hamiltonian. It's just a finite set of qubits coupled to their neighbors.

And yet, tune that lattice system to exactly the critical point — the second-order transition line in Fig. 1a — and something happens that isn't visible in the microscopic description at all: correlations start extending across arbitrarily long distances, the system becomes insensitive to exactly how far apart the atoms are or exactly what the coupling strength was, and at long distances/low energies the effective description that falls out is a continuum field theory with the full conformal symmetry group (scale invariance plus the extra special-conformal transformations that come with it in 1+1D — the Virasoro algebra machinery). None of that symmetry was put in by hand. It appears only in the low-energy, long-wavelength limit, as a kind of statistical accident of criticality — which is exactly what "emergent" means in condensed matter, as opposed to a theory like AdS/CFT where conformal symmetry is more like a starting postulate on one side of the duality.

The Majorana-fermion analogy from the background section captures the mechanism concretely: the actual atoms don't behave like free relativistic fermions, but right at criticality, the *effective* low-energy excitations do — reorganizing into modes with the linear dispersion $E = \hbar v k$ (a "light cone," Section 4) that has no business appearing in a system of non-relativistic trapped atoms. That linear dispersion emerging is the microscopic-to-continuum handoff in action: short-distance physics (exact atom spacing, exact Rabi frequency) stays completely non-universal and platform-specific, while the long-distance layer converges onto the same CFT that *any* other microscopic system in the same universality class would produce — a different lattice model, a different atomic species, even a real solid-state magnet, if it flowed to the same fixed point.

That's also why the paper's central claim is falsifiable in such a sharp way: because the CFT is emergent rather than assumed, its specific predictions (2:4:6:8, the TCI boundary ratios) are genuine consequences of the atoms' interactions landing on that universality class — not something guaranteed by the setup. If the platform, hardware, or theory were slightly wrong, the emergent description could easily have failed to be Ising or TCI at all.
