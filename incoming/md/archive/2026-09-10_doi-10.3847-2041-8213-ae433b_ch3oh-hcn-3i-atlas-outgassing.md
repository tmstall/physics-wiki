## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**"The Comet From Another Star Is Leaking Way More Rocket Fuel Than Cyanide, and the Two Gases Aren't Even Coming From the Same Side of It."**

3I/ATLAS — only the third interstellar object ever confirmed — is outgassing methanol and hydrogen cyanide in proportions almost never seen in a solar system comet, and the two molecules trace back to opposite hemispheres of the nucleus.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Interstellar objects are visitors: chunks of material ejected from other star systems' planet-forming disks that happen to pass through ours. We've only ever confirmed three — 1I/'Oumuamua (2017, no detectable coma at all), 2I/Borisov (2019, a fairly "normal" comet chemically), and now 3I/ATLAS, discovered in mid-2025. Each one is a free sample of chemistry baked under a different star, and every measurement of what's actually in the coma (the gas/dust envelope a comet sheds as sunlight warms its ices) is a data point on how universal — or not — our own solar system's protoplanetary chemistry really is.

3I/ATLAS has already turned out to be compositionally strange by every measure taken so far. JWST found a coma dominated by CO2 rather than water at r_H=3.32 au, with CO2/H2O and CO2/CO ratios well above typical comets — read either as evidence of heavy radiation processing or formation close to the CO2 ice line. Optical spectroscopy found an unusually high nickel-to-iron ratio. This paper adds a third independent axis: radio detections of methanol (CH3OH) and hydrogen cyanide (HCN), mapped with enough spatial resolution to ask not just "how much of each molecule" but "where on the nucleus is each one coming from."

The instrument matters here: this used the ALMA **Atacama Compact Array (ACA)** — the array's twelve 7-meter dishes, as opposed to the flagship 12-meter array. The ACA trades resolving power for much better sensitivity to faint, extended, low-surface-brightness structure — exactly what you want for a coma, which is diffuse gas, not a point source. Six epochs were taken pre-perihelion (comet reached perihelion at q=1.35 au on 2025 October 29), spanning r_H = 2.6→1.7 au, targeting a 13-line CH3OH ladder and the HCN J=4–3 transition in ALMA Band 7 (~338–354 GHz).

**Paper Type & Stakes:** This is an observational interferometric-mapping paper with substantial radiative-transfer modeling attached — not a discovery-of-a-new-object paper, but a compositional/kinematic characterization of an object whose very existence is already the discovery. The stakes are calibrating how "normal" or "exotic" interstellar material is, one molecule at a time, while the object is still observable (interstellar objects pass through once and never come back).

**Prior Belief Check:** The general theme — 3I/ATLAS being chemically anomalous — is no longer surprising to experts; it's now the third independent line of evidence for that view (after CO2/H2O and Ni/Fe), so this is confirmatory of a mounting pattern rather than a shock result on its own. What *is* genuinely surprising, and would raise eyebrows among cometary scientists, is the specific finding that HCN and CH3OH production peak in *opposite* hemispheres of the coma. The naive expectation — more sunlight, more sublimation, on the sunward side, for everything — is violated by HCN. That's the part of this paper that isn't just "another exotic abundance number."

**Replication & Convergence Note:** This is a single-facility (ALMA ACA), single-team result for the spatially resolved CH3OH story — no independent group has reproduced these maps. The HCN production rate, however, gets a genuine (if imperfect) independent cross-check: the JCMT single-dish detected HCN on nearby dates (Coulson et al. 2026), and once beam-size and expansion-velocity assumptions are reconciled, the two instruments agree within 1σ. That's convergence on the bulk number, not on the spatially resolved asymmetry — nothing else has resolved *where* the HCN comes from.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Concept 1 — Coma production, parent vs. daughter species.** A comet's nucleus is a lump of ice and dust. As it nears the Sun, ice sublimates (goes solid → gas directly) into an expanding cloud called the coma. Some molecules ("parent species") come off the nucleus surface directly and start decaying (getting photodissociated by sunlight) the instant they're released. Others are "daughter species" or come from an *extended source* — they don't leave the nucleus as themselves; they're released some distance out, either because they're a fragment of a parent molecule breaking apart, or because they're riding along on icy dust grains that only shed their own ice once they've drifted away from the nucleus and warmed up a bit. The **Haser model** (1957) is the standard toy model for both cases, and it's the load-bearing tool this entire paper's "where does the molecule come from" argument rests on.

**Analogy:** Think of it as a two-stage TTL (time-to-live) cache-expiration pipeline. The nucleus is a write source emitting "objects" (molecules) at some rate Q (production rate, molecules/s), each moving outward at velocity v. A pure parent species is a cache entry that starts decaying the instant it's written — its number density falls off as a simple exponential with distance, entirely predictable once you know Q, v, and its decay rate β (photodissociation rate, in s⁻¹). A daughter/extended-source species is different: it doesn't get "written" at the origin at all — it's the *output* of an upstream process (the parent decaying, or a grain shedding ice) that itself only starts appearing at some effective delay downstream. That delay, converted into a distance via v/β_parent, is the **parent scale length**, L_p. L_p=0 means "written at the origin" (direct nucleus release). L_p>0 means "the object doesn't even start existing until some distance out" — a delayed cache-fill.

*Worked equation box — the Haser daughter-density profile:*

$$n_d(r) = \frac{Q\,\beta_p}{4\pi v\, r^2 (\beta_d-\beta_p)}\Big[e^{-\beta_p r/v} - e^{-\beta_d r/v}\Big]$$

**Symbol definitions:**
n_d(r): number density of the daughter/extended-source molecule at distance r from the nucleus (molecules/cm³).
Q: production rate of the parent feeding this species (s⁻¹).
v: outflow (expansion) velocity (km/s).
β_p, β_d: decay rates (s⁻¹) of the parent and of the daughter species itself. L_p ≡ v/β_p is the parent scale length reported in the paper's Table 1.

**What this actually means:** near the nucleus (r→0), this profile goes to *zero* — because the daughter hasn't been "produced" from its parent yet; there's a rise-then-fall shape, peaking somewhere out at ~L_p, then falling off at large r once its own decay dominates. Contrast this with a pure parent (L_p=0): n_d(r) ∝ e^(-βr/v)/r², a simple monotonic falloff starting immediately at the nucleus — like a cache-write followed by instant, ordinary staleness decay, versus a value that has to propagate through an upstream dependency before it even shows up. This is exactly the shape difference the paper's statistical machinery (below) is built to detect.

**Breaks when:** the TTL-cache picture assumes a clean, single-stage delay. Real coma physics can have a *distribution* of grain sizes and ejection speeds feeding the daughter population continuously and non-uniformly (not a single fixed L_p), and the paper itself notes this is a simplification — "the simplest model capable of fitting the observed asymmetric line profile," not a claim that there's literally one discrete production shell.

**Concept 2 — Interferometer baselines probe different spatial scales.** ALMA doesn't take a picture the way a camera does — each pair of antennas (a "baseline," with a physical separation and thus an angular scale it's sensitive to) measures one number (a complex "visibility") that encodes structure at one particular angular scale. Short baselines (antennas close together) are only sensitive to *broad, extended* structure spread over a wide area; long baselines (antennas far apart) are sensitive to *fine, compact* structure concentrated in a small area, and are blind to the broad stuff.

**Analogy:** this is a level-of-detail (LOD) / mip-map hierarchy, run in reverse. In a rendering pipeline, a coarse mip level captures the low-frequency "big picture" of a texture over the whole frame and can't represent fine detail; a fine mip level captures detail but only within a narrow crop. Short ALMA baselines are the coarse mip level — they "see" the whole extended coma but blur past anything compact near the nucleus; long baselines are the fine level — they zoom in on structure within a few thousand km of the nucleus but are essentially deaf to anything spread out over tens of thousands of km. The paper explicitly extracts spectra "from differing baseline ranges" (Figures 2, 3, 7–12) precisely to use this — if a line's shape or strength changes systematically as you go from short to long baselines, that's direct evidence the emission isn't all coming from one compact spot.

**Breaks when:** you push the mip-map picture to imply the data can be "reconstructed" cleanly level by level the way a renderer would — real interferometers have sparse, irregular baseline coverage (not a clean discrete pyramid), which is exactly why this paper deliberately fits models directly to the raw visibilities in the Fourier domain rather than first building an image with CLEAN and then measuring it — CLEAN's deconvolution step can inject its own artifacts from that same sparse coverage, and they explicitly avoid it for this reason (Appendix C).

**Central analogy for this paper:** *A comet nucleus as a mixed-TTL, multi-region cache.*

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

The observing strategy: six ACA epochs (Aug 28 through Oct 1, 2025) in Band 7, each hunting either the CH3OH rotational ladder (up to 13 lines spanning excitation energies E_u=16.8–114.8 K — i.e., lines that only "light up" if the gas is warm enough to populate that particular energy level) or the HCN J=4–3 line (only Sept 12 and 15). Two model geometries were fit to the resulting spectra and maps: a **1D model** (spherically symmetric, but with a global bulk Doppler offset Δv as a single free parameter standing in for any real asymmetry — a coarse patch, not a real geometric split) and a genuine **3D model** that splits the coma into a sunward cone (region R1) and everything else (region R2), each region getting its *own* independent production rate, expansion velocity, and scale length.

The headline result is a contrast in outgassing geometry between the two molecules. For **HCN**, the line profile is significantly redshifted (shifted toward the observer receding, i.e. away) on both Sept 12 and 15, and the 3D fit attributes this to the coma's antisunward (shadow-side) hemisphere being wildly more productive than the sunward side — 25× more productive on Sept 12, dropping to only 1.4× on Sept 15 (three days later — but remember, the nucleus's own rotation period is 16.16 hours, so 72 hours is roughly 4.5 full rotations; the "sunward-facing" surface patch at each epoch is not the same patch). Statistically, HCN's scale length is fully consistent with L_p=0 — indistinguishable from ordinary direct nucleus sublimation, at 99% confidence.

**CH3OH tells the opposite spatial story.** Its line shows a *blueward* offset (opposite sign from HCN) and an asymmetric profile that, when fit in 3D, comes out *more* productive from the sunward side (ratio Q1/Q2 = 1.6±0.5 on Sept 18). More importantly, the scale-length statistics say something HCN's don't: **CH3OH shows evidence of genuine extended-source (delayed-release) production, not pure direct sublimation.**

*Worked example — how the L_p>258 km result was actually obtained.* The paper doesn't just fit one value of L_p and report an error bar; it sweeps L_p across a wide range (100 km to ~10,000 km), refits the production rate and velocity at each fixed L_p, and records the goodness-of-fit statistic χ²(L_p) at each point — building a curve, Δχ²(L_p) = χ²(L_p)−χ²_min, using cubic-spline interpolation between the sampled points (Figure 3(b)). This is precisely a hyperparameter sweep with a validation-loss curve: L_p is the hyperparameter, χ² is the loss, and the "acceptable region" is everything within a tolerance band of the minimum. The tolerance thresholds come from standard χ² statistics for one degree of freedom: Δχ²=1 defines the 1σ (68%) band, Δχ²=6.63 defines the 99% band. Reading off Figure 3(b): the χ² curve stays flat (acceptable) out past L_p=258 km before climbing above the 99%-confidence threshold — meaning values of L_p below 258 km are excluded at 99% confidence. That's a real, extended, non-zero scale length — CH3OH is not behaving like a pure parent.

But — and this is a genuinely important asterisk the paper is careful about — this doesn't prove *all* the CH3OH comes from an extended source. The data has poor signal-to-noise on the longest baselines (the "fine mip level," in Section 3's analogy — the part of the data that would show a compact, nucleus-hugging component if one existed), so a smaller *additional* direct-release contribution can't be statistically excluded. The 1D and 3D best-fit L_p values themselves (2884+1860/-1192 km and 4107+2621/-1820 km, respectively) agree with each other within 1σ, but carry enormous uncertainty ranges — this is a "yes, there's an extended component" result, not a "we've precisely located the source" result.

**Assumption Audit**

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes higher production on the sunward (illuminated) side reflects simple insolation-driven sublimation — more sunlight, more gas, everywhere. The paper actually finds HCN does the *opposite*: 25× more production from the shadow side on Sept 12. The authors explicitly flag that this might be a real compositional feature (heterogeneous nucleus) *or* an artifact of catching different rotational faces on different dates — they do not resolve which.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes detecting both molecules "in the coma" means they're sampling the same underlying reservoir. The paper's interpretive frame is the opposite — it explicitly reaches for the Rosetta/67P precedent of a nucleus with chemically distinct hemispheres, meaning HCN and CH3OH may be tracing physically separate ice reservoirs on the nucleus, not two readouts of one well-mixed ice.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes "L_p>258 km at 99% confidence" settles that CH3OH is purely an extended-source species. It doesn't — the paper explicitly states low S/N on long baselines "prevented definitively ruling out CH3OH as purely a parent species." The 99%-confidence statement rules out L_p=0 as the *only* source; it doesn't rule out a mixed origin.

> **Watch:** Reader likely assumes that because the 3D (two-hemisphere) model visibly fits the asymmetric CH3OH line shape better by eye (Figure 3(c)), it's the statistically preferred description. The paper's own F-test says otherwise: F=1.45 against a 95%-confidence critical value of 2.99 — they cannot reject the simpler 1D model. The extra geometric complexity is not statistically required by this dataset, even though it's the more physically motivated picture.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**1. Spatially resolved, kinematically resolved coma mapping of an interstellar object — a genuine step up in resolution class.** Prior 3I/ATLAS chemistry results (JWST CO2/H2O, optical Ni/Fe, JCMT single-dish HCN) are line-integrated abundance numbers — bulk composition, no spatial information. This paper resolves *where in the coma* each species comes from and how its line profile evolves across interferometric baselines, turning a bulk-abundance question into a nucleus-heterogeneity question. That's new to the field for this object, not just new to the reader.

**2. Fitting directly in the Fourier (visibility) domain instead of on CLEAN images.** This is a genuinely careful methodological choice, not a novel algorithm — but it matters: it sidesteps the deconvolution artifacts that CLEAN's iterative point-source-removal process can inject into faint, extended emission, which is exactly the regime a comet coma lives in. It's the difference between fitting a model against raw telemetry versus against a rendered, potentially lossy dashboard built from that telemetry.

**3. The record-second CH3OH/HCN ratio.** At 124+30/-34 and 79+11/-14, these ratios sit 1.2σ–4.7σ above the solar system comet population mean (26±27), and are surpassed by only one previously known comet — the famously anomalous C/2016 R2 (Pan-STARRS), at 280±72. This is a genuinely new, extreme data point on the existing solar-system-comet CH3OH/HCN distribution (Figure 4(d)), not a reinterpretation of old data.

**Predictive Content Check**

*Falsifiable handle (always):* Two concrete, checkable predictions exist. First, the fitted power law Q(CH3OH)(r_H) = (378±116)×10²⁶ r_H^-(5.2±0.6) s⁻¹ makes a specific, testable prediction for how sharply methanol production should keep climbing as 3I approaches (and passes) perihelion — a wrong exponent or a flattening trend would falsify it directly against future ALMA epochs. Second, whether HCN's antisunward dominance is a genuine compositional signature or a rotational-phase artifact is directly falsifiable: further epochs sampling the full 16.16-hour rotation should either keep showing the same hemisphere dominating (heterogeneous-nucleus interpretation survives) or show the "hot side" tracking the Sun as the nucleus turns (projection-effect interpretation wins). Neither test has been run yet.

*Formalism load (conditional):* Skipped deliberately. The SUBLIME radiative-transfer code, the Haser scale-length formalism, and the Fourier-domain χ²/F-test machinery are all pre-existing, previously validated tools (Cordiner et al. 2022–2025) applied to new data, not novel mathematical machinery invented for this result — the math is doing real, necessary work, but it isn't this paper's own contribution, so the "is it decorative" question doesn't really apply here.

## 6. Limitations & Open Questions

> Low signal-to-noise on the longest (highest-resolution) baselines means the data cannot rule out a direct-nucleus (parent) contribution to CH3OH alongside the detected extended-source component. **(A) Consensus** — this is explicitly and repeatedly stated by the authors themselves as the reason they can only claim a *lower bound* on L_p, not a full characterization. **(paper §Abstract, §3.1)**

> HCN's antisunward-dominant outgassing could be a real heterogeneous-nucleus signature, or could simply be a projection effect from sampling different rotational faces of the nucleus on Sept 12 vs. 15 (72 hours apart, ~4.5 rotations at the measured 16.16 hr period). **(B) Contested** — the authors themselves raise this as a live, unresolved alternative and explicitly call for more HCN observations to test it; this isn't a settled disagreement in the broader field, it's an open question the paper itself poses. **(paper §4.1)**

> The two-hemisphere (3D) geometric model, despite visually fitting the asymmetric CH3OH line shape better, is not statistically preferred over the simpler spherically-symmetric-plus-offset (1D) model at 95% confidence (F-test, F=1.45 vs. critical value 2.99). **(A) Consensus** — this is the paper's own explicit statistical result, not an outside critique; both models are presented as consistent with the data. **(paper Appendix C.3)**

> Expansion velocities and kinetic temperatures on several dates (Aug 28, Sept 22, Oct 1 for CH3OH) were not independently retrieved from those epochs' own data — they were fixed or scaled (v ∝ r_H^0.5) from the one date (Sept 18) with high enough S/N and spectral resolution to constrain them directly. **(B) Contested** — this is standard, well-established practice in the field when S/N is limiting, and the paper is transparent about it, but it does mean several of the tabulated production rates carry an assumed-not-measured kinematic input, which the paper's own text flags as a real limitation. **(paper §3.1, §4.2)**

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

Roth et al. used the ALMA Atacama Compact Array to observe interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS across six epochs between late August and early October 2025 (heliocentric distance r_H = 2.6→1.7 au, pre-perihelion), detecting methanol (CH3OH, up to 13 rotational lines) and hydrogen cyanide (HCN, the J=4–3 transition). Fitting both spherically symmetric (1D) and hemisphere-split (3D, sunward vs. antisunward) non-LTE radiative-transfer models directly to the interferometric visibilities (deliberately avoiding CLEAN-image artifacts), they found the two molecules behave in starkly different — in fact geometrically opposite — ways. HCN's line profile is redshifted and its production is statistically consistent with ordinary direct sublimation off the nucleus surface, but concentrated (at least on Sept 12) overwhelmingly in the antisunward, shadowed hemisphere — the opposite of what simple sunlight-driven sublimation would predict. CH3OH, meanwhile, shows a blueshifted, sunward-leaning asymmetry, and its production rate as a function of distance from the nucleus is statistically inconsistent with pure direct release: some component of it must be coming from an extended source (icy grains lofted into the coma before releasing their own ice) at scale lengths beyond ~258 km, though a smaller directly-released component can't be excluded given the data's noise on the finest angular scales. CH3OH's overall production rate also rose sharply and steeply (as r_H^-5.2±0.6) between August and October, including a notable uptick right around r_H=2 au — the inner edge of where water ice typically starts sublimating in earnest, suggesting CH3OH production is coupled to the broader activation of the comet's water-ice engine. The resulting CH3OH/HCN production-rate ratios (124 and 79, on the two HCN-observing dates) are among the highest ever measured in any comet — second only to the famously peculiar C/2016 R2 (Pan-STARRS) — reinforcing, via a third independent chemical tracer, the picture already emerging from JWST's CO2/H2O measurements and optical Ni/Fe ratios that 3I/ATLAS is compositionally unusual relative to solar system comets.

The interpretive framing the authors reach for — a nucleus with heterogeneous ice composition across its surface, following the precedent set by Rosetta's direct imaging of 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko's two chemically distinct hemispheres — is the most physically motivated reading, but it's explicitly offered as one candidate explanation among at least two (the other being a rotational/projection artifact), and the paper is careful not to overclaim which one is correct.

**Where I'm least confident in this analysis:** the exact algebraic form of the Haser daughter-density equation as printed in the paper's Appendix C.1 (their Eq. C1). The OCR/image extraction of that specific equation produced a dimensionally inconsistent grouping of terms, which strongly suggests a transcription artifact from the page image rather than an error in the paper itself — so I presented the standard, textbook Haser (1957) daughter formula instead (the one the paper itself cites and is manifestly built on), rather than risk repeating a garbled version verbatim. The qualitative content of the analysis — the rise-then-fall shape distinguishing extended-source from direct-parent production, and how that maps onto the Δχ²(L_p) scale-length test — doesn't depend on getting that one equation's exact term-grouping letter-perfect, but if you want to cite the paper's precise notation, it's worth checking Appendix C.1 directly against the published PDF rather than this transcription.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. An interstellar comet is outgassing methanol and cyanide from what look like two different sides of its nucleus — the shadow side is pumping out more cyanide than the sunlit side is, which is backwards from the naive "sunlight drives sublimation" intuition, and nobody yet knows if that's a real feature of the object or just an artifact of catching it at the wrong moment in its 16-hour spin.

2. This comet's methanol-to-cyanide ratio is the second-highest ever measured in any comet, solar-system or otherwise — beaten only by one famously freakish comet from our own system — adding a third independent piece of evidence (after CO2-rich JWST data and weird Ni/Fe ratios) that this interstellar visitor formed under, or was processed by, conditions genuinely different from anything that built the comets orbiting our own Sun.

3. The statistical case that methanol is (at least partly) coming from icy dust grains rather than the bare nucleus surface is solid at the 99%-confidence level — but the data isn't sharp enough on its finest angular scales to say methanol *isn't also* coming straight off the nucleus at the same time; it's evidence for "at least one extra source," not proof of "only one source."

## 9. Shorter Summary

3I/ATLAS is only the third interstellar comet ever confirmed, and every chemical measurement of it so far says it's unusual. This study used ALMA's compact array to map methanol and hydrogen cyanide in its coma across six nights in late 2025, with enough spatial resolution to ask not just how much of each gas exists, but where on the comet each one comes from.

The two molecules told opposite stories. Cyanide's spectral line pointed toward the shadowed, non-sunlit side of the coma being far more productive than the sunlit side — backwards from what simple "sunlight drives sublimation" intuition predicts — while its overall production pattern still looked like ordinary direct release from the nucleus surface. Methanol did the reverse: it leaned toward the sunlit side, and statistically, it could not be explained by direct release alone — some of it has to be coming from icy grains that drift away from the nucleus before releasing their own ice, though a smaller directly-released component can't be ruled out given how faint the signal was on the finest angular scales the telescope could resolve.

Methanol's total production also shot up sharply as the comet approached the Sun, and the resulting methanol-to-cyanide ratio turned out to be one of the most extreme ever measured in any comet — second only to one famously bizarre comet from our own solar system. Combined with earlier findings of an unusual carbon-dioxide-rich coma and a strange nickel-to-iron ratio, this adds a third independent line of evidence that 3I/ATLAS's building blocks came from a chemical environment genuinely unlike the one that produced our own solar system's comets.

The authors' favored explanation is that the nucleus itself has chemically different regions — similar to how the European Rosetta mission found the comet 67P had two distinct hemispheres with different ice compositions. But they're upfront that an alternative explanation — simply catching the fast-spinning nucleus in different orientations on different observing nights — hasn't been ruled out either. More observations, especially of cyanide across a full rotation, are needed to settle which explanation is correct.

## Appendix — Deep-Dive Discussion Log (2026-09-11)

**Topic:** Unpacking the Section 4 worked example — how the paper's headline scale-length statistic (CH3OH's L_p > 258 km at 99% confidence) is actually computed.

The reader asked for more detail on the mechanics behind the χ²/Δχ² scale-length sweep, since the original walkthrough moved through it faster than the underlying statistics warranted. The deep dive rebuilt the concept from first principles:

**1. What χ² is, from scratch.** For each data point, take (observed − predicted)/uncertainty — "how many error bars off is the model" — then square and sum across all points. A good model gives χ² roughly equal to the number of data points; a systematically wrong model gives something much larger.

**2. What's actually being fit.** Not an image — interferometric visibilities (Figure 6's Re(Visibility) vs. uv-distance, binned by baseline length). A compact source (small L_p) and an extended source (large L_p) predict different visibility-vs-baseline-length curves, because an extended source's signal gets resolved out faster on long baselines.

**3. Why L_p is *swept* rather than fit directly.** At each candidate L_p (held fixed), the production rate Q and expansion velocity v are re-optimized to their best possible values for that L_p, and only then is χ² recorded. This is a hyperparameter sweep with an inner refit at each grid point — profile likelihood, in the statistics literature (the paper cites Avni 1976 for exactly this technique) — and it's necessary because Q, v, and L_p are partially degenerate; refitting Q and v at each L_p isolates what L_p specifically is doing to the fit quality.

**4. A worked illustrative toy example** (explicitly flagged as hand-constructed round numbers matching the qualitative shape of Figure 6, not digitized values read off the real plot — extraction fidelity from a rendered chart image isn't trustworthy enough to present as exact) walked through computing χ² by hand for two candidate models — a compact (L_p=0) and an extended (L_p≈3000 km) — against four toy visibility data points, showing concretely why the compact model can rack up a much larger χ² when it can't reproduce how fast the signal falls off at long baselines.

**5. Why Δχ²=1 ↔ 68% and Δχ²=6.63 ↔ 99%.** These thresholds come from the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom, which governs the profile-likelihood confidence interval for a *single* parameter of interest (L_p) once the nuisance parameters (Q, v) have been optimized away at each grid point — this is the content behind the paper's own Appendix C.2 statement that "the value of Δχ² ... depends only on the number of parameters being simultaneously estimated, not the number of parameters in the fitting function." The 258 km bound is where the swept Δχ²(L_p) curve crosses the 6.63 threshold on its low-L_p side.

**6. What the result does and doesn't establish**, revisited with this mechanism in hand: it rules out L_p=0 (and everything below 258 km) as a statistically viable *sole* description at 99% confidence — it does not pin down the exact L_p precisely (hence the large asymmetric uncertainty on the best-fit value), and it doesn't exclude a mixed model with some residual direct-nucleus component, which the paper doesn't test.

**Analogy refinement — Breaks when:** the "hyperparameter sweep / validation-loss curve" analogy carries the *mechanics* (sweep a value, retrain nuisance parameters, read off a curve) but not the *statistical guarantee*. A typical ML validation-loss tolerance band is a heuristic; the 1-and-6.63 thresholds here are exact, distribution-derived consequences of Gaussian errors plus one parameter of interest — considerably firmer ground than the analogy alone would suggest.

**Where this might be revisited next, if wanted:** the Levenberg-Marquardt minimizer (`lmfit`) itself, the F-test comparing the 1D and 3D models that follows immediately after this in the main analysis, or the mechanics of how the asymmetric error bars on L_p (e.g. 2884+1860/-1192 km) come out of the same Δχ² curve.
