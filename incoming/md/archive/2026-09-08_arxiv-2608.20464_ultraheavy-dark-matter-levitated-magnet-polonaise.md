## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**A Milligram Magnet Just Out-Muscled a Nanogram Mirror: The First Dedicated Hunt for Dark Matter as Heavy as a Living Cell.**

A floating magnet the size of a grain of sand, watched by a superconducting sensor precise enough to feel a TeV/c nudge, spent nine nights listening for a dark matter particle massive enough to weigh what a bacterium weighs — and heard nothing conclusive, but drew the tightest line yet across seven decades of previously unexplored mass.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

Most dark matter direct-detection experiments — XENONnT, LZ, PandaX, and the rest of the noble-liquid family — are built to catch WIMPs: particles somewhere between a proton's mass and roughly a TeV/c², interacting through ordinary particle collisions in a tank of liquid xenon. That regime dominates the field's funding and headlines for a good reason: it's the mass range the classic "WIMP miracle" thermal-freeze-out calculation points to. But the WIMP window has been closing for two decades without a confirmed signal, and theory never actually required dark matter to live there. A large and growing body of model-building — composite dark matter, dark-sector confinement, primordial black holes formed from density fluctuations, Q-balls, and other non-thermally-produced relics — predicts particles (or particle-like blobs) that are enormously heavier: not GeV-scale, but somewhere between a heavy virus and a bacterium in mass. This is the "ultraheavy dark matter" (UHDM) regime, and it is intrinsically hostile to the xenon-tank strategy: a UHDM particle streaming through the galaxy is so rare (fixed local mass density spread over fewer, heavier particles) that a detector needs to be sensitive to a single passage, not a scattering rate — which calls for a completely different kind of instrument.

That's the gap this paper fills. The POLONAISE collaboration (Leiden University, Rice University, IFAE Barcelona, and NTU Singapore) — the same instrument lineage that reported the first magnetically-levitated search for ultralight dark matter in 2025 (Phys. Rev. Lett. 134, 251001) — points essentially the same apparatus at the opposite end of the mass spectrum. Instead of hunting for a wave-like field oscillating the magnet gently and continuously, this paper hunts for a single, rare, violent kick: one massive dark matter particle scattering once off the levitated magnet and leaving a sudden, lasting jump in its motion. The instrument's job changes from "detect a periodic signal buried in noise" to "detect one anomalous transient in nine days of near-silence" — a search strategy closer to a seismometer waiting for a single distant earthquake than to a radio receiver tuned to a known frequency.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** this is an experimental physics paper reporting a new application — a mass-range extension, not a new detection principle — of an existing levitated-sensor platform. It sets new exclusion limits on ultraheavy dark matter's coupling to ordinary matter across roughly seven decades of previously under-explored particle mass; it does not claim a detection. The stakes are incremental-but-real: this is a genuinely new region of parameter space closed off (or at least fenced in), using a technique — milligram-scale magnetic levitation — young enough that its ultimate sensitivity ceiling isn't yet known.

**Prior Belief Check:** nothing here contradicts mainstream physics or is surprising to specialists. UHDM is an accepted, if minority, corner of the dark matter model space, and levitated-sensor searches for it were already an active, anticipated research direction — this group's own 2025 ultralight paper, and independent optical-levitation UHDM work cited in this paper, both predate it. What is genuinely new is not the idea but the reach: this result pushes seven orders of magnitude past the previous best levitated-sensor limit at the high-mass end, purely by trading a nanogram optically-trapped bead for a milligram magnetically-trapped one. Experts in the sub-field will find the *engineering* achievement — sub-femtonewton force sensitivity sustained on a milligram mass for over a week — more noteworthy than the physics conclusion, which is a null result.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** this is a single-collaboration, single-apparatus result — one specific instrument at Leiden University, operated and analyzed entirely by the POLONAISE team. There is no independent confirmation, and by the nature of an exclusion-limit paper there is nothing yet *to* confirm — no detection is claimed. What would strengthen confidence is a second, independently-built levitated-sensor experiment (a different lab, ideally a different levitation technology — optical or electrostatic rather than magnetic) reproducing a comparable or tighter exclusion curve over the same mass range, the way multiple xenon experiments now cross-check each other in the WIMP regime. That doesn't exist yet for UHDM at the milligram-sensor scale.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

**Concept 1 — What "ultraheavy" actually means here.**
Every dark matter mass claim needs a hook to something you can picture, so: a proton weighs about 1 GeV/c². A typical bacterium weighs roughly a picogram, which works out to about 5.6×10¹¹ GeV/c² — five hundred sixty billion proton masses. A single (larger) human cell, at around a nanogram, is closer to 5.6×10¹⁴ GeV/c². This paper searches the window from 10⁶ to 10¹⁵ GeV/c² — a range that starts around a million protons' worth of mass and runs straight through "bacterium" up to "human cell," with its best sensitivity landing almost exactly at the upper end. The Rice press framing — "potentially reaching the mass of a living cell" — isn't loose color; it's a genuinely accurate order-of-magnitude description of where this search's ceiling sits, once you note that "living cell" spans about three decades of real mass depending on which cell you mean.

**Analogy:** think of the WIMP search program as looking for a single dust mote drifting through a room — individually undetectable by touch, but so numerous you can filter the air and catch huge numbers of them per second. UHDM is the opposite: instead of dust, imagine a single grain of sand occasionally drifting through the same room. Far rarer (mass conservation — the same total dark matter density spread over far fewer, heavier particles), but each passage, if you could feel it at all, would deliver a real, distinct nudge rather than a statistical dusting.

**Breaks when:** you push this to ask "why not just wait longer and count nudges the way you'd count dust hits?" — you can, and that is exactly this paper's strategy (nine days, count transients), but the analogy breaks on rate: UHDM's expected event rate in any real detector is so low that "waiting longer" buys you almost nothing within a human research timescale unless the detector is either enormous or, as here, cleverly engineered to be sensitive to an individual event rather than a statistical excess.

**Concept 2 — Coupling and mediators: why "detecting" dark matter needs a middleman.**
Dark matter is defined by what it *doesn't* do: it doesn't interact via the electromagnetic or strong force (that's why it's dark and why it clumps into stable structures rather than radiating energy away like ordinary gas). But "doesn't interact via known forces" doesn't mean "interacts via no force at all" — most direct-detection experiments, this one included, posit some new, so-far-unseen force, carried by a hypothetical mediator particle (a new kind of boson), that lets dark matter nudge ordinary matter at some very small but nonzero rate. The strength of that nudge is the "coupling," usually written as α (analogous to the fine-structure constant α≈1/137 that sets the strength of ordinary electromagnetism). A coupling of zero means truly undetectable by this method; a coupling above the sensitivity floor produces a detectable kick.

**Analogy:** picture two computers on a network that share no common protocol — they can't exchange packets at all. A "mediator" is like discovering they actually share a little-used legacy protocol nobody accounted for; the "coupling strength" is the effective bandwidth of that channel. Zero bandwidth is genuine isolation (no mediator exists); a channel too slow to matter in practice looks identical to isolation from the outside — until you build a receiver sensitive enough to catch even a single packet.

**Breaks when:** you try to use it to explain *why* a mediator should exist at all — the network analogy takes the mediator's existence for granted the same way the paper does. Nothing here derives a mediator from first principles; it's a working hypothesis motivated by other theoretical constructions (dark photons, scalar mediators), not a prediction unique to this experiment.

**Concept 3 — Magnetic levitation and the SQUID readout.**
The detector itself is disarmingly simple in concept: take a tiny, strongly magnetized object (here, 0.356 milligrams — a fleck you could just barely see, built from three neodymium-iron-boron cube magnets plus a small glass sphere for balance) and float it, frictionlessly, inside a superconducting trap cooled to cryogenic temperatures. A Type-I superconductor expels magnetic field lines (the Meissner effect), so the trap doesn't touch the magnet at all — it repels it into a stable floating position purely through induced supercurrents. With nothing physically touching the magnet, there's essentially no mechanical friction to damp its motion, which is why its "quality factor" (a measure of how many oscillations it sustains before losing energy) exceeds 2.25 million — extraordinarily high, and the single biggest reason this technique can be sensitive to something as faint as a TeV/c momentum kick. A superconducting pickup coil linked to a DC SQUID (a superconducting quantum interference device — the most sensitive magnetic-flux sensor that exists) then reads out the magnet's tiny motions as a change in magnetic flux.

**Analogy:** this is the physical equivalent of an air-bearing turntable versus one running on greased metal bearings. A greased bearing has friction that constantly bleeds energy away, burying any small external nudge in mechanical noise. An air bearing (or here, a magnetic-levitation "bearing" with no contact at all) removes that noise floor almost entirely, so a nudge that would be completely invisible on the greased version becomes a clean, measurable event.

**Breaks when:** you push it to imply the system is noise-free — it isn't. Removing mechanical friction just changes *which* noise sources dominate (here: environmental vibration and seismic coupling, addressed with 110–130 dB of vibration isolation and a dedicated concrete foundation) rather than eliminating noise altogether. The paper's own unresolved 8 candidate transients are a direct reminder that "no mechanical friction" does not mean "no unexplained signal."

**Concept 4 — Coherent enhancement and force range: why the neutron count matters so much.**
The levitated magnet contains roughly 1.07×10²⁰ neutrons. If a hypothetical UHDM particle interacts separately and independently with each neutron, you might expect the total signal to grow only as the square root of that number (the way independent random noise sources partially cancel). But if the mediator's force range is long compared to the size of the region the neutron sits in, all those neutrons respond *in phase*, coherently, to the same passing dark matter particle — and the total force scales linearly with neutron count instead. That's the difference between a whisper and a shout with the same number of people, depending on whether they speak randomly or in unison.

**Analogy:** in GPU computing, when many threads in a warp each request a nearby memory address, a coalescing memory controller merges those requests into a single wide transaction — total bandwidth utilized scales with the number of threads, not with the square root of it, exactly because the requests are aligned (coherent) rather than scattered. A long-range mediator does the same thing to the neutrons in the levitated magnet: coherent (aligned) coupling turns "one particle's worth of force" into "10²⁰ particles' worth of force, added directly," which is the entire reason a milligram-scale sensor can out-perform far larger, less coherent detectors in this specific regime.

**Breaks when:** you push it to assume coherence always holds — it doesn't. It only holds while the mediator's force range (call it λ) is larger than the effective radius of the levitated particle (≈260 micrometers here). Once λ shrinks below that radius, only a thin surface shell of neutrons couples effectively, and the coherent enhancement is lost — this transition is worked out explicitly below, and it's exactly what shapes the paper's own exclusion curve.

**Worked numerical micro-example — when does the coherence bonus actually apply?**

The paper models the new force as a Yukawa-type potential between the dark matter particle and each neutron:

$$U(r) = \frac{\hbar c\,\alpha}{r}\, e^{-r/\lambda}$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$U(r)$: the potential energy of the interaction at separation $r$ (units of energy).
$\hbar c$: reduced Planck's constant times the speed of light, a standard unit-conversion constant equal to about $197.3\ \text{MeV}\cdot\text{fm}$, or equivalently $1.973\times10^{-7}\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{m}$.
$\alpha$: the dimensionless coupling strength of the new force (this paper's central measured/constrained quantity).
$r$: the distance between the dark matter particle and a given neutron.
$\lambda = \hbar/(m_\phi c)$: the force's range, set by the mediator boson's mass $m_\phi$ — a heavier mediator makes for a shorter-range force, exactly as the W and Z bosons' large mass makes the weak force short-ranged compared to the (massless-mediator) infinite-range electromagnetic force.

**What this actually means:** this is the same functional form as the Yukawa nuclear force or a screened Coulomb potential — an inverse-distance force cut off by an exponential decay once you get past distance $\lambda$.

Now the concrete question: for which mediator masses does $\lambda$ exceed the levitated particle's effective radius, $R_\mathrm{eff}\approx260\ \mu\text{m}$, so that the coherence bonus described above actually applies? Using $\lambda = \hbar c / (m_\phi c^2)$:

For $m_\phi c^2 = 1\ \text{meV}$: $\lambda = (1.973\times10^{-7}\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{m}) / (10^{-3}\ \text{eV}) \approx 197\ \mu\text{m}$ — right at the same order as $R_\mathrm{eff}$, the crossover point.

For $m_\phi c^2 = 0.1\ \text{meV}$: $\lambda \approx 1.97\ \text{mm}$ — nearly ten times larger than $R_\mathrm{eff}$, so essentially the whole $10^{20}$-neutron target couples coherently. This is exactly the regime the paper calls "$m_\phi \ll 1\ \text{meV}/c^2$," where it reports its strongest limit, $\alpha_n$ as low as $3.2\times10^{-9}$.

For $m_\phi c^2 = 10\ \text{meV}$: $\lambda \approx 19.7\ \mu\text{m}$ — more than ten times *smaller* than $R_\mathrm{eff}$, so only a thin outer shell of neutrons feels the force at any given moment; the coherent bonus collapses, and the paper's own Fig. 3 shows the exclusion limit weakening sharply at exactly this mediator mass.

This hand calculation reproduces, from the paper's own headline formula and one physical constant, precisely the three mediator-mass benchmarks (0.1, 1, and 10 meV/c²) the paper itself chose to plot — which is a good sign the underlying formalism is doing real, load-bearing work rather than being decorative (a point revisited formally in Section 5's Predictive Content Check).

**Central analogy for this paper:** a coherently-coupled, warp-scale force antenna.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

**What POLONAISE actually built and ran.** The detector is a 0.356 mg ferromagnet — three neodymium-iron-boron (Nd₂Fe₁₄B) cube magnets plus a small glass sphere for mechanical balance — levitated inside a Type-I superconducting tantalum trap, cooled to cryogenic temperature so the trap is fully superconducting and repels the magnet's field via the Meissner effect. A superconducting pickup coil, inductively coupled to the magnet's motion, feeds a DC SQUID that converts tiny displacements into a readable flux signal. The whole assembly sits on a dedicated concrete foundation with multiple stages of vibration isolation delivering 110–130 dB of attenuation against environmental seismic and acoustic noise — because with essentially no mechanical damping (quality factor above 2.25 million), the dominant remaining noise source is the ground shaking, not the sensor itself.

**Why the search strategy is "hunt for one kick," not "average over many."** For a dark matter particle in the 10⁶–10¹⁵ GeV/c² range, the expected number of particles passing through even a laboratory-scale volume per unit time is minuscule — this is the direct consequence of holding the local dark matter mass density fixed while cranking up the mass of each individual particle: fewer, heavier particles. So instead of looking for a periodic signal or a statistical excess in an event-counting histogram (as WIMP xenon experiments do), the team looks for a single abrupt, lasting change — an "impulse" — in the magnet's motion, consistent with one scattering event delivering a one-time momentum kick $q$. They collected data only at night (1900–0700 local time) across roughly six weeks (December 2025–January 2026) specifically to avoid daytime human activity in the building, and after quality cuts kept 219.66 hours (about 9.15 days) of usable "quiet" data.

**How a candidate kick is identified.** They demodulate the SQUID's raw output into a complex amplitude signal and look for a persistent *step* in that amplitude — because the oscillator's very high quality factor means a real momentum kick doesn't decay away quickly; it should show up as a sustained shift lasting thousands of seconds, not a brief blip. They build a profile-likelihood test statistic $\mathcal{T}$ that compares adjacent segments of data for evidence of a shared step, treating the fraction of the kick's energy in each segment and each segment's noise power as nuisance parameters to be fit rather than assumed. A candidate is kept only if $\mathcal{T} > 100$ — a threshold chosen because the pure-noise (null) distribution of $\mathcal{T}$, confirmed both analytically and by simulating noise-only data, predicts a false-alarm probability of just $4.4\times10^{-15}$ across the *entire* search — i.e., essentially zero accidental candidates from noise alone at that threshold.

**What they found.** Eight candidates survived, with reconstructed momentum kicks ranging from 1.52 to 12.8 TeV/c (median 3.1 TeV/c). Detection efficiency reaches 50% at a momentum of 1.21 TeV/c, but because systematic uncertainties in that efficiency estimate grow large near the low-momentum edge, the team conservatively drew their analysis threshold at 1 TeV/c, where efficiency is only about 17% — a deliberately cautious choice that costs sensitivity but avoids overclaiming precision they don't have.

**How eight candidates become an exclusion limit rather than a discovery claim.** The paper does not attempt to argue any of the eight candidates is dark matter. Instead — and this is a methodologically conservative move worth flagging explicitly — they treat *all eight* as if they could be genuine signal (rather than trying to model and subtract them as background) and compute upper limits on the coupling $\alpha_n$ using the "optimum interval" method, which derives a bound from the largest gap in the observed spectrum that's anomalously empty of events. This method is known to *over-cover* — meaning the resulting exclusion is conservative (stronger caveats, weaker/safer claims) rather than optimistically tight. Folding in the 219.66-hour livetime and the momentum-dependent detection efficiency, they translate the null (or at least inconclusive) result into upper limits on $\alpha_n$ as a function of dark matter mass $m_\mathrm{DM}$ and mediator mass $m_\phi$.

**Headline number.** For mediator masses much lighter than 1 meV/c² (the long-range, fully-coherent regime worked out numerically in Section 3), the 95%-confidence-level exclusion reaches $\alpha_n = 3.2\times10^{-9}$, with leading sensitivity — meaning the tightest limit of any existing experiment — spanning dark matter masses from roughly $4\times10^5$ to $8\times10^{14}\ \text{GeV}/c^2$. For comparison, prior optical-levitation searches for the same physics topped out around $10^8\ \text{GeV}/c^2$, limited by their much smaller (nanogram-scale) target mass; this magnetic-levitation platform extends the accessible mass ceiling by roughly seven orders of magnitude, purely as a consequence of using a target about a hundred thousand times heavier while retaining comparable or better force sensitivity (0.07 fN/√Hz).

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** a reader could reasonably assume "detecting dark matter using a levitated magnet" means the paper reports a detection. It doesn't. This is an exclusion-limit (null-result) paper. The eight candidate events are explicitly *not* claimed as dark matter — the paper's own language stays neutral, and the analysis method (treat-all-as-possible-signal, then bound conservatively) is specifically designed to produce a valid limit whether or not any of the eight are real physics.

> **Watch:** a reader with a WIMP-search mental model might assume the mass range $10^6$–$10^{15}\ \text{GeV}/c^2$ still describes something roughly particle-sized. It describes something spanning "a million protons" up to "a human cell" — twenty-one orders of magnitude above a proton and, at the high end, a genuinely macroscopic (if literally microscopic-in-everyday-terms) object by particle-physics standards.

> **Watch:** the reported exclusion is not model-independent. It assumes one specific interaction channel — a light-boson-mediated, neutron-coupling Yukawa force, coherently summed across the target. A UHDM candidate that interacts with ordinary matter *only* gravitationally (e.g., certain primordial-black-hole or Planck-relic scenarios genuinely discussed elsewhere in the UHDM literature) would produce no signal in this detector at any mass, and this paper says nothing about that possibility one way or the other.

> **Watch:** the momentum measurement ($q$, in TeV/c) is the actual observable — it is not a direct dark matter mass measurement. Converting a distribution of momentum kicks into a constraint on $m_\mathrm{DM}$ requires assuming a galactic dark matter velocity distribution (the Standard Halo Model, implicit in the $\langle dR/dq\rangle$ rate formula) and a local dark matter density; a different, plausible velocity/density assumption would shift the mass-axis calibration of Fig. 3 somewhat, though not the underlying momentum data itself.

## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

Two things stand out as the paper's real contribution, and it's worth being precise about which is which.

**Genuinely new to the field:** extending a *magnetic* (as opposed to optical) levitated-sensor platform to the ultraheavy dark matter search, and specifically exploiting its milligram-scale mass. The physics of coherent Yukawa scattering off a macroscopic target isn't new — it's the same logic behind fifth-force torsion-balance experiments and prior optical-levitation UHDM searches. What's new is the engineering choice: swap a nanogram optically-trapped bead for a milligram magnetically-trapped one, retain comparable or better force sensitivity by eliminating mechanical contact almost entirely (quality factor > 2.25 million), and the target-mass increase alone buys roughly seven orders of magnitude of extra sensitivity at the high-mass end, because a heavier target both scatters more UHDM particles per unit time (larger cross-sectional exposure) and, when coherence holds, produces a proportionally larger coherent kick per scattering event.

**Genuinely new to the reader (and arguably to this specific mass regime), but not conceptually novel to physics broadly:** the "hunt for one impulse in nine quiet days" analysis strategy itself — profile-likelihood step detection with a rigorously characterized false-alarm rate, followed by conservative optimum-interval limit-setting that doesn't require modeling backgrounds it can't yet explain. This is methodologically closely related to how gravitational-wave transient searches and some dark-matter-substructure microlensing searches handle rare-event detection; POLONAISE is porting a mature statistical toolkit into a new experimental context rather than inventing a new one.

## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

The POLONAISE collaboration (Leiden University, Rice University, IFAE Barcelona, and Nanyang Technological University Singapore) built on their own 2025 demonstration of a magnetically-levitated ferromagnetic particle — originally used to search for ultralight, wave-like dark matter — and retargeted essentially the same apparatus at the opposite end of the dark matter mass spectrum: particles so heavy (10⁶ to 10¹⁵ GeV/c², spanning roughly a million-proton mass up to the mass of a human cell) that even the enormous local dark matter mass density corresponds to a vanishingly low number density of individual particles. Where a wave-like ultralight search looks for a small, continuous oscillation, an ultraheavy search has to look for a single, rare, discrete kick — the momentum transferred when one heavy dark matter particle scatters once off the levitated target.

The instrument itself is a 0.356-milligram ferromagnet floating, with essentially zero mechanical friction, inside a superconducting trap, its motion read out by one of the most sensitive magnetic-flux sensors that exists (a DC SQUID). Because the target contains roughly 10²⁰ neutrons and the hypothesized new force can, under the right conditions, couple to all of them coherently rather than independently, the whole assembly behaves like a single enormous "antenna" for this kind of interaction — turning a fundamentally tiny per-particle coupling into a macroscopically measurable force, provided the force's range is larger than the roughly 260-micrometer effective size of the target.

Running for about nine days of cumulative quiet (nighttime-only) data between December 2025 and January 2026, the team applied a statistically rigorous step-detection method to find abrupt, lasting jumps in the magnet's motion — the signature of a single scattering event — and found eight candidates, with momentum kicks between about 1.5 and 13 TeV/c. Rather than trying to explain these away individually, they took the conservative route: treat all eight as possible signal, and use a method (the "optimum interval" technique) that derives a valid upper bound on the interaction strength regardless of whether the candidates are real or not. The result is the tightest exclusion limit yet set on this particular flavor of ultraheavy dark matter across a huge stretch of previously under-explored mass — roughly seven orders of magnitude beyond what the best prior levitated-sensor (optical) experiments could reach, purely because a milligram-scale magnetic target is about a hundred thousand times heavier than the nanogram-scale beads optical traps can hold.

The interpretive core of this write-up is the Section 3 worked example: taking the paper's own Yukawa-potential formula and plugging in one physical constant reproduces, independently, the exact three mediator masses the paper itself singles out in its results figure — and explains, in plain physical terms, *why* the exclusion limit weakens so sharply once the mediator gets heavier than about 1 meV/c². That calculation is the single best window into why this specific technique works as well as it does: it isn't raw sensitivity alone, it's raw sensitivity multiplied by a coherence bonus that only survives while the new force's range comfortably exceeds the physical size of the sensor.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. **Most dark matter searches assume the particle is atom-sized; this one is built for particles as heavy as a bacterium or a human cell** — a mass range so far outside the usual WIMP window that the entire detection strategy has to change, from "count many faint hits" to "wait patiently for one unmistakable kick."

2. **A milligram floating magnet beats a nanogram floating glass bead by seven orders of magnitude of sensitivity at the high-mass end** — not because the physics is different, but because a heavier target both gets hit more often and, when the new force's range is long enough, adds up the response of all 10²⁰ of its neutrons coherently rather than randomly.

3. **This is a "we didn't find it, but we know more precisely where it isn't" result** — eight unexplained blips were treated conservatively as possible signal rather than swept under the rug, and the resulting exclusion limit is real scientific progress even though (in fact, partly *because*) nothing was confidently detected.

## 9. Shorter Summary

Most dark matter hunts look for particles roughly atom-sized, using tanks of liquid xenon to catch huge numbers of faint collisions. But some theories predict dark matter particles far heavier — from a million times a proton's mass up to roughly the mass of a bacterium or even a human cell. That "ultraheavy" regime needs an entirely different kind of detector: instead of catching many faint hits, you need to catch one rare, unmistakable kick.

This Rice-led collaboration, working with Leiden University in the Netherlands, built exactly that: a tiny magnet (0.356 milligrams, smaller than a grain of rice) floating frictionlessly inside a superconducting trap, its motion tracked by one of the most sensitive magnetic sensors ever built. Because the magnet contains about a hundred billion billion neutrons, and a hypothetical new dark-matter-carrying force can make all of them respond together rather than independently, the whole setup behaves like a single giant antenna for this kind of collision — despite being physically tiny.

Watching only at night, to avoid vibrations from people working in the building, the team collected about nine days of usable data. They found eight unexplained blips in the magnet's motion — momentary kicks strong enough that, if real, they'd correspond to genuine dark matter collisions. Rather than assuming these were noise and discarding them, the team took the more careful path: treat all eight as though they might be real signal, and calculate the strongest exclusion limit that's still valid either way. The result is the tightest constraint yet on this specific kind of ultraheavy dark matter across a huge stretch of previously unexplored particle masses — about ten million times better reach, at the highest masses, than the best prior magnetically-untethered dark matter search.

This isn't a discovery. It's a null result, honestly reported, that meaningfully narrows down where an entire class of dark matter theories could still be hiding — and it demonstrates that a technique barely a few years old (milligram-scale magnetic levitation) can already outperform its more mature optical-levitation cousin by seven orders of magnitude in this particular mass range, simply by using a heavier, more tightly coupled target. Whether those eight blips are background noise or something more interesting is the open question the next run will need to answer.

## Appendix — Deep-Dive Discussion Log (2026-09-09)

Two follow-up exchanges, both anchored on Takeaway #3 ("we didn't find it, but we know more precisely where it isn't") and the paper's conservative treatment of its eight unexplained candidate events.

### Q1 — Why treat all eight candidates as possible signal instead of trying to explain them away?

The conservative move has a specific shape: rather than building a background model and subtracting it, the team folds all eight events into the limit calculation as if they might be real, using the optimum-interval method — a technique explicitly known to *over-cover*, meaning it hands back a weaker limit than an optimally-tight one would. They're trading away some of their own reach in exchange for not having to defend a background model they can't yet justify.

That trade has a documented cautionary precedent from directly adjacent territory: the ~130 GeV Galactic Center gamma-ray line. That was a real, statistically significant excess in real Fermi-LAT data — for a while the best dark matter hint anyone had — that eventually traced to an instrumental artifact in Fermi-LAT's own effective-area calibration, with DAMPE's independent non-detection helping kill it. The lesson wasn't "model backgrounds more carefully" — it was "an unexplained excess you can't independently confirm is background is itself an unjustified inference just because you assumed it away." POLONAISE is visibly applying that lesson: unable to distinguish the eight transients from instrumental artifacts, they let that uncertainty cost them sensitivity honestly rather than quietly assuming it away for a tighter-looking number.

**Analogy (scoped to this point, not the paper's central antenna analogy):** this is the same posture as a Byzantine-fault-tolerant system that can't distinguish a transient network blip from a genuine node failure. The naive approach filters the blip out as noise and reports a clean uptime number. The fault-tolerant approach budgets for the case where it was real, costing throughput but making the reported number one that survives audit. The 130 GeV line was the physics equivalent of a system that filtered out a real fault as noise and reported an uptime figure it couldn't back up.

The epistemically important part: this makes the null result *more* trustworthy, not less, precisely because it isn't as tight as it could have been. A tighter, unjustified-subtraction-based limit would be less reliable knowledge even with a better-looking number on paper. What this paper actually delivers is a smaller, honestly-bounded region of parameter space where this specific coupling provably isn't — ground that stays solid regardless of what the eight events turn out to be.

### Q2 — How does the optimum interval method actually work?

**The problem it solves:** a hypothesized coupling $\alpha_n$ predicts both a shape for the expected rate vs. momentum, $dR/dq$, and a total expected event count $\mu$. The eight candidates might be all signal, all background, or a mix — with no trustworthy background model to say which. Yellin's optimum interval method (the standard dark-matter-search technique from 2002 that this paper uses) sets a valid upper limit on $\mu$ without ever assuming an answer to that question.

**The core building block.** Re-express each candidate's momentum as a position $u$ on a 0-to-1 axis via the hypothesis's own predicted cumulative distribution — "what fraction of expected signal events should fall below this point." If $\mu$ is the true expected count, candidates behave like a Poisson scatter of points on that axis, and the cleanest fact about such a scatter is:

$$P(\text{a specific interval of length } x \text{ contains zero points}) = e^{-\mu x}$$

**Symbol definitions:** $\mu$ — total events the hypothesis predicts over the run; $x$ — length of a sub-interval of the 0-to-1 transformed axis, as a fraction of the whole range.

**What this means:** the zero-count case of a Poisson process — event count in a stretch of length $x$ is Poisson($\mu x$), so $P(\text{zero}) = e^{-\mu x}$. A bigger gap, or a bigger hypothesized $\mu$, both make "nobody landed here" less believable — so a large empty gap is evidence against high $\mu$, without needing to know which candidates are real.

**Why the real formula needs more than this.** You don't pre-register one interval; you scan all candidates for the single largest gap anywhere — a maximum-of-many-samples statistic, which needs a look-elsewhere correction. Yellin's actual $C_0(x,\mu)$ formula is the closed-form version of exactly that correction — computed exactly rather than estimated by simulation, which is what makes the method fast and reusable.

**Optimum interval vs. maximum gap.** Plain "maximum gap" only considers empty (zero-candidate) stretches. Optimum interval also considers stretches with 1, 2, 3... candidates, picking whichever interval — of any count — gives the strongest evidence against a given $\mu$. That catches suspiciously *clustered* candidates too, not just suspiciously empty regions.

**Toy worked example** (illustrative positions, not the paper's real momenta — the real $dR/dq$ shape wasn't in the retrieved material). Suppose the eight candidates transform to $u = 0.10, 0.35, 0.40, 0.63, 0.65, 0.80, 0.95, 0.98$. Consecutive gaps: $0.10, 0.25, 0.05, 0.23, 0.02, 0.15, 0.15, 0.03, 0.02$ — largest is $x=0.25$. Using the simple building-block formula alone (not yet multiplicity-corrected): at $\mu=8$, $P(\text{empty}) = e^{-8(0.25)} = e^{-2} \approx 0.135$ — not yet damning. At $\mu=20$: $e^{-5}\approx0.0067$ — genuinely improbable. The real optimum-interval number is inflated above this naive estimate to account for scanning all nine gaps (and all 1-, 2-, 3-candidate sub-intervals) for the worst one, so the actual excluded $\mu$ sits higher than this toy suggests — but the qualitative story holds: crank $\alpha_n$ up, $\mu$ rises, empty gaps get less plausible, and at some crossover the data rules the hypothesis out at 95% confidence. That crossover, swept over $\alpha_n$, is the quoted limit, $3.2\times10^{-9}$.

**Where "over-covers" comes from.** The method deliberately throws away information: no attempt to estimate which fraction of the eight are background, no use of any energy-dependent background shape — just "here are eight positions on an axis." A profile likelihood with a correctly-specified background model would extract more constraining power from the same eight numbers by exploiting spectral-shape differences the optimum interval method ignores on purpose. That forgone power is exactly what over-coverage means: the method's nominal 95% interval actually contains the true value more than 95% of the time, because it's playing it safe rather than squeezing out every bit of leverage the data would technically support.
