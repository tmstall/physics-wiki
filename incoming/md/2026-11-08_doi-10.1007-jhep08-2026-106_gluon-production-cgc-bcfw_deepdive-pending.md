## Appendix — Deep-Dive Discussion Log (2026-11-08)

### Q1. Section 4.2: off-shell momenta "don't factor"; what is the transverse-coordinate trick and why does it make the shift rules "one transverse coordinate and one spinor"?

**Setup.** A source moving along light-like direction p produces a field gluon with momentum k obeying p·k = 0 (the eikonal condition; a nucleus moving along + gives k⁻ = 0). Since p is light-like it factors, p = |p⟩[p|. With any light-like reference q, define

κ = ⟨q|k|p] / ⟨qp⟩,  κ* = ⟨p|k|q] / [pq].

**Why q drops out.** k acting on |p] gives a 2-spinor whose bracket with ⟨p| is ⟨p|k|p] = 2p·k = 0. In two dimensions that forces it parallel to |p⟩, so k|p] = κ|p⟩ for some number κ; then ⟨q|k|p]/⟨qp⟩ = κ for any q. Same argument on the other side for κ*. Only the longitudinal coefficient x = q·k / q·p depends on q. Writing k = x p + k_T gives k² = −κκ*.

**Physical reading.** In the frame where p is the + direction, κ ~ k_x + i k_y and κ* ~ k_x − i k_y: the two circular (helicity ±1) components of the transverse kick, an I/Q phasor pair. Off-shell propagator 1/k² = −1/|κ|², the CGC's familiar 1/k_⊥².
*Analogy limit:* for real momenta κ and κ* are conjugates (positive/negative-frequency halves of a real signal); under a complex BCFW shift they become independent (analytic signal off the real axis), which is the lever the recursion uses.

**Shift rules.**
- Off-shell leg partnered with on-shell leg j: k → k + z|p⟩[j|. Preserves p·k = 0 (⟨pp⟩ = 0). κ → κ + z[jp], κ* fixed; partner spinor |j⟩ absorbs the compensating shift. Propagator −1/(κκ*) has a pole at κ(z) = 0, i.e. k² = 0: the "source leg goes on-shell" pole, whose residue bootstraps off-shell amplitudes from on-shell ones (§5).
- Shift along the common direction p (two same-direction legs): neither κ nor κ* moves, only x. All same-direction off-shell propagators 1/|κ|² and eikonal propagators 1/(p·ℓ) (since p·p = 0) are frozen → only internal-gluon poles (§6).
- Production: proton eikonal propagators 1/(p̄·ℓ) with p̄·p ≠ 0 do move under the nucleus-direction shift → the single extra eikonal pole (§7).

The parameterization sorts every propagator by whether a given shift can touch it; that sorting generates the three pole types.

### Q2. "Explain what's going on and why" (conceptual level)

**Problem.** BCFW deforms momenta by complex z and reads the answer from poles; the deformation is legal only if each leg keeps its defining property. Free gluon: k² = 0, preserved by shifting one spinor (rank-one stays rank-one). Source gluon: k² is free; the defining property is p·k = 0 (belongs to a source along p), which generic coordinates hide.

**Fix.** Coordinates in which the constraint is built in: a piece along p plus the two transverse numbers κ, κ* (the physical sideways kick, in its two helicity components).

**Why q-independence matters.** If κ depended on the arbitrary helper q, a gauge-like convention would be smuggled back in; showing independence proves κ, κ* are physical — the paper's whole discipline of carrying only convention-free quantities.

**Payoff.** Every propagator's dependence is visible: off-shell ~ 1/(κκ*); eikonal depends only on source direction; internal on momentum sums. Choosing a shift = choosing which propagators may move = where poles can appear.
*Analogy:* instruction encoding with each architectural field in its own bit range, so a write touches one field and you know statically which dependent entries it invalidates (the shift = the write, invalidated entries = propagators that can pole).
*Breaks when:* in hardware the dependency map is complete by construction; here completeness (large-z falloff) must still be proven per shift — the Appendix C issue, and why mixed-direction production shifts are argued rather than proven.

### Q3. Worked numeric version (2×2-matrix picture)

Momentum as a matrix: k = [[k⁰+k^z, k^x − i k^y], [k^x + i k^y, k⁰ − k^z]], det k = k². Free gluon: det = 0 → rank one → factors into two spinors.

Source along z, p = (1,0,0,1): p·k = 0 ⇒ k⁰ = k^z ≡ x. Take k^x = 3, k^y = 4:
k = [[2x, 3−4i], [3+4i, 0]], det = −25 → does not factor.

Structure: bottom-right entry = 0 *is* the source condition; top-left 2x is the "along p" piece (rank one alone); off-diagonals 3 ± 4i are κ, κ* (product 25, virtuality −25; x doesn't enter k²). §4.2 is this picture written frame-independently; q only picks the frame.

Shift: add z × (matrix with |p⟩ = (1,0)ᵀ on the left) → only the top row changes:
k(z) = [[2x + za, (3−4i) + zb], [3+4i, 0]].
- Bottom-right stays 0 → constraint preserved automatically.
- One transverse entry moves, the other is fixed.
- det = −(3+4i)[(3−4i) + zb] vanishes at one z → leg momentarily on-shell → pole (source-leg-on-shell pole; residue is an on-shell amplitude).
(In this matrix convention the moving entry is top-right; the paper's κ/κ* labeling may be reversed.)

"One spinor": momentum conservation makes a partner on-shell leg j = |j⟩[j| absorb −z|p⟩[j|, giving (|j⟩ − z|p⟩)[j| — still rank one, still massless; only its left spinor changed.

Special shift: add p itself → only top-left moves, det untouched, no off-shell pole → §6's internal-gluon-only recursion.

### Q4. Stripped-down version (reader found the spinor machinery beyond current background)

Assessment: the gap is a specific specialist prerequisite (spinor-helicity, graduate-level amplitude theory), not a general ceiling.

Core idea with machinery removed — signal-processing bridge: knowing a transfer function's pole locations and strengths lets you rebuild it by partial fractions. BCFW does that for amplitudes: nudge inputs with a complex knob, find where the amplitude blows up, rebuild from the blowups. §4.2 just picks variables so that (a) the nucleus gluons' rule ("stay attached to the source direction") can't be broken while turning the knob, and (b) you can see at a glance which knob settings cause blowups — like choosing I/Q coordinates so a phase rotation touches one coordinate. The matrices/spinors are implementation; the paper's conclusions don't require following them.

### Q5. The BCFW engine paragraph (§3 "Concept" box), sentence by sentence

1. *Rational function, simple poles where an internal particle goes on-shell.* Each internal line contributes 1/P²; it blows up exactly when P² = 0, i.e. the internal particle could be real and free. Tree level = no loop integrals, so only ratios of polynomials with those blowups.
2. *Deform two external momenta by complex z.* Add z·(vector) to one leg, subtract from another: momentum conservation automatic; vector chosen so legs stay massless. Each P(z)² is linear in z → one pole per propagator. Amplitude becomes A(z); physical answer is A(0).
3. *Falloff ⇒ A(0) = −Σ residues.* Partial fractions, as for transfer functions. Toy check: A(z) = 1/((z−1)(z−2)), A(0) = 1/2. A(z)/z has residue A(0) at 0, −1 at z = 1, +1/2 at z = 2; residues sum to zero when A falls off, so A(0) = −(−1 + 1/2) = 1/2 ✓. You rebuild A(0) from pole data without evaluating there.
   *Why falloff matters:* like needing a strictly proper transfer function before partial fractions — if numerator degree is too high there's a polynomial piece no pole sees; in BCFW that's a missed "boundary term." Proving falloff for the CGC shifts is the Appendix C issue.
4. *Residue factorizes into two smaller amplitudes.* At the pole the internal particle is real; the process splits into "make it" × "it does the rest." Pipeline-register analogy: once the stage boundary holds a well-defined value, each half computes independently, each with fewer legs.
5. *No gauge choice.* Individual Feynman diagrams are gauge-dependent bookkeeping (microarchitectural intermediate state whose artifacts cancel only in the sum); BCFW's building blocks are whole physical amplitudes (architecturally visible results), down to the three-point base cases.
   *Analogy limit:* a pipeline split always works; a BCFW split only where the internal line can go on-shell, and only if falloff holds.

Tie-back: CGC adds off-shell source legs, so step 2's "keep legs legal while deforming" needs new rules — §4.2's transverse coordinates are those rules (a technical patch on steps 2 and 4; steps 1, 3, 5 are the real logic).

### Q6. Simplest "what and why" of BCFW

- **Goal:** compute the amplitude — how likely colliding particles produce a given set of outgoing particles.
- **Old way's problem:** Feynman diagrams add thousands of pieces, most gauge-dependent junk that cancels only at the end.
- **BCFW:** (1) add a dial z that tweaks two particles' momenta; the real answer is at z = 0. (2) The answer blows up at a few dial settings. (3) At each blowup the collision splits into two smaller collisions joined by one real particle. (4) The residue theorem says the answer at zero is the sum of those blowups: big answer = Σ (small × small). (5) Repeat down to the known simplest case.
- **Why better:** every piece is a real physical answer; nothing to cancel.
- **Condition:** the answer must die off when the dial is turned far, or part of the answer is missed.
- **This paper:** makes BCFW work when one colliding object is a heavy nucleus, whose gluons don't obey the usual rules; §4.2 lets them be "tweaked by the dial" safely.
- Analogy: reconstruct a filter from its poles; BCFW reconstructs a collision from where it blows up.

### Q7. The two new CGC pole types, simply

Ordinary BCFW has one blowup type: an internal gluon becomes real and the collision splits in two. The CGC has extra ingredients, so two more blowup types:

**New pole #1 — the nucleus's gluon becomes a real gluon.** Normally the source gluon is a field "push," not a real particle. At one dial setting it becomes an ordinary real gluon of definite spin direction. Result: nucleus amplitude = ordinary amplitude × simple factor. Why it matters: ordinary amplitudes are tabulated, so nucleus amplitudes come almost free (the §5 bootstrap).

**New pole #2 — the fast-moving source line (eikonal propagator).** A quark in the proton moves near light speed; each gluon it picks up rotates its color charge slightly (compass needle nudged by passing magnets). Each stretch between nudges is an eikonal propagator, and the dial can make one blow up. Result: the source line splits at that spot — "before the kink" and "after the kink" become two smaller amplitudes, times an unchanged simple factor. Why it matters: still big = small × small, so the recursion works with source lines present.

**Why "choose shifts to avoid or exploit":** which two legs carry the dial decides which blowups appear. Avoid them → fewer terms; hit pole #1 deliberately → convert nucleus amplitudes into known ordinary ones. Like choosing which variable to expand in: same answer, much less work.
