## 1. Punchy Title & One-Sentence Hook

**Mix the Neutrino Flavors, Lose the Supernovae: A 195-Star Simulation Census**

In 195 one-dimensional collapse simulations, letting neutrinos instantly scramble their flavors behind the stalled shock raises the share of massive stars that fail to explode and quietly become black holes. Weighted by how common each star mass is, the share rises from 27% to between 48% and 88%, and most of the newly failed explosions sit in the 16–30 solar-mass band where astronomers have long noticed "missing" supernova progenitors.

## 2. Big-Picture Context

A star born with more than about eight times the Sun's mass ends with an iron core that can no longer support itself. The core collapses in under a second, rebounds at nuclear density, and launches a shock wave outward. That shock stalls within tens of milliseconds, a couple of hundred kilometres out, as it loses energy breaking up infalling iron and is smothered by the matter still raining in. From there the star has two possible endings. Either the enormous flood of neutrinos pouring out of the newborn proto-neutron star re-energizes the shock and the star explodes as a core-collapse supernova, leaving a neutron star behind. Or the shock never recovers, matter keeps piling onto the proto-neutron star, and it collapses into a black hole with little or no visible explosion. Which stars take which branch sets the birth rates and mass spectra of neutron stars and black holes, the chemical yields of galaxies, and the feedback that massive stars inject into their surroundings.

The old textbook picture was a single mass cutoff: stars below roughly 40 solar masses explode, heavier ones make black holes. Over the past fifteen years, large grids of spherically symmetric (1D) simulations dismantled that picture. They showed that explodability is "non-monotonic": it zig-zags with initial mass because the core structure a star ends up with depends sensitively on its shell-burning history. Some stars as light as 13 solar masses fail, while some heavier ones explode. On the observing side, three puzzles push in the same direction. The **red supergiant problem**: pre-explosion images of ordinary hydrogen-rich (Type IIP) supernovae turn up no red-supergiant progenitors above roughly 16–20 solar masses, even though such stars clearly exist. The **supernova rate problem**: the measured cosmic core-collapse rate falls about a factor of 2 below what the star-formation rate predicts. And **failed-supernova searches** (the Large Binocular Telescope "survey about nothing", plus a 2026 Andromeda candidate) suggest that some fraction of massive stars simply vanish. The paper quotes that fraction as 5–50%.

Meanwhile, every production supernova code has ignored one piece of neutrino physics: neutrinos change flavor. The standard justification, from a 2012 study, was that collective flavor oscillations only kick in far outside the stalled shock, too late to matter. Work since then on neutrino self-interactions has undermined that justification. "Fast" flavor instabilities, driven by neutrino–neutrino forward scattering, can grow on centimetre scales and nanosecond timescales right near the proto-neutron star. A handful of studies in 2023–2026 bolted schematic flavor mixing onto supernova simulations of a few individual stars. Some found explosions helped, some found them hindered, and the sign depended on progenitor mass and on where the mixing happened. Nobody had asked what flavor conversion does to a whole *population* of collapsing stars.

**Paper Type & Stakes:** this is a computational-astrophysics Letter, a population study that reruns an established 195-progenitor grid with a parametric (switched-on, not self-consistent) flavor-mixing recipe. At stake is whether neutrino flavor physics, so far treated as a correction to individual simulations, actually controls the neutron-star-to-black-hole ratio of the Universe.

**Prior Belief Check:** the direction of the effect partly aligns with prior work and partly complicates it. Ehring et al. (arXiv:2301.11938, v2, PRD 2023) already found that the same equipartition recipe hinders explosion of a 20-solar-mass star in 1D, because of extra cooling near the neutrinosphere. The companion paper (arXiv:2605.18972, v3) says flavor conversion confined to the gain region *helps* explosions. Against that backdrop, the population result is mildly surprising to specialists. Even the shallowest-mixing case (conversion only below a density of 10⁹ g/cm³, near the shock) nearly doubles the failure count, 50 → 99 models. So "helps near the gain region" does not survive aggregation over a realistic progenitor mix. The size of the effect is also striking: at the deepest mixing threshold, 188 of 195 stars fail. Experts will not be surprised that flavor conversion *can* matter. That has been the field's working assumption since about 2020. What is new is a quantitative, population-level estimate of how much it could matter, and that estimate rests on an upper-bound-style recipe. This is a significant step for the question, not a settled answer to it.

**Replication & Convergence Note:** this is a single-group result (Gogilashvili and Tamborra, with their own companion paper as the methods basis), from one code (GR1D with the STIR turbulence model), one equation of state, and one progenitor set. No independent group has run a flavor-conversion population study yet. Independent confirmation would look like a different code family running a comparable grid. Examples: Fornax with its Box3D subgrid scheme, which Wang & Burrows (arXiv:2503.04896, v2) used for a few models; the multi-angle Boltzmann approach of Akaho et al. (arXiv:2601.08269, v2); or a 2D/3D set with a dynamical subgrid flavor model. Such a run should reproduce the net rise in failures concentrated at 16–30 solar masses. This matters because the multi-dimensional studies so far find *mass-dependent* signs, helping low-mass and hindering high-mass stars, which 1D could systematically miss.

## 3. Necessary Background Crash-Course

### 3.1 How neutrinos are supposed to blow up a star

Start with the energy budget. The collapsing core releases about 10⁵³ erg of gravitational binding energy. Neutrinos carry away roughly 99% of it over a few seconds. A typical supernova explosion needs only about 10⁵¹ erg, so the explosion runs on a 1% tax on the neutrino flood. The question is whether enough of that flood gets absorbed in the right place.

The right place is the **gain region**, the layer of hot, infalling matter between roughly 100 and 200 km, just inside the stalled shock. Below a certain radius (the *gain radius*), matter is hot enough that it emits neutrinos faster than it absorbs them, so it cools on net. Above the gain radius it absorbs more than it emits, so it heats on net. If the gain region soaks up energy fast enough to push the shock outward against the ram pressure of infalling matter, the shock revives and the star explodes.

Only some neutrinos can do the heating. In the gain region the dominant absorption processes are "charged-current" reactions: an electron neutrino turns a neutron into a proton plus an electron, and an electron antineutrino turns a proton into a neutron plus a positron. Muon and tau neutrinos (lumped together as "heavy-lepton" or νx neutrinos) cannot do this. At these energies they cannot create the heavy muon or tau, so they interact only through much weaker "neutral-current" scattering and leave without depositing much heat.

The absorption probability grows with the square of neutrino energy. That gives a worked number. Take two electron neutrinos at 12 MeV and 16 MeV. Their absorption cross-sections are in the ratio (16/12)² = 1.78, so a 33% harder neutrino is 78% more likely to be absorbed. Roughly speaking, the heating rate tracks the electron-flavor luminosity times the mean squared energy, diluted by distance squared. Anything that makes the electron-flavor neutrinos more numerous or harder in the gain region helps the explosion. Anything that drains energy into the heavy-lepton channel, which streams out without heating, hurts it.

> **Analogy:** the proto-neutron star is a power station feeding a city (the shock) through two kinds of cable. Electron-flavor cables have plugs that fit the city's sockets. Heavy-lepton cables carry real power but have plugs that fit nothing in town, so their power runs straight past the city and out to sea. The explosion depends not on total generation but on how much flows through plugs the city can use.

> **Breaks when:** you treat the two cable types as fixed hardware. Neutrinos can *change* which cable they are in mid-flight. That is the whole subject of this paper, and a cable network has no way to represent a power packet that is partly in both cables at once (a quantum superposition of flavors) until something forces it to pick.

### 3.2 Neutrino flavor conversion, and why "fast" conversion is the new worry

A neutrino created as an electron neutrino is a quantum superposition of three mass states. Because those states travel with slightly different phases, the flavor content oscillates as the neutrino propagates. In vacuum, and in ordinary matter (the MSW effect), these oscillations are slow on supernova-core scales, with oscillation lengths of kilometres or more. Near the core they are further suppressed by the dense matter, which is the basis of the 2012 argument that flavor conversion is irrelevant behind the shock.

The game changes when the neutrinos themselves are the dense medium. Near the proto-neutron star there are so many neutrinos that each one's flavor evolution depends on the flavor state of all the others it forward-scatters off. The equations become nonlinear and collective. One branch of this collective behavior, **fast flavor conversion**, is triggered when the angular distribution of electron-lepton number has a "crossing". In some directions there are more electron neutrinos than antineutrinos, and in other directions it is the other way round. When that happens, flavor perturbations grow exponentially at a rate set by the neutrino density itself. That rate can reach roughly 10⁹ per second, with spatial scales of centimetres. A supernova hydro grid has cells of hundreds of metres to kilometres and time steps of microseconds, so the instability is invisible to it, many orders of magnitude below what it can resolve. Recent kinetic simulations and analyses (the paper cites Refs. [47–53]) show that such instabilities, plus collisional and mass-driven cousins, can occur inside the neutrinosphere region, not only beyond the shock.

> **Analogy:** a supernova code tracking neutrino energy flow is like a profiler sampling a CPU at 1 kHz, while fast flavor conversion is a cache-coherence storm that completes in nanoseconds. The profiler cannot see the storm. It sees only the steady-state aftermath: the counters look different from what its model predicted. A "subgrid model" means guessing that aftermath from coarse-grained inputs without resolving the storm.

> **Breaks when:** you expect the aftermath to be a unique, well-defined state the way a coherence protocol settles into a known final state. The asymptotic flavor state of fast conversion is still an active research problem (Refs. [70–78]). It depends on the angular distribution, collisions, and inhomogeneity, and does not generally reach full mixing. So the "aftermath" this paper assumes is itself a modeling choice, not a known protocol endpoint.

### 3.3 Flavor equipartition: the paper's mixing recipe

Because the real flavor outcome is unknown, the authors use a deliberately simple recipe from Ehring et al. (2023): **instantaneous flavor equipartition**. In each of the code's 18 energy bins, wherever the matter density is below a chosen threshold, they redistribute neutrinos so that the flavors are as equal as allowed. Four quantities are held fixed: the total neutrino number per bin, the total momentum per bin, and the *net* electron-lepton number (electron neutrinos minus electron antineutrinos). That last constraint stops the recipe from changing the plasma's lepton budget in a way real flavor conversion cannot.

A tiny example shows how it moves energy. Use one simple rule consistent with the constraints (the exact algorithm is in Ehring et al. 2023 and the companion paper, which I did not read in full). The rule: make the electron antineutrinos and each of the four heavy-lepton species (νμ, ν̄μ, ντ, ν̄τ) equal, and let the electron neutrinos carry the conserved lepton excess on top. Algebraically, if a bin holds N neutrinos in total with electron-lepton excess L, each heavy species and the electron antineutrinos end at (N − L)/6, and the electron neutrinos at (N − L)/6 + L.

- *Low-energy bin, where electron flavors dominate:* 6 electron neutrinos, 4 electron antineutrinos, 2 of each heavy species. Then N = 18 and L = 2, so each heavy species and the antineutrinos end at 16/6 ≈ 2.67, and the electron neutrinos at ≈ 4.67. Electron neutrinos drop from 6 to 4.67. Low-energy heating neutrinos are *removed* into the heavy-lepton channel.
- *High-energy bin, where heavy-lepton neutrinos dominate:* they decouple deeper and hotter, so take 1 electron neutrino, 1 electron antineutrino, 2 of each heavy species. Then N = 10 and L = 0, so every species ends at 10/6 ≈ 1.67. Electron neutrinos *rise* from 1 to 1.67, and so do electron antineutrinos.

Mixing therefore swaps soft electron-flavor neutrinos for hard ones. Because absorption scales with energy squared, that shift raises the heating per neutrino even though the total electron-flavor number can fall.

That is why the recipe cuts both ways. In the gain region, it hardens the electron-flavor spectrum, and hardness is squared in the heating, so heating rises. Deeper down, near and inside the neutrinosphere, it opens an escape route: energy that was trapped in strongly interacting electron-flavor neutrinos is handed to weakly interacting heavy-lepton neutrinos that stream out. That speeds cooling and contraction of the proto-neutron star and lowers the electron-flavor luminosity reaching the gain region.

> **Concept:** equipartition imposes the maximum flavor mixing allowed by number, momentum, and lepton-number conservation, applied instantly and everywhere below a density threshold. Two opposing effects follow. Mixing in the gain region raises charged-current heating by hardening the electron-flavor spectrum. Mixing at and below the neutrinosphere raises net cooling by diverting energy into flavors that escape without heating. The net sign depends on how deep the mixing region reaches.

> **Vehicle:** a building has a hot-water system with two pipe networks. The red network feeds radiators in the rooms. The blue network vents straight to the roof. At some junctions a plumber installs mixing valves that force the two networks to the same temperature and flow. A valve near the radiators can push hotter water into the red network, so the rooms warm up. A valve down at the boiler diverts boiler output into the blue network, so it leaves through the roof before it reaches any room.

> **Analogy:** red network ↔ electron-flavor neutrinos that heat via charged-current absorption. Blue network ↔ heavy-lepton neutrinos that escape. Valve near the radiators ↔ mixing in the gain region (low threshold density, 10⁹ g/cm³). Valve at the boiler ↔ mixing near or inside the neutrinosphere (10¹¹–10¹³ g/cm³). Where the valves sit decides whether the building gets warmer or colder.

> **Breaks when:** you ask what the valves do to the boiler itself. In the star, the "boiler" (proto-neutron star) responds to losing energy by contracting and heating, which changes its future output. A plumbing network has no feedback path in which venting heat to the roof makes the boiler shrink and change its burn. Note too that in this paper's setup a deeper threshold does not *move* the valve. It adds valves everywhere from that depth outward, so the 10¹³ case includes the gain-region valves as well.

### 3.4 1D+ simulations: spherical codes with a turbulence patch

Real supernovae are violently three-dimensional. Buoyant plumes churn the gain region, and the shock can slosh (the standing accretion shock instability, SASI). That turbulence helps explosions, partly by keeping matter in the gain region longer so it absorbs more energy. Pure 1D simulations of all but the lightest stars fail to explode for exactly this reason. Full 3D simulations cost millions of core-hours each, so nobody runs 195 of them.

The workaround is "1D+": a spherical code plus a model of turbulence. This paper uses GR1D (an open-source general-relativistic 1D code, 850 radial zones) with the **STIR** model (Supernova Turbulence In Reduced-dimensionality), which adds mixing-length convection. Mixing-length theory is the same approximation stellar-evolution codes use: a blob rises about one "mixing length" before dissolving into its surroundings. Here that length is a dimensionless parameter, α_MLT = 1.51, calibrated so the 1D+ models match 3D behavior on average. Neutrino transport uses the "M1" moment method in 18 energy bins for three species: electron neutrinos, electron antineutrinos, and one combined heavy-lepton species. M1 tracks only the energy density and flux of neutrinos at each radius (the zeroth and first angular moments). It closes the equations with an analytic guess for the next moment instead of following the full angular distribution.

> **Analogy:** 1D+ with M1 is like modeling a data-center's thermal behavior with per-rack average temperature and airflow plus a calibrated "turbulence factor", rather than full computational fluid dynamics of every server. It runs fast enough to sweep 195 configurations, and its calibration knob is tuned against a handful of full simulations.

> **Breaks when:** the important physics is in the shape of the flow rather than its averages. M1 discards exactly the angular detail (the electron-lepton-number "crossing") that triggers fast flavor conversion, which is why flavor conversion must be imposed by hand. STIR cannot produce SASI sloshing, which the paper concedes would help compact, high-accretion stars explode.

### 3.5 Compactness and islands of explodability

To predict which stars might explode before simulating them, modelers use **compactness**: how tightly the inner 2.5 solar masses of the star are packed just before collapse.

$$\xi_{2.5} = \frac{M/M_\odot}{R(M)/1000\ \mathrm{km}}, \qquad M = 2.5\,M_\odot$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$\xi_{2.5}$ : compactness parameter (dimensionless)
$M$ : the enclosed mass at which compactness is evaluated, here 2.5 solar masses
$M_\odot$ : one solar mass
$R(M)$ : the radius that encloses mass $M$ just before collapse (km)

**What this actually means:** if 2.5 solar masses fit inside 5,000 km, the compactness is 2.5 / 5 = 0.5. If they spread to 10,000 km, it is 0.25. High compactness means a dense core that dumps matter onto the shock quickly. That brings strong ram pressure, which makes explosion harder, even though it also produces more neutrino luminosity. Think of it as the shock's "incoming request rate". A server facing a higher request rate needs proportionally more capacity to stay ahead. In this set, compactness hovers near 0.15–0.2 for 15–20 solar masses, rises to about 0.45 near 23 solar masses, and spikes to about 0.55 near 40 solar masses (Fig. 1, top).

Plotting success (explosion) against initial mass gives the "islands of explodability": runs of exploding stars separated by runs of failures, matching compactness bumps only loosely.

> **Breaks when:** you treat compactness as a hard threshold. It is a one-number summary of a multi-parameter structure. Two-parameter criteria (Ertl et al. 2016) and newer ones (Maltsev et al. 2025) do better, and the paper itself shows failures at low compactness (13–16 solar masses) and successes at moderate compactness.

### 3.6 The initial mass function: why low-mass stars dominate the rate

Not all 195 models count equally in nature. Stars form with an **initial mass function** (IMF), and the Salpeter form says the number of stars per unit mass falls as the mass to the power −2.35.

Worked example: compare a 9.5-solar-mass star with a 20.5-solar-mass star. The mass ratio is about 2.16, and 2.16 to the power 2.35 is about 6.1. So for every star near 20.5 solar masses, nature makes about six near 9.5. My Python check (script listed in §4) splits the 9–120 solar-mass range into bands, with these Salpeter shares of all collapsing stars: 9–13 solar masses ≈ 40%, 13–16 ≈ 15%, 16–30 ≈ 27%, 30–120 ≈ 17%. Changing the slope from −2.0 to −2.7 moves the 16–30 band only between 28% and 25%. So the 16–30 band, where the paper finds the largest flavor-conversion effect, carries about a quarter of all core collapses regardless of the exact IMF.

> **Analogy:** IMF weighting is a traffic-weighted average over code paths. A failure in a path that serves 40% of requests (low-mass stars) matters six times more than the same failure in a rarely hit path, so you weight each benchmark by its call frequency before quoting an overall failure rate.

> **Breaks when:** you assume the weights are known per model. Each simulated star stands in for a slice of mass. How wide that slice is depends on how densely the grid samples that mass range, and the paper does not spell out its per-model weighting. Also, the IMF says nothing about binary interaction, which reshapes cores and removes envelopes for a large fraction of massive stars. A single-star IMF weighting cannot capture that.

### 3.7 Remnant mass: baryonic versus gravitational

The paper reports the **baryonic mass** of the proto-neutron star: the number of nucleons times their mass, measured inside the neutrinosphere at 1 second after bounce. Observers measure the **gravitational mass**, which is smaller because the neutron star's binding energy (radiated away as neutrinos) subtracts from it. The paper converts using the Timmes–Woosley–Weaver fit.

$$M_{\rm bary} = M_{\rm grav} + 0.075\,M_\odot\,(M_{\rm grav}/M_\odot)^2$$

**Symbol definitions:**
$M_{\rm bary}$ : baryonic mass (rest mass of the nucleons), solar masses
$M_{\rm grav}$ : gravitational mass (what binary orbits and gravitational waves measure), solar masses
$0.075\,M_\odot$ : fit coefficient for the binding-energy deficit

**What this actually means:** solving the quadratic, a 1.40 baryonic star weighs about 1.28 gravitationally (a 0.12 deficit), and a 1.90 baryonic star about 1.69 (a 0.21 deficit). So the paper's 1.2–1.9 baryonic range maps to about 1.11–1.69 gravitational. It is like the difference between a file's uncompressed size and its on-disk size: the conversion is mechanical, but you must apply it before comparing with what the observer's tool reports.

> **Breaks when:** you treat the 1-second neutrinosphere mass as the final neutron-star mass. Footnote [93] concedes it is a lower bound on mass growth for exploding models, because 1D cannot capture continued accretion through downflows that coexists with outflows in multi-D. For failed models it says nothing about the final black-hole mass.

> **Central analogy for this paper:** mixing valves on the heating versus vent pipes.

## 4. Core Technical Explanation

### 4.1 The setup, step by step

**Step 1 — Take a standard population.** The authors take the Sukhbold et al. (2016) single-star, solar-metallicity progenitor set (arXiv:1510.04643, v3), the most widely used grid for explodability studies. They simulate 195 of its stars from 9 to 120 solar masses. The grid is dense (fractions of a solar mass apart) below 30 solar masses and sparse above. In Fig. 1, gray bars mark mass intervals with no simulated model. Each star collapses with the SFHo nuclear equation of state, and runs 1–3 seconds past bounce.

**Step 2 — Run a 1D+ engine.** Each collapse uses GR1D with M1 neutrino transport in 18 energy bins, NuLib interaction rates, and STIR turbulence with α_MLT = 1.51. The companion paper and Boccioli et al. document this engine. Without flavor conversion it is a standard 1D+ explodability machine, comparable to Boccioli & Fragione (arXiv:2404.05927, v3), who ran 341 progenitors with a similar GR1D+STIR setup.

**Step 3 — Switch on flavor mixing.** At 20 ms after bounce, the authors begin applying instantaneous equipartition in every zone whose baryon density falls below a threshold. They run four thresholds, 10⁹, 10¹⁰, 10¹¹, and 10¹³ g/cm³, plus a no-mixing baseline. That gives 5 × 195 = 975 simulations. According to the companion's Fig. 1 (as described here), the thresholds sit roughly as follows: 10⁹ in the gain region near the shock, 10¹⁰ near the gain radius, 10¹¹ near the neutrinosphere, and 10¹³ deep inside the proto-neutron star. Because mixing applies *everywhere below* the threshold density, i.e. *outside* the corresponding radius, a higher threshold means a larger mixing region that reaches deeper. The 10¹³ case mixes everything from deep inside the proto-neutron star out through the gain region.

**Step 4 — Call each run.** A model "explodes" if its shock passes 1,000 km. Otherwise it is a failed supernova that will make a black hole.

**Step 5 — Weight and count.** The authors report the raw fraction of failed models and a Salpeter-IMF-weighted fraction (Table I). They add that a Kroupa IMF gives similar numbers.

**Step 6 — Read off remnant masses.** For each run, they record the baryonic mass inside the flavor- and energy-averaged neutrinosphere at 1 second (or at the last time step, if the run ended earlier) and plot it against initial mass, separately for exploding and failing models (Fig. 2).

### 4.2 Result 1 — failure fractions climb steeply with mixing depth

Table I, together with the integer counts it implies (each percentage is an exact count out of 195):

| Mixing threshold | Failed models | Raw failed fraction | Salpeter-weighted failed fraction | Implied visible-supernova deficit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| None | 50 / 195 | 25.6% | 27.0% | ×1.37 |
| 10⁹ g/cm³ | 99 / 195 | 50.8% | 47.6% | ×1.91 |
| 10¹⁰ g/cm³ | 151 / 195 | 77.4% | 61.9% | ×2.62 |
| 10¹¹ g/cm³ | 182 / 195 | 93.3% | 73.9% | ×3.83 |
| 10¹³ g/cm³ | 188 / 195 | 96.4% | 88.3% | ×8.55 |

(The "visible-supernova deficit" column is mine: one divided by the IMF-weighted fraction that explodes. It is the factor by which the observed supernova rate would undershoot a star-formation-based prediction if failed collapses are invisible.)

**Fig. 1** is the heart of the paper. It shows five horizontal barcodes, one per scenario, with green for exploding and black for failing models along the initial-mass axis. The baseline row shows the familiar islands. A big failure block sits from about 12.5 to just under 15 solar masses. There are long green stretches from about 15 to 22 and from about 23.5 to 30 solar masses, with scattered black failures. Above 30 solar masses, models mostly fail, with a few green slivers. Moving down the rows, the green stretches in 16–30 erode first: by the 10¹⁰ row they are mostly black, and by 10¹¹ almost entirely black. The low-mass end (9–12 solar masses) holds out longest. In the 10¹³ row, the only survivors are a few low-mass models and a thin green sliver near 19–20 solar masses.

Two features of the numbers deserve unpacking.

First, the raw and weighted fractions **cross over**. With no mixing, the weighted fraction (27.0%) exceeds the raw one (25.6%), because baseline failures sit in the populous 13–15 band. With mixing, the weighted fraction falls *below* the raw one (e.g. 88.3% vs 96.4%), because the last survivors are the cheap, common low-mass stars. In the 10¹³ case, the 7 surviving models carry 11.7% of the IMF weight, about 1.7% each, versus an average of 0.5% per model (1/195). That is what you expect if the survivors sit at 9–12 solar masses. It is consistent with Fig. 1 and is a nice internal check that the weighting is behaving sensibly.

Second, even the shallowest mixing **hurts on net**. The 10⁹ threshold confines mixing to the gain region, exactly where the companion paper says mixing increases heating. Yet the failure count still doubles, 50 → 99. Fig. 1 shows why: a few models switch from failure to success, but many more switch the other way, notably around 20–23 solar masses. The paper explains the overall trend as flavor conversion "redistributing energy from electron-flavor neutrinos to heavy-flavor neutrinos, with a consequent decrease of the efficiency of neutrino energy deposition". The heating boost from harder spectra is real in some models, but in this 1D+ setup the energy-diversion channel wins more often.

The physical story for the mass dependence is that intermediate-mass stars (16–30 solar masses) are "marginal". With no mixing, their explosions succeed by a slim margin, because their cores are moderately compact and their accretion rates moderate. A modest reduction in effective heating flips them. Very low-mass stars (9–12) have steep density gradients and low accretion rates, so their shocks run away easily and they tolerate a heating hit. Stars that already failed stay failed.

### 4.3 Result 2 — remnant masses shift, in opposite directions for the two branches

**Exploding models (Fig. 2, top).** The baryonic remnant masses span about 1.2–1.9 solar masses. They follow a sawtooth with initial mass that tracks compactness: low near 9–12, a bump near 15, a peak of about 1.85 near 22–23, lower again at 25–30. At the same initial mass, mixing runs (colored) sit systematically below the baseline (black), typically by a few hundredths to about 0.08 solar masses on my reading of the figure. The difference is largest for the shallowest thresholds. The authors explain this through shock timing. When mixing happens in the gain region and the star still explodes, the shock revives earlier and more energetically, so less matter accretes onto the proto-neutron star before infall is cut off.

**Failing models (Fig. 2, bottom).** At 1 second, the baryonic masses span about 1.45–1.95 solar masses, slightly *larger* with mixing because accretion continues unchecked. The caption notes that the baseline and 10⁹ symbols are hidden under the orange 10¹⁰ symbols, which means the differences are small. The authors stress that these are not black-hole masses. A failed star keeps accreting its envelope, through fallback, for much longer than 1 second.

**The comparison with observed neutron stars.** After converting to gravitational mass, the exploding models span about 1.11–1.69 solar masses (my recomputation of the paper's conversion). The paper argues that the mixing runs agree "better with the bulk of the observed neutron star population, peaking around 1.2–1.4", and "ease the tension... with the observed low-mass end". That tension refers to Müller, Heger & Powell (arXiv:2407.08407, v2, PRL 2025): simulations struggle to make neutron stars as light as the 1.174-solar-mass companion in J0453+1559, and their 3D runs got down to about 1.19.

### 4.4 What the paper says about its own robustness

The authors list several caveats in the body, and these are not boilerplate. The weighted fractions of 73.9% and 88.3% for the two deepest thresholds are "seemingly in tension with observational constraints" and "should be interpreted with caution". A dynamical subgrid model is needed. Proto-neutron-star convection is missing, and deep mixing is expected to boost it, which might restore some heating. The equation of state could change the quantitative impact. The baseline explodability depends on the progenitor set and on 1D progenitor structure. STIR cannot reproduce SASI, whose absence "may bias our CCSN explodability landscape toward failed explosions, especially for the most compact progenitors". Their robustness claim is narrower: the *relative* increase of failures with mixing, measured against their own no-mixing baseline, is robust.

**Headline 3 — "naturally easing the supernova-rate problem". Partly reproduced.** Horiuchi et al. **Sensitivity check — how much of the mixing effect must be wrong before the deep cases become acceptable?** Take the paper's own upper bound for the observed failed fraction, 50%. For each threshold, I computed the share of the *extra* failures (above the 27.0% baseline) that would have to revert to explosions to bring the weighted fraction down to 50%:
- 10⁹: 0%. It already sits at 47.6%, inside the range.
- 10¹⁰: 34% of the extra 34.9 points.
- 10¹¹: 51% of the extra 46.9 points.
- 10¹³: 62% of the extra 61.3 points.

These break points are plausible to reach. The paper itself lists two biases that push toward over-failing: missing SASI and missing proto-neutron-star convection. Multi-D studies (Ehring et al. 2D, arXiv:2305.11207 v2; Akaho et al., arXiv:2601.08269 v2) find that mixing *helps* lower-mass stars explode, which the 1D+ setup partly misses. Reverting a third to a half of the extra failures is the kind of correction those effects could plausibly supply. The conclusion that mixing raises the failure rate survives. The specific large numbers (74%, 88%) do not survive with much confidence.

I also ran a sensitivity check on the IMF. The paper's robustness statement ("Kroupa gives similar results") is weaker than it sounds. Above about 0.5 solar masses the Kroupa IMF has slope −2.3, almost identical to Salpeter's −2.35. Over 9–120 solar masses the two give the same band shares to within about one percentage point (my run: 9–13 band 39.4% vs 40.4%; 16–30 band 27.4% vs 27.1%). Swapping them tests nothing. A real IMF test would vary the high-mass slope (e.g. −2.0 to −2.7), which shifts the 30–120 band from 24% to 12% of collapses. Since that band mostly fails in every scenario, a shallower IMF would raise all the weighted fractions, including the baseline.

**Type-appropriate robustness test (simulation): dependence on a tuned parameter and on an independent code.** The paper reports no convergence or resolution test of its own. It inherits the engine's validation from the companion and from Boccioli et al. The most consequential tuned input is the mixing threshold itself, which the paper treats as a free proxy, and its outcomes span 48% to 88%. That spread is the systematic error bar on the headline. For an independent-code comparison, the closest available results all use the same equipartition idea in other codes:
- Ehring et al. (1D, one 20-solar-mass star; arXiv:2301.11938 v2): mixing hinders explosion through cooling at the neutrinosphere. Same sign as this paper for a 20-solar-mass star. ✔
- Ehring et al. (2D; arXiv:2305.11207 v2, PRL 2023): helps 9–12 solar-mass stars, hinders about 20. Partially consistent. This paper also finds low-mass stars most resilient, but in 1D+ they are not *helped* on net.
- Mori et al. (3D, phenomenological; arXiv:2501.15256 v2, PASJ 2025): explosion energies up to about 10⁵¹ erg depending on the critical density, because of harder electron-antineutrino spectra. That is a *helping* result for their models.
- Akaho et al. (multi-angle Boltzmann, no equipartition assumption; arXiv:2601.08269 v2, PRL 2026): fast conversion helps lower-mass and hinders higher-mass progenitors, controlled by accretion rate.
- Wang & Burrows (Fornax + Box3D; arXiv:2503.04896 v2): effects of order 10% on heating; 1D shock radii change by only 10–20 km.

Taken together, the outside codes agree with this paper on the *mass trend* (high-mass stars are more vulnerable). They disagree on the *magnitude* (Wang & Burrows find modest effects) and on the *sign for low-mass stars* (multi-D studies find help). So the population fractions are the most code-dependent part of the paper.

### Assumption Audit

> **Watch:** You likely assume the four thresholds are four estimates of *where* flavor conversion happens, with the truth somewhere among them. In fact each run applies full mixing *everywhere* below its density, from that depth out through the shock, from 20 ms onward, without switching off. Even the 10⁹ case is an "always on, maximally mixed" scenario within its region. Real fast conversion turns on and off as the angular crossings come and go, and kinetic studies find partial rather than full equilibration in many cases. So the four rows are not a bracket around reality. They are four variants of an aggressive upper-end recipe, and the true answer could lie below all of them, including near the baseline.

> **Watch:** You likely assume "FC increases failures" contradicts the companion paper's message that conversion near the gain region *enhances* explosions. Both hold, at different levels. Individual models can be helped. In Fig. 1, some failures turn into successes at 10⁹ and 10¹⁰. But over the 195-star population the hindered models outnumber the helped ones at every threshold, 99 failures versus 50 even at 10⁹. The population claim is a net count, and it hides a two-way flow of models between the columns. That flow is exactly what a multi-D treatment, where low-mass stars are helped more, could rebalance.

> **Watch:** You likely assume the lower neutron-star masses with flavor conversion mean that the same stars now make lighter neutron stars, by enough to fix the low-mass-tail problem. Per star, the shift is a few hundredths of a solar mass in gravitational mass (about 0.04 for a 0.05 baryonic shift). At the population level, the larger effect is selection: deep mixing removes the heavy-remnant 16–30 solar-mass exploders and leaves mostly 9–12 solar-mass ones. Also, the plotted masses are 1-second neutrinosphere masses, which footnote [93] labels lower bounds, and the lightest models already convert to about 1.11 solar masses with or without mixing. > **Watch:** You likely assume the baseline (27% failure) is a fixed reference against which the mixing effect is measured. The baseline is itself model-dependent. Their robustness claim is only about the relative increase, measured against their own baseline. ## 5. What's Genuinely New or Clever

**New to the field: the first population-scale flavor-conversion study.** Previous work put schematic flavor mixing into one to six progenitors (Ehring et al. with one 20-solar-mass star in 1D and a few in 2D; the companion with six; Mori et al. and Akaho et al. with a handful each). This Letter runs the full 195-model Sukhbold grid at five settings, about a thousand simulations. That turns a "helps or hinders?" debate about individual stars into a quantity that observers can test: the IMF-weighted failed-supernova fraction, with its implied deficit in the visible supernova rate. The step from "mechanism" to "population statistic" is the contribution. Nothing about the recipe or the code is new. What is new is applying it across the grid and reading off a rate.

**The mass-band localization is the sharpest physical result.** Flavor conversion does not shift explodability uniformly. It eats the 16–30 solar-mass islands first, the marginal exploders, while 9–12 solar-mass stars hold out. That localization is what connects the result to the red supergiant problem, which is precisely a shortage of exploding progenitors above about 16–19 solar masses (Davies & Beasor, arXiv:2001.06020 v1, the only version, place the upper limit at about 19.2 solar masses with the problem significant at roughly 2σ). It also explains the raw-versus-weighted crossover: the populous low-mass stars, which dominate the rate, are the most resistant. A reader new to this area should take away that explodability is not one dial. Microphysics changes hit marginal regions of the progenitor landscape, and the IMF then decides how much that matters for rates.

**A quiet methodological point:** using the density threshold as a "proxy for the uncertain radial extent" turns an unsolved microphysics question (where and how strongly does fast conversion occur?) into a one-parameter family of scenarios. Each row of Table I is a conditional prediction: *if* mixing reaches this deep, *then* this many stars fail. That makes it possible to invert the logic. Observed failed-supernova fractions could in principle bound how deep strong flavor conversion can reach in real cores, provided the rest of the model is trusted. The paper does not make this inversion explicitly, but its Table I is set up for it.

### Predictive Content Check

1. **Falsifiable handle:** the paper predicts an IMF-weighted failed-collapse fraction of 48–88% (versus 27% without flavor conversion), with the extra failures concentrated in stars born at 16–30 solar masses. Three observables could prove this wrong:
   - (a) **Direct failed-supernova surveys.** Continued disappearing-star monitoring (the LBT survey, the Andromeda candidate of De et al. 2026, and Rubin/LSST-era searches) measures the failed fraction directly. A measured fraction firmly below about 45% would exclude every mixing scenario in this paper *within this 1D+ model*. Below about 60% excludes the three deepest. The baseline 27% would then be preferred.
   - (b) **Progenitor masses of vanishing stars.** If failed-supernova candidates come mainly from 16–30 solar-mass red supergiants (luminosities around 10^5.1–10^5.5 solar), that supports the pattern. If they come mainly from more massive or less massive stars, it does not.
   - (c) **The diffuse supernova neutrino background.** Black-hole-forming collapses emit a hotter, briefer neutrino burst. A failed fraction of 60–90% would harden the predicted background spectrum noticeably, testable with Super-Kamiokande's gadolinium phase and with JUNO and Hyper-Kamiokande.

   The supernova-rate deficit gives a sharp number: 1.9× (shallowest mixing) up to 8.6× (deepest). Since the measured gap is about 2×, the deep scenarios already sit in tension with existing data, and the paper partly concedes this.

   Note that the red-supergiant and rate "easing" claims are *reinterpretations* of known anomalies. The paper did not predict them in advance. They are new consequences of the model only in the sense that the model now produces them. The genuinely predictive parts are (b) and the neutron-star-mass trend.

**The flavor-conversion recipe is an aggressive, static upper-end scenario, and the headline fractions inherit its spread (48–88%).** Instant, maximal equipartition everywhere below a fixed density, switched on at 20 ms and never off, is not what kinetic calculations show. ## 7. Detailed Summary & Explanation

The authors ask whether neutrinos changing flavor inside a collapsing star changes how many massive stars explode as supernovae, and how many quietly become black holes. They take 195 standard single-star models from 9 to 120 solar masses and collapse each one five times in a spherically symmetric code with a calibrated turbulence model. One run has no flavor conversion. The other four force neutrinos into maximal flavor mixing everywhere outside a chosen depth, with the depth set by a threshold matter density from 10⁹ g/cm³ (near the stalled shock) to 10¹³ g/cm³ (deep in the newborn neutron star).

Without mixing, about a quarter of the stars fail (27% when weighted by how common each mass is). Mixing raises this sharply and monotonically with depth: 48%, 62%, 74%, and 88% weighted. The losses concentrate among stars of 16 to 30 solar masses, which were marginal exploders to begin with, while the abundant 9–12 solar-mass stars hold out longest. Mechanically, mixing hands energy from the electron-type neutrinos, which heat the gas behind the shock, to the muon- and tau-type neutrinos, which escape without heating. Deeper mixing also speeds the cooling of the neutron star. Among stars that still explode, mixing tends to revive the shock earlier, so the leftover neutron stars come out slightly lighter, by a few hundredths of a solar mass. Among stars that fail, the core keeps accreting and ends up slightly heavier at the one-second mark.

The authors connect the results to three observational puzzles. Stars above about 16–19 solar masses seem to be missing among supernova progenitors. Supernovae appear rarer than star formation predicts. And the lightest neutron stars seem hard to make. They also concede, in the body, that their two deepest scenarios produce more failed supernovae than observations seem to allow.

**Why this summary is framed this way.** I chose to present Table I as a family of conditional scenarios rather than a single answer. The paper's spread across scenarios (48–88%) is larger than the baseline itself, and all four rows use the same aggressive full-mixing recipe. The key interpretive choices were:
1. Separating the robust qualitative finding (mixing raises failures, especially at 16–30 solar masses, in this framework) from the fragile quantitative ones (the specific percentages).
2. Recomputing the rate implications, which shows that only the shallowest scenario matches the factor-of-two supernova-rate gap and that the paper's own "tension" admission should extend to the 10¹⁰ case.
3. Splitting the neutron-star-mass effect into a small per-star shift and a larger population selection effect.

The reader should take away a new, quantitatively bounded worry: flavor physics that every supernova code has ignored could double, or more, the share of massive stars that collapse silently. Whether it actually does depends on flavor-conversion physics that is not yet solved.

## 8. Three Crystallized Takeaways

1. Neutrinos that switch flavor inside a collapsing star can starve the explosion. Energy moves out of the neutrino types that heat the gas into types that escape without heating. In 195 simulated stars, the share that fails and becomes a black hole rises from about a quarter to between half and nearly nine in ten, depending on how deep the mixing reaches.
2. The damage is concentrated in stars born at 16–30 times the Sun's mass, the same range where astronomers notice too few supernova progenitors. That makes flavor conversion a candidate contributor to that long-standing puzzle, but not yet a proven one.
3. The numbers come from an aggressive, switched-on mixing recipe in one-dimensional simulations. The deepest-mixing versions already predict more failed supernovae than observations seem to allow. The real lesson is that supernova codes can no longer ignore neutrino flavor, not that half of massive stars definitely fail.

## 9. Shorter Summary

When a massive star's core collapses, a flood of neutrinos must re-energize a stalled shock wave for the star to explode as a supernova and leave a neutron star. If heating falls short, the star collapses into a black hole with little or no visible explosion. Only electron-type neutrinos heat the gas effectively. The muon and tau types mostly escape.

Neutrinos can change type ("flavor") on very short scales near the core, but standard simulations ignore this. Gogilashvili and Tamborra simulated 195 stars of 9 to 120 solar masses in a one-dimensional code with a turbulence model. They forced maximal flavor mixing everywhere outside a chosen depth and repeated the runs for four different depths.

Without mixing, about 27% of stars fail, after weighting for how common each mass is. With mixing, failures climb to 48%, 62%, 74%, or 88% as the mixing region reaches deeper. Stars of 16–30 solar masses are hit hardest, and the abundant lighter stars hold out longest. Among stars that still explode, the neutron stars come out slightly lighter, by a few hundredths of a solar mass.

The authors link this to three puzzles: missing supernova progenitors above about 16–19 solar masses, supernovae that are rarer than star formation predicts, and very light neutron stars. My recomputation shows that only the shallowest-mixing case matches the roughly factor-of-two shortfall in observed supernovae. The deeper cases predict far too few explosions, a problem the authors partly acknowledge. Their neutron-star-mass improvement is small per star and partly reflects which stars still explode.

The mixing recipe is deliberately extreme. It is instant, complete, and always on. The simulations also omit effects, such as shock sloshing and convection inside the neutron star, that would favor explosions. The result is a single-group, single-code estimate, best read as a strong warning that neutrino flavor physics may substantially shift the neutron-star-to-black-hole balance, not as a measured rate.
